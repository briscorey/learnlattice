Knowing & Understanding

Show what you know about the maths / science

Level 3–4: Limited
What this looks like
You can recall some facts but explanations are incomplete or vague.
Example response — Algebra topic

A variable is a letter. It can be x. You use it in maths.

↑ To move to 5–6: Add a definition in your own words, give more than one example, and explain when or why you would use a variable.
Level 5–6: Substantial
What this looks like
You can explain concepts in your own words and apply knowledge to familiar situations.
Example response — Algebra topic

A variable is a letter that represents an unknown number. For example, in the expression 3x + 5, the variable is x. This means we can substitute different values for x to get different answers.

↑ To move to 7–8: Explain the connection between variables and equations. Use a real-world context. Show how changing the variable changes the result.
Level 7–8: Excellent
What this looks like
You apply knowledge to unfamiliar situations and make connections between concepts.
Example response — Algebra topic

A variable is a symbol, usually a letter, that represents a quantity that can change. Unlike a constant, which stays the same, a variable can take different values. In real life, this is useful because we can model situations where we don't know the answer yet — for instance, if a phone plan charges ¥30 per month plus ¥0.50 per minute, the total cost is C = 30 + 0.5m, where m is the number of minutes used. By changing m, we can predict the cost for any number of minutes, which is why algebra is powerful for making decisions.

B

Investigating Patterns

Show how you find and describe patterns

Level 3–4: Limited
You can describe what you see but don't explain the pattern or make predictions.
Example — Number pattern investigation

The numbers go up. Each time it gets bigger. I think the next one is 21.

↑ To move to 5–6: Say by how much the numbers increase. Write the pattern as a rule. Verify your prediction.
Level 5–6: Substantial
Example — Number pattern investigation

I tested the first five cases and recorded the results in a table. The outputs were 3, 7, 11, 15, 19. I noticed that each output increases by 4. This suggests the rule is output = 4n − 1, where n is the case number. For case 3: 4(3) − 1 = 11 ✓.

↑ To move to 7–8: Explain why the pattern works (not just what it is). Test with a large case number. Discuss whether the rule works for all cases or has limitations.
Level 7–8: Excellent
Example — Number pattern investigation

After organising my results in a table, I identified that the common difference is +4 between each consecutive output. Since the pattern starts at 3 when n = 1, and increases by 4 each time, this is a linear sequence. I wrote the general rule as T(n) = 4n − 1. To verify, I tested case 50: T(50) = 4(50) − 1 = 199. I checked this against my table and it was correct. This rule works for all positive integers because the relationship is linear — it would not work for n = 0 or negative values, which are outside the problem context.

C

Communicating

Present your mathematical / scientific thinking clearly

Level 3–4: Limited
Some correct ideas but hard for the reader to follow. Missing labels, unclear notation.
Example — Presenting results

I did it and the answer is 42. I used the formula. The graph shows it goes up.

↑ To move to 5–6: Show your working step by step. Label your graph with a title, axis labels, and units. Use the correct mathematical symbols.
Level 5–6: Substantial
Example — Presenting results

Using the formula A = πr², I calculated the area of the circle. The radius is 7 cm, so A = π × 7² = π × 49 ≈ 153.94 cm². I displayed my results in a table (Table 1) and plotted them on a line graph (Figure 1). The area is measured in cm².

↑ To move to 7–8: Explain what your graph shows. Use consistent notation throughout. Add a brief interpretation of your results.
Level 7–8: Excellent
Example — Presenting results

To determine the relationship between radius and area, I calculated A = πr² for radii from 1 cm to 10 cm (Table 1). Figure 1 shows the resulting curve, which demonstrates that as the radius doubles, the area quadruples — confirming a quadratic relationship. All values are rounded to 2 decimal places and expressed in cm². I chose a line graph rather than a bar chart because the data is continuous, and a smooth curve better shows the non-linear relationship.

D

Applying in Real-Life Contexts

Connect maths / science to the real world

Level 3–4: Limited
Identifies the problem but doesn't use mathematics / science to solve it.
Example — Budgeting investigation

We need to buy paint for the classroom. I think we need a lot of paint. It will cost some money.

↑ To move to 5–6: Calculate the area to be painted. Find the cost per litre. Show your working with real numbers.
Level 5–6: Substantial
Example — Budgeting investigation

The classroom wall is 8 m × 3 m, so the area is 24 m². One litre of paint covers approximately 10 m², so we need 24 ÷ 10 = 2.4 litres. Since paint is sold in whole litres, we should buy 3 litres. At ¥89 per litre, the total cost is ¥267.

↑ To move to 7–8: Consider multiple options (different brands/sizes). Discuss accuracy and limitations. Make a justified recommendation.
Level 7–8: Excellent
Example — Budgeting investigation

After measuring the classroom wall (8 m × 3 m = 24 m²) and subtracting the window area (1.5 m × 1.2 m = 1.8 m²), the paintable area is 22.2 m². I researched two brands: Brand A covers 10 m²/L at ¥89/L, and Brand B covers 12 m²/L at ¥115/L. Brand A requires ⌈22.2 ÷ 10⌉ = 3 litres (¥267), while Brand B requires ⌈22.2 ÷ 12⌉ = 2 litres (¥230). I recommend Brand B because it is ¥37 cheaper overall, even though the unit price is higher. However, this assumes the coverage estimates are accurate — in practice, rough walls may need more paint, so buying an extra litre would be prudent.

1

2

3

4
