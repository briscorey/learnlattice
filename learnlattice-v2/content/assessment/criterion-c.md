## What Is MYP Criterion C?

Criterion C assesses how well students communicate their mathematical or scientific thinking. This goes far beyond "show your working." The IB expects students to use appropriate mathematical language, move between different forms of representation (tables, graphs, equations, diagrams), organise their reasoning into coherent arguments, and present their work so that another mathematician or scientist could follow it.

In practice, Criterion C is often assessed alongside Criterion B (in investigations) or Criterion D (in real-world applications). The key question is: can someone else read this work and understand not just the answer, but the reasoning behind it?

## Why Criterion C Matters

Many students can solve problems correctly but cannot explain their thinking. Criterion C rewards the student who writes clearly, labels their axes, defines their variables, and structures their argument logically — even if their mathematical answer is less sophisticated than a peer who scribbles the right formula without explanation.

This makes Criterion C especially important for multilingual learners. A student who communicates mathematical ideas clearly in their second language is demonstrating a genuine skill. ## Common Weaknesses in Criterion C

The most frequent issue is students who skip straight to calculations without stating what they are doing or why. At levels 1-2, work is often a jumble of numbers without labels, units, or explanation. At levels 3-4, students show their method but do not connect it to the question. The jump to 5-6 requires coherent structure — the work reads as an argument, not a collection of steps.

At levels 7-8, students move fluently between representations. They might describe a pattern verbally, show it in a table, graph the relationship, and write the algebraic rule — all connected by clear reasoning. This is rare and impressive when it happens.
