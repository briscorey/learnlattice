## What Is MYP Criterion B?

Criterion B is where MYP mathematics becomes genuinely different from traditional maths teaching. Instead of solving problems someone else has set up, students investigate patterns, formulate general rules, and provide mathematical justification for why those rules work.

This is the criterion that most teachers find hardest to assess — and the one students find most rewarding when it is taught well. A strong Criterion B investigation starts with a concrete, visual pattern that students can explore without being told what to look for. The best tasks are low-floor, high-ceiling: every student can draw the first few cases, but only the most capable students will prove the general rule algebraically.

## The Four Strands of Criterion B

At MYP Year 3 and above, Criterion B assesses four connected skills: selecting and applying problem-solving techniques to discover patterns, describing those patterns as relationships or general rules, verifying that the rules work for cases beyond the ones tested, and justifying why the rules are true. The jump from level 5-6 to level 7-8 almost always comes down to justification — can the student explain *why* the pattern works, not just *that* it works?

## How to Write a Good Criterion B Investigation

The best investigations start with something students can see and touch — blocks, matchsticks, dots, or shapes. The pattern should be genuinely interesting and lead to a rule that surprises students. Avoid investigations where the answer is obvious from the first three cases. For detailed guidance, read our blog post: [How to Write a Criterion B Investigation That Students Actually Want to Do](/blog/how-to-write-a-criterion-b-investigation/).

## Common Mistakes in Criterion B Assessment

The most common mistake is confusing description with justification. A student who writes "the rule is 2n + 1" has described the pattern. A student who explains *why* each new case adds 2 by referring to the geometric structure has justified it. These are different achievement levels.

Another frequent error is scaffolding too heavily. If the task tells students exactly what to investigate and how, they cannot demonstrate the "select and apply" strand at a high level. The best tasks provide an entry point but leave the direction of investigation open.
