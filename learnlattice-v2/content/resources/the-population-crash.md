## The Hook

In 1944, the US Coast Guard released 29 reindeer on St. Matthew Island in the Bering Sea. The island had no predators and abundant lichen.

By 1963, the population had exploded to **6,000 reindeer**.

By 1966, there were **42 left**. The rest had starved.

**What happened?** And could mathematics have predicted the crash?

---

## Model the Growth

### Phase 1: Exponential Growth

Start with 10 deer. Each year, the population doubles.

| Year | Population | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 10 | — |
| 1 | 20 | +10 |
| 2 | 40 | +20 |
| 3 | 80 | +40 |
| 5 | 320 | |
| 10 | 10,240 | |

Plot this on a graph. This is **exponential growth** — the J-curve.

### Phase 2: The Carrying Capacity

The island can sustain a maximum of 2,000 deer (based on lichen regrowth rate). This is the **carrying capacity** (K).

What happens when the population exceeds K?
- More deer than food → starvation
- Weakened deer → disease spreads
- Less lichen → soil erosion → even less food next year
- **Positive feedback loop → population crash**

### Phase 3: The S-Curve vs The Crash

In a stable ecosystem, population growth slows as it approaches K, forming an **S-curve** (logistic growth).

But if the population **overshoots** K — grows past it before the braking effect kicks in — the crash can be catastrophic. The reindeer ate the lichen faster than it could regrow. Once the lichen was gone, there was nothing to eat.

---

## Simulation

Use dice or a spreadsheet to model:
1. **Birth rate:** Roll a die for each group of 10 deer. 1-4 = 10 new deer. 5-6 = 0 new deer.
2. **Death rate:** If population > K, roll for each group of 10. 1-3 = 10 die. 4-6 = survive.
3. **Resource recovery:** Each year, lichen regrows by 10% of what was consumed.

Run the simulation for 20 years. Plot the population. Do you get a crash?

---

## Teaching Notes

The St. Matthew Island reindeer story is one of the most dramatic case studies in ecology. It is real, well-documented, and mathematically rich. Students who see the numbers understand carrying capacity viscerally — not as a definition to memorise, but as a limit that kills when exceeded.

The simulation connects mathematics to ecology: exponential growth, carrying capacity, and feedback loops are all concepts that students model with dice and graphs before encountering the equations.
