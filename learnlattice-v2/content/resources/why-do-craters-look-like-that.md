## The Hook

Show students a photograph of the Tycho crater on the Moon. The bright rays radiating outward extend for hundreds of kilometres. Ask:

**"This crater is 85 kilometres wide. It was made by a rock about 10 kilometres across, travelling at 20 kilometres per second. It happened 108 million years ago. How do we know all of that — from a photograph taken from Earth?"**

The answer is that craters are not just holes. They are recordings. Every crater preserves information about the speed, size, angle, and energy of the impact that created it. And today, you are going to learn to read them.

## Why This Investigation Matters

Crater counting is how planetary scientists date surfaces across the solar system. A surface with many craters is old. A surface with few craters is young — something resurfaced it (lava, ice, erosion). The Moon has no atmosphere and no plate tectonics, so its craters are preserved for billions of years. It is the solar system's memory.

By the end of this investigation, students can look at any cratered surface — the Moon, Mars, Mercury, a moon of Jupiter — and read its history. That is genuine scientific literacy.

## Core Concepts

### Impact mechanics

When an object hits a surface at high speed, its kinetic energy converts to heat and pressure in milliseconds. The resulting shockwave excavates material in all directions — this is why craters are circular even when the impact angle is oblique. The ejecta (thrown-out material) forms the bright rays visible around fresh craters.

### Energy and crater size

The diameter of a crater depends on the energy of the impact, which depends on both mass and velocity: KE = ½mv². Since velocity is squared, a small fast object carries far more energy than a large slow one. Doubling the speed quadruples the energy.

### Reading the record

Older craters are eroded and overlapped by newer ones. Fresh craters have sharp rims and bright rays. By counting craters of different sizes and degradation states, scientists estimate the age of a surface — a technique called crater counting.

## Investigation Tasks

**Part A — The height-crater relationship**

Drop a marble into the flour tray from heights of 20cm, 40cm, 60cm, 80cm, and 100cm. Smooth the surface between each trial. For each height, measure crater diameter, crater depth, and the length of the longest ejecta ray. Three trials per height. Plot crater diameter against drop height.

**Part B — The angle of impact**

Drop the marble at different angles. How does the crater shape change? How does the ejecta pattern change? Photograph each crater and compare to images of real lunar craters at oblique angles.

**Part C — Connecting to the Moon**

Compare your best flour crater photographs to images of Tycho, Copernicus, and Aristarchus. Identify: crater rim, central peak (if any), ejecta rays, secondary craters. What features appear at both scales? What features only appear at planetary scale?

## Formative Assessment Checkpoints

After Part A, students should be able to state that higher drops produce larger craters. After Part B, they should recognise that angle affects crater shape. By Part C, they should articulate how laboratory models connect to real planetary science — and acknowledge the limitations of the model (speed, scale, atmosphere).

## Common Misconceptions

Students often believe craters are shaped by the impactor sitting in the ground, like a ball in sand. The key insight is that at high speeds, the impact is an explosion — the impactor often vaporises completely. The crater is shaped by the shockwave, not by the object.

Another common error: assuming all craters are the same age. On Earth, weathering and plate tectonics erase craters. On the Moon, they accumulate for billions of years. This difference is what makes the Moon so valuable for understanding solar system history.
