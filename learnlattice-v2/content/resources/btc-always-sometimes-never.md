## The Hook

Sort each statement into **Always True**, **Sometimes True**, or **Never True**.

Then **prove** your classification — examples alone are not enough for "Always" or "Never."

---

## The Statements

1. The sum of two odd numbers is even.
2. The product of two prime numbers is odd.
3. A square number has an odd number of factors.
4. The sum of three consecutive numbers is divisible by 3.
5. An even number divided by 2 gives an odd number.
6. The difference between two square numbers is odd.
7. A prime number is odd.
8. The product of any number and zero is zero.
9. Adding 1 to a square number gives a prime number.
10. The sum of the digits of a multiple of 9 is also a multiple of 9.
11. An odd number multiplied by an odd number gives an odd number.
12. The cube of a negative number is negative.

> **Work at your vertical whiteboard.** Sort first by intuition, then test with examples, then try to prove.

---

## The Key Distinction

For **Sometimes True** statements, you need:
- At least one example where it IS true
- At least one example where it is NOT true

For **Always True** or **Never True**, examples are not enough. You need a **proof** or **logical argument** that covers all possible cases.

> Statement 1: "The sum of two odd numbers is even." Let the odd numbers be (2a + 1) and (2b + 1). Their sum is 2a + 2b + 2 = 2(a + b + 1), which is always even. ✓

## Teaching Notes

**BTC Practices Used:** Card sort activity at vertical surfaces, random groups. The statements can be printed on cards for physical sorting.

**MYP Criterion B Connection:** The "prove your classification" requirement directly assesses the verification and justification strands. Students must go beyond checking examples to provide general arguments.

**MYP Criterion C Connection:** The mathematical arguments require precise language and logical structure.

**Differentiation:** Remove statements 4, 6, 10, and 12 for Grade 6. Add "The sum of two irrational numbers is irrational" for Grade 9.

**Common Misconception:** Students often classify statement 2 as Always True (forgetting that 2 is prime and even, making 2 × 3 = 6 even). This is a powerful teaching moment about the danger of assuming patterns from insufficient examples.
