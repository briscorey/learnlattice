## The Hook

A pharmacist receives a prescription for 0.125 mg of a drug. The available tablets are 0.5 mg each. How many tablets — or what fraction of a tablet — does the patient need?

0.125 ÷ 0.5 = 0.25 — a quarter tablet.

Now consider: a nurse misreads 0.125 mg as 1.25 mg and administers ten times the correct dose. Decimal errors in medicine are one of the leading causes of preventable harm in hospitals worldwide. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices specifically trains staff to write "0.125 mg" not ".125 mg" (no leading zero = easy to misread as "1.25").

Decimal fluency isn't a school exercise — it's a professional skill that carries real consequences.

## Core Concepts: Step by Step

### 1. Rounding Decimals

Rounding is not about laziness — it's about communicating the *right level of precision* for the context.

**The rule:**
- Look at the digit immediately to the *right* of the place you are rounding to.
- If it is 5 or more → round up (increase the target digit by 1).
- If it is less than 5 → round down (keep the target digit the same).
- Remove all digits to the right of the target place.

**Step-by-step: round 3.4762 to 2 decimal places (hundredths)**
1. Target place: hundredths → the digit is **7**.
2. Look one place right: thousandths digit is **6**.
3. 6 ≥ 5 → round up: 7 becomes **8**.
4. Answer: **3.48**

**Context matters:**
- A pharmacist rounds to 3 decimal places (0.001 mg precision matters).
- A supermarket rounds to 2 decimal places (cents).
- A weather forecaster rounds to 1 decimal place (0.1°C is enough).
- A builder rounds to the nearest whole centimetre.

### 2. Estimation with Decimals

A well-trained estimator catches errors before they cause problems.

**Front-end estimation:** use only the whole number parts.
- 6.83 + 4.27 ≈ 6 + 4 = 10 (exact: 11.1 — front-end under-estimates)

**Compatible numbers:** adjust to numbers that are easy to work with.
- 49.7 × 2.1 ≈ 50 × 2 = 100 (exact: 104.37)

**Step-by-step: estimate 18.4 ÷ 3.7**
1. Round: 18 ÷ 4 = 4.5 (or use 20 ÷ 4 = 5 for easier numbers)
2. Exact answer: 4.97... → estimate of ~5 is good.
3. If a student got 49.7, the estimate tells them immediately it's wrong.

### 3. Multi-Step Decimal Problems

Most real problems involve several operations. The key skill is planning the sequence before calculating.

**Worked example — Currency conversion:**
You have $250 AUD. The exchange rate to JPY is 96.43 yen per dollar. Airport commission: 2.5%. How many yen do you receive?

Step 1: Calculate commission deducted.
$250 × 0.025 = $6.25
Remaining: $250 - $6.25 = **$243.75**

Step 2: Convert to yen.
$243.75 × 96.43 = ?
Estimate first: 244 × 96 ≈ 244 × 100 = 24,400 yen

Calculate: 243.75 × 96.43 = **23,504.8125**

Round to nearest whole yen (you can't have a fraction of a yen): **23,505 yen**

> This type of problem appears in every international business transaction, online purchase in a foreign currency, and travel budget.

### 4. Decimal Operations and Unit Conversion

Many unit conversions are simply decimal multiplications or divisions by powers of 10.

| Conversion | Operation | Example |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| km → m | × 1000 | 2.4 km = 2400 m |
| m → cm | × 100 | 1.85 m = 185 cm |
| kg → g | × 1000 | 0.375 kg = 375 g |
| L → mL | × 1000 | 0.5 L = 500 mL |
| m → km | ÷ 1000 | 850 m = 0.85 km |

**Step-by-step: convert 3.6 km to cm**
1. km → m: 3.6 × 1000 = 3600 m
2. m → cm: 3600 × 100 = **360,000 cm**

Or directly: 1 km = 100,000 cm, so 3.6 × 100,000 = **360,000 cm**

### 5. Significant Figures

In science and measurement, significant figures communicate the precision of a measurement.

**Rules for identifying significant figures:**
1. All non-zero digits are significant: 3.47 → 3 sig figs
2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant: 3.047 → 4 sig figs
3. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant: 3.470 → 4 sig figs (the zero was written deliberately)
4. Leading zeros are not significant: 0.0047 → 2 sig figs (the zeros are just placeholders)

**Step-by-step: round 0.048621 to 3 significant figures**
1. Identify sig figs: 0.0**4**8621 → first sig fig is 4
2. Count 3: 4, 8, 6 → target place is the third (hundredths-of-thousandths = 6)
3. Digit to right is 2 → round down
4. Answer: **0.0486**

## Learning Flow (4 Weeks)

**Week 1 — Precision and Rounding**
- Open with the medication dosage scenario. Ask: what's the difference between 0.125 and 1.25? Between 1.25 and 12.5?
- Context-based rounding: students receive five different professional scenarios and must justify what level of rounding is appropriate for each.
- Significant figures introduction — tie to measurement with rulers and measuring cylinders.

**Week 2 — Estimation and Mental Strategies**
- Estimation warm-ups: daily 5-minute "is it closer to X or Y?" decimal estimation challenges.
- Error detection: students mark a set of calculator outputs as "reasonable" or "suspicious" based on estimation. They must explain their reasoning.
- Mental multiplication tricks: × 0.5 = ÷ 2, × 0.25 = ÷ 4, × 1.5 = × 1 + × 0.5.

**Week 3 — Multi-Step Problems in Context**
- Financial literacy contexts: GST calculations (10% = × 1.1), currency conversion, unit pricing, pay calculations at hourly rates with part-hours.
- Science contexts: converting between SI units, calculating average speed (distance ÷ time where both are decimals), mixing concentrations.
- Students write their own multi-step decimal problem set for a context of their choice, with solutions.

**Week 4 — Summative Assessment**
- A cross-context task: students are given a travel planning scenario. They must convert a budget between currencies, calculate distances in multiple units, compare value for money across different options, and justify their final recommendation with precise decimal calculations.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often think | What's accurate |
|--------------------------|----------------|
| More decimal places = more accurate | Precision must match the measurement tool and context |
| 3.470 and 3.47 are identical | 3.470 signals one more decimal place of precision was measured |
| Leading zeros count as significant | 0.0047 has 2 sig figs; the zeros are positional placeholders |
| Round at every step of a multi-step problem | Round only at the final answer to avoid accumulated rounding error |

---

> **Teaching Tip:** The "round only at the final step" rule is counterintuitive but crucial. Show students two solutions to the same problem — one where rounding happens at each step, one where it doesn't. The difference in the final answer is your most persuasive argument.
