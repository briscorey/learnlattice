## The Hook

Your brain takes time to process information. The delay between seeing something and responding is called your **reaction time**.

How fast are you? And is your dominant hand faster than your non-dominant hand?

---

## The Experiment

### The Ruler Drop Test

1. Partner holds a 30cm ruler vertically, with 0cm at the bottom
2. Your thumb and finger are open at the 0cm mark
3. Without warning, your partner drops the ruler
4. Catch it as fast as you can
5. Record the distance where you caught it (cm)

### Convert distance to time

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}}$$

where d = distance in metres, g = 9.8 m/s²

| Distance caught (cm) | Reaction time (ms) |
|---|---|
| 5 | 101 |
| 10 | 143 |
| 15 | 175 |
| 20 | 202 |
| 25 | 226 |
| 30 | 247 |

### Collect Data
- 10 trials with your dominant hand
- 10 trials with your non-dominant hand

---

## Analyse

For each hand, calculate:
- **Mean** reaction time
- **Range** (highest - lowest)
- **Any outliers?** (did you flinch early? Were you distracted?)

**Draw two box plots** side by side. Compare.

> Is the difference between hands **bigger** than the spread within each hand? If the box plots overlap heavily, the difference might not be meaningful.

---

## Teaching Notes

This investigation teaches the most important idea in statistics: that comparing averages is not enough. Two means can differ by 15ms, but if the standard deviations are 30ms each, the difference is meaningless. The box plots make this visible.

Every student produces unique data from their own body — which creates ownership and motivation. The physics formula for converting distance to time is a bonus cross-curricular connection.
