## The Hook

A friend says: "I can always tell Coke from Pepsi."

You decide to test them. You prepare 10 cups. 5 Coke, 5 Pepsi. in random order. They taste each one and guess.

**They get 7 out of 10 correct.**

Are they really tasting the difference? Or did they just get lucky?

---

## The Mathematics of Guessing

If someone is **purely guessing** between two options, they have a 50% chance of being right each time. In 10 trials, we would expect **5 correct by chance alone**.

But getting exactly 5 is not guaranteed. Sometimes a guesser gets 6. Or 7. Even 8. by pure luck.

### How likely is 7 out of 10 by guessing?

Use coin flips to simulate. Flip 10 coins (heads = correct guess). Record how many heads. Repeat 30 times.

| Heads out of 10 | Tally | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 0-3 | | |
| 4 | | |
| 5 | | |
| 6 | | |
| 7 | | |
| 8+ | | |

> **How often did a guesser score 7 or more?** If it happened 15% of the time, then 7/10 is not very impressive. it could easily be luck.

### The Standard

In science, the conventional threshold is **5%**. If there is less than a 5% chance of getting this result by guessing, we say the result is **statistically significant**.

For 10 trials with p = 0.5: getting **9 or more correct** has probability 1.1%. That is significant. Getting **7 correct** has probability 17.2%. **Not significant.**

---

## Design a Better Test

If 10 trials is not enough, how many would you need?

> **Key insight:** More trials = more power to detect a real difference. In 20 trials, scoring 15/20 has a 2.1% probability by chance. now it IS significant.

---

## Teaching Notes

This investigation teaches p-values and statistical significance without ever using those words. Students discover the concept through simulation and coin flipping.

The taste test is engaging because students WANT to believe their friend has a special ability. The mathematics shows that what feels impressive (7/10) is actually within the range of luck. This is a formative experience in critical thinking.
