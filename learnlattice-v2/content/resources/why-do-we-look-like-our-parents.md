## The Hook

People say you have your mother's eyes. Your father's nose. Your grandmother's curly hair.

But you are not a photocopy of any of them. You are a unique combination that has never existed before and will never exist again.

**How does information travel from parent to child? And why do siblings — who have the same parents — look different from each other?**

Two coins and ten minutes will show you.

---

## Part 1: One Gene, Two Coins

Each coin represents one parent. Heads = dominant allele (B). Tails = recessive allele (b).

**Flip both coins together 20 times. Record each result:**

| Outcome | Genotype | What it looks like | Tally | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Both heads | BB | Dominant trait (e.g. brown eyes) | | |
| One head, one tail | Bb | Dominant trait (carrier) | | |
| Both tails | bb | Recessive trait (e.g. blue eyes) | | |

> **Prediction:** Before you start, what ratio do you expect? Write it down.

### Class Data

Pool everyone's results on the board. With 500+ flips, the ratio should approach **3:1** (dominant : recessive).

> This is the same ratio Gregor Mendel found with pea plants in 1866. He did not know about DNA. He just counted very, very carefully.

---

## Part 2: Why Siblings Are Different

Now use **two pairs** of coins. Each pair represents one gene.

- Pair 1: Hair (Heads = dark, Tails = light)
- Pair 2: Freckles (Heads = freckles, Tails = no freckles)

Flip all four coins 10 times. Record the combination each time.

> **How many unique combinations did you get out of 10 flips?**

With just 2 genes, there are 9 possible combinations. With 3 genes, there are 27. With 20,000 genes (the human genome), the number of possible combinations from two parents is greater than the number of atoms in the observable universe.

**That is why you are unique.**

---

## The Punnett Square

The coin flips gave you the data. The Punnett square explains the data.

Draw a 2×2 grid. Put one parent's alleles across the top, the other down the side. Fill in the combinations.

|  | **B** | **b** |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | BB | Bb |
| **b** | Bb | bb |

Three out of four boxes show the dominant trait. One shows recessive. **The 3:1 ratio is not luck — it is mathematics.**

---

## Teaching Notes

This is a Bruner CPA masterclass. The coins are the **concrete** model. Students physically experience the randomness of inheritance before encountering the abstract Punnett square. The square then becomes a tool for explaining what they already discovered, not a formula to memorise.

Pool the class data on the board — this is where the magic happens. Individual results are noisy (8:12 instead of 15:5), but the class total will be close to 3:1. This naturally teaches the concept of sample size without a single mention of statistics.

Jo Boaler's influence: there is no speed pressure. Students who need more time can flip more coins. Students who finish early can model three or four genes simultaneously. The investigation has a low floor and no ceiling.
