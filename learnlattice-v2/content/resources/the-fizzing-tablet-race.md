## The Hook

Drop an antacid tablet into a glass of cold water. Start a timer. Wait for the fizzing to stop.

Now drop an identical tablet into hot water.

**It finishes in half the time.** Same tablet. Same water. Different temperature. Why?

---

## The Experiment

### Question
How does temperature affect the rate of reaction between an antacid tablet and water?

### Method
1. Fill 5 beakers with 100mL of water at different temperatures: 10°C, 20°C, 30°C, 40°C, 50°C
2. Drop one antacid tablet into each. Start timing immediately
3. Record the time for fizzing to stop completely
4. Plot a graph: temperature (x-axis) vs time to dissolve (y-axis)

### Variables
- **Independent:** Temperature of water
- **Dependent:** Time for tablet to dissolve
- **Controlled:** Volume of water, brand/size of tablet, same beaker size

---

## Analyse

> Your graph should show a **curve** — not a straight line. As temperature increases, the time decreases, but not at a constant rate.

**Why?** Higher temperature means particles move faster. Faster particles collide more often and with more energy. More energetic collisions means more successful reactions per second.

This is **collision theory**: for a reaction to happen, particles must collide with enough energy and in the correct orientation.

---

## Extension: Surface Area

Repeat the experiment at room temperature with:
- A whole tablet
- A tablet broken in half
- A tablet broken into quarters
- A crushed tablet

**The crushed tablet reacts fastest.** More surface area means more particles are exposed to the water at once — more collisions per second.

---

## Teaching Notes

Antacid tablets are cheap, dramatic, and safe. The fizzing is visible, audible, and timed — three sensory channels of data. This is Papert's constructionism: students build their understanding of reaction rates from direct experience before encountering collision theory.

The temperature graph is reliably non-linear, which teaches an important lesson: not all relationships are straight lines. Students who expect y = mx + b are surprised — and that surprise creates a genuine need to understand why.
