## The Hook

A machinist needs to cut a steel rod that is 7½ metres long into pieces that are each ¾ of a metre. How many pieces can she cut?

7½ ÷ ¾ = ?

Most students freeze here. But this is just a real question that factories, construction sites, and kitchens face every day. The answer — 10 complete pieces — requires understanding what dividing by a fraction actually *means*.

This unit resolves that confusion permanently by building from visual models to symbolic rules, with the reasoning always coming before the procedure.

---

## MYP Curriculum Focus

- **MYP Big idea:** Multiplication and division of fractions extend the same operations from whole numbers — the meaning of each operation doesn't change, only the numbers involved.
- MYP content links: multiplication of fractions and mixed numbers, division of fractions using reciprocals, real-world problem solving, and connections to ratio and proportion.

## Inquiry Questions

**Factual:** What does it mean to multiply or divide by a number less than 1?

**Conceptual:** Why does dividing by a fraction give a larger result than the original?

**Debatable:** Should students be required to understand *why* "keep, change, flip" works, or is knowing the rule sufficient?

## Core Concepts: Step by Step

### 1. Multiplying Fractions — The Rule and the Reason

**The rule:** multiply numerators together, multiply denominators together.

$$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}$$

**But why?** Consider ½ × ¾.

This means: "take ½ of ¾." Visually: draw a rectangle. Shade ¾ of it horizontally (3 out of 4 columns). Now shade ½ of *that* vertically (1 out of 2 rows). The overlap is 3 out of 8 equal pieces = **3/8**.

½ × ¾ = (1×3)/(2×4) = **3/8** ✓

**Step-by-step: ⅔ × 5/7**
1. Multiply numerators: 2 × 5 = **10**
2. Multiply denominators: 3 × 7 = **21**
3. Simplify if possible: 10/21 — no common factors, already in simplest form.
4. Answer: **10/21**

**Simplifying before multiplying (cross-cancellation):**
⅘ × 15/16:
- 4 and 16 share a factor of 4: 4÷4=1, 16÷4=4
- 15 and 5 share a factor of 5: 15÷5=3, 5÷5=1
- Now multiply: (1×3)/(1×4) = **3/4**

This gives the same answer as multiplying first (60/80 = 3/4) but with smaller numbers.

**Multiplying mixed numbers:**
Always convert to improper fractions first.

**Step-by-step: 2⅓ × 1¾**
1. Convert: 2⅓ = 7/3; 1¾ = 7/4
2. Multiply: (7×7)/(3×4) = 49/12
3. Convert back: 49 ÷ 12 = 4 remainder 1 → **4 1/12**

### 2. What Happens When You Multiply by a Fraction?

Multiplying by a proper fraction (less than 1) makes the result *smaller* than the original. This surprises many students.

| Calculation | Result | What happened |
|------------|--------|---------------|
| 12 × ½ | 6 | Result is half of 12 |
| 12 × ¾ | 9 | Result is ¾ of 12 |
| 12 × 1¼ | 15 | Result is bigger (multiplier > 1) |
| ½ × ½ | ¼ | Half of a half is a quarter |

> **Key understanding:** Multiplying by a fraction less than 1 is the same as taking a *part* of the original. This is why "× ½" means the same as "÷ 2".

### 3. Dividing Fractions — What It Means

Division asks: "how many of *this* fit into *that*?"

**Whole number intuition:** 12 ÷ 3 asks "how many groups of 3 fit into 12?" Answer: 4.

**Fraction division:** 3 ÷ ½ asks "how many halves fit into 3?" Each whole contains 2 halves, so 3 wholes contain **6 halves**. Answer: 6.

Notice: 3 ÷ ½ = 3 × 2 = 6. Dividing by ½ is the same as multiplying by 2.

**This generalises:** dividing by a/b is the same as multiplying by b/a (the reciprocal).

$$\frac{p}{q} \div \frac{a}{b} = \frac{p}{q} \times \frac{b}{a}$$

This is "keep, change, flip" — but the *reason* is that flipping the divisor gives its reciprocal, and multiplying by the reciprocal undoes the division.

**Step-by-step: ¾ ÷ ⅜**
1. Keep: ¾
2. Change ÷ to ×
3. Flip: ⅜ → 8/3
4. Multiply: ¾ × 8/3 = (3×8)/(4×3) = 24/12 = **2**

Check: does ¾ ÷ ⅜ = 2 make sense? "How many ⅜ fit into ¾?" ⅜ + ⅜ = 6/8 = ¾ ✓ Exactly 2 fit.

**Step-by-step with mixed numbers: 7½ ÷ ¾ (the machinist problem)**
1. Convert: 7½ = 15/2
2. Keep, change, flip: 15/2 × 4/3
3. Simplify before multiplying: 15 and 3 share factor 3 (15÷3=5, 3÷3=1); 4 and 2 share factor 2 (4÷2=2, 2÷2=1)
4. Multiply: (5×2)/(1×1) = 10/1 = **10**

The machinist can cut **10 complete pieces**.

### 4. Fraction Operations in Algebra

Fraction operations appear constantly in algebra. The same rules apply.

**Simplifying algebraic fractions:**
(3x/4) × (8/x) = 24x/4x = **6** (for x ≠ 0)

**Step-by-step: (2a/5) ÷ (4a/15)**
1. Keep, change, flip: (2a/5) × (15/4a)
2. Simplify: 2 and 4 → ÷2; 15 and 5 → ÷5; a cancels
3. = (1 × 3)/(1 × 2) = **3/2**

## Learning Flow (3–4 Weeks)

**Week 1 — Multiplication: Meaning Before Method**
- Open with: "Is ½ × ¾ bigger or smaller than ¾?" Students predict and justify before calculating.
- Area model: students draw rectangle models for at least 3 fraction multiplications, identify the overlap, and connect to the symbolic rule.
- Fraction of a quantity problems: ⅖ of 35, ¾ of 48 — connect to multiplication.
- Cross-cancellation practice: compare "multiply first then simplify" vs "simplify first then multiply" — same answer, different effort.

**Week 2 — Division: Building the Concept**
- Grouping problems: "how many ½ metres of ribbon in 4 metres?" "how many ¾ cup servings in 6 cups?" Students solve by drawing or counting first.
- Introduce the pattern: 4 ÷ ½ = 8, 4 ÷ ¼ = 16, 4 ÷ ⅛ = 32. Ask: what's the pattern? Why does dividing by smaller fractions give bigger answers?
- Develop keep-change-flip from the reciprocal reasoning — students should be able to explain *why*, not just perform.
- Mixed number division: convert first, then apply.

**Week 3 — Applications and Multi-Step Problems**
- Cooking at scale: recipes require both × and ÷ fractions. Scale a recipe for a non-standard number of servings.
- Construction and measurement: cutting materials, tiling, fabric.
- Financial contexts: splitting costs, calculating fractional portions of a budget.
- Students write and solve their own fraction word problems, swap with a partner.

**Week 4 — Summative Assessment**
- A multi-step task: students receive a project scenario (e.g. organising a school event with a budget, materials to cut, and servings to calculate) requiring multiplication, division, and mixed numbers throughout. Written justification for each calculation step required.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often think | What's accurate |
|--------------------------|----------------|
| Multiplying always makes bigger | Multiplying by a fraction less than 1 makes the result *smaller* |
| Dividing always makes smaller | Dividing by a fraction less than 1 makes the result *bigger* |
| Keep-change-flip is just a trick | It comes from multiplying by the reciprocal, which undoes the division |
| Mixed numbers: multiply whole numbers and fractions separately | Must convert to improper fractions first to avoid errors |

---

> **Teaching Tip:** The single most effective activity in this unit is the grouping model for division. Students who physically draw "how many ¾ fit into 3?" on a number line before seeing the algorithm develop genuine understanding of why the result is larger than the dividend. The algorithm then feels inevitable rather than arbitrary.
