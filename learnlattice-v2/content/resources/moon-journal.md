## The Hook

For the next 28 nights, you will look at the Moon and draw exactly what you see.

Not what you think it should look like. What you actually see. The shape. The direction of the bright side. The position in the sky.

After 28 days, your drawings will reveal a pattern that humans have known for 30,000 years. but that most modern students have never actually observed.

---

## Your Moon Journal

Every clear night (skip cloudy nights), observe the Moon and record:

| Night | Date | Time | Draw what you see | Direction of bright side | Position in sky |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | | | |
| 2 | | | | | |
| ... | | | | | |
| 28 | | | | | |

### Tips for observing
- Look at the same time each night (early evening works best)
- Note which direction the lit side faces (left or right)
- If you cannot see the Moon, it might have already set. or it might be a new moon

---

## After 28 Days

Lay out all your drawings in order. You should see:

1. **New Moon** → dark circle (or invisible)
2. **Waxing crescent** → thin sliver, lit on the RIGHT
3. **First quarter** → right half lit
4. **Waxing gibbous** → mostly lit
5. **Full Moon** → fully lit
6. **Waning gibbous** → mostly lit, left side
7. **Third quarter** → left half lit
8. **Waning crescent** → thin sliver, lit on the LEFT
9. Back to new Moon

> **The cycle takes 29.5 days.** This is why months are roughly 30 days. the word "month" comes from "Moon."

---

## The Model

Use a lamp (Sun), a ball (Moon), and your head (Earth). In a dark room, hold the ball at arm's length and slowly orbit it around your head. Watch the illuminated fraction change. you will see every phase.

> **The Moon does not produce light.** It reflects sunlight. The phases are caused by our changing viewing angle as the Moon orbits Earth.

---

## Teaching Notes

This is the slowest investigation on LearnLattice. and possibly the most powerful. Over 28 nights, students develop a personal relationship with an astronomical cycle that most humans have lost touch with.

Start this investigation at the new moon (check an astronomy app for the date). Students who begin at full moon will struggle to observe the waning phases because they occur after midnight.

The lamp-and-ball model should happen AFTER the observations, not before. Let students discover the pattern from real data, then explain it with the model. This is Papert in its purest form.
