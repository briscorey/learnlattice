## The Hook

An earthquake happens somewhere under the ocean. Nobody saw it. Nobody felt it.

But three seismograph stations — one in Tokyo, one in Sydney, one in Manila — each recorded the exact time the waves arrived.

Tokyo: 14:03:22. Sydney: 14:05:47. Manila: 14:02:58.

**Using only these times and the speed of seismic waves, can you find the epicentre?**

---

## The Method: Triangulation

Seismic waves travel at approximately 8 km/s through the Earth's crust.

### Step 1: Calculate distance from each station

$$\text{distance} = \text{speed} \times \text{time delay}$$

If Station A detected the earthquake 50 seconds before Station B, then Station B is 50 × 8 = 400 km further from the epicentre.

### Step 2: Draw circles

On a map, draw a circle around each station with a radius equal to its calculated distance from the epicentre.

### Step 3: Find the intersection

The epicentre is where all three circles intersect. Two circles give you two possible points. Three circles give you one.

---

## Practice Problem

Three stations detect an earthquake:

| Station | Time of detection | Distance from epicentre |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 seconds | ? |
| B | 30 seconds later | ? |
| C | 50 seconds later | ? |

Using the map provided, draw circles and locate the epicentre.

---

## Extension: Why Three Stations?

With one station, you know the distance but not the direction — the epicentre could be anywhere on a circle.

With two stations, the circles intersect at two points — you have narrowed it to two possibilities.

With three stations, there is only one point where all three circles meet. **This is triangulation.**

GPS works on exactly the same principle — but with satellites instead of seismograph stations, and radio waves instead of seismic waves.

---

## Teaching Notes

This investigation connects mathematics (circles, distance, speed-time calculations) to earth science in a way that feels genuinely useful. Students are solving a real problem that real seismologists solve.

Print a simplified map with three marked stations. Provide compasses for drawing circles. The physical act of drawing circles and finding the intersection point is far more powerful than calculating coordinates.
