## The Hook

A single sheet of A4 paper weighs 5 grams. It bends under its own weight.

But engineers build bridges from paper, cardboard, and bamboo that hold thousands of times their own weight.

**Your challenge:** Build a bridge from ONE sheet of paper that spans a 20cm gap between two tables. No tape. No glue. Just folds. How much mass can it hold?

---

## The Rules

- **Material:** One sheet of A4 paper (80gsm)
- **Span:** 20cm gap between two books or tables
- **Load:** Add weights (coins, washers, or marbles) until it collapses
- **No adhesives.** Only folding, rolling, and interlocking

### Round 1 — Individual (10 minutes)
Each student builds their own bridge. Test it. Record the maximum load.

### Round 2 — Engineering teams (15 minutes)
Groups of 3. One new sheet. You have seen what works and what fails. Combine ideas. Build the strongest bridge you can.

### The Weigh-In
Test every bridge. Record the results on the board. Crown the winner.

---

## The Science: Why Shape Matters

A flat sheet bends because all the material is at the same height — there is nothing resisting the downward force.

A **corrugated** sheet (zigzag folds) is strong because the peaks and valleys create depth. The top of each fold resists compression while the bottom resists tension.

A **tube** is even stronger. Roll the paper into a cylinder and it can support remarkable loads — because force is distributed around the entire circumference.

This is why bridges use **I-beams** (shaped like the letter I) rather than flat bars. The top and bottom flanges resist bending while the thin web between them saves material.

$$\text{Strength} \propto \text{depth}^2$$

Doubling the depth of a beam makes it **four times** stronger.

---

## Teaching Notes

This is a Liljedahl vertical-surface investigation — groups should be random, working standing up, testing and iterating physically. The competition element drives genuine engineering thinking: students who fail learn from the winning designs.

No equipment needed beyond paper, coins, and two books. It works in any classroom in any school. The mathematical relationship (strength ∝ depth²) is an extension for high-achieving students — the core lesson is that form affects function.
