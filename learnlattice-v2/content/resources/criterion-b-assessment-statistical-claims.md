## The Assessment Task

### Is the Headline True?

A news article claims: **"Teenagers spend an average of 7 hours per day on screens."**

**Part (i):** Design a survey to collect screen time data from students in your school. Describe your method, sample size, and how you will minimise bias.

**Part (ii):** Collect your data. Present it using at least two appropriate statistical displays. Calculate the mean, median, and interquartile range.

**Part (iii):** Based on your data, evaluate the newspaper's claim. Is 7 hours per day consistent with what you found? Describe any patterns in your data (e.g., differences between genders, year levels, weekdays vs. weekends).

**Part (iv):** Discuss the limitations of both the newspaper's claim and your own investigation. What would you need to know about the newspaper's study to properly evaluate it?

---

## Student Response A — Achieving Level 3-4

> **Method:** I asked 15 students in my class how many hours they use screens per day.
>
> **Data:** Mean = 5.2 hours. I made a bar chart.
>
> **Conclusion:** The newspaper is wrong because our average is 5.2, not 7.
>
> **Limitations:** My sample was small.

### Commentary

The survey is conducted but the sample is too small and not randomised (one class only). Only one statistical measure is calculated and one display created. The conclusion is definitive ("the newspaper is wrong") without acknowledging that different samples could give different results. The limitation is identified but not developed.

**What would move this to 5-6:** Larger sample, two displays, median + IQR, and a more nuanced conclusion.

---

## Student Response B — Achieving Level 5-6

> **Method:** Anonymous online survey sent to 60 students across Grades 7-9. Asked for weekday and weekend screen time separately.
>
> **Data:** Weekday mean = 4.8 hours (median 4.5). Weekend mean = 6.9 hours (median 7.0). Combined mean = 5.4 hours. Box plot and histogram presented.
>
> **Conclusion:** The claim of 7 hours is close to our weekend average but higher than our weekday average. The combined average of 5.4 hours is lower than claimed. The newspaper may have included weekend data more heavily, or their sample may have been older teenagers.
>
> **Limitations:** Self-reported data may be inaccurate. Students might underestimate screen time. We don't know the newspaper's sample size or methodology.

### Commentary

Excellent separation of weekday/weekend data reveals a pattern the task didn't explicitly ask for. The nuanced conclusion considers multiple explanations for the discrepancy rather than simply declaring the claim "right" or "wrong." Limitations identify both self-reporting bias and the need to evaluate the original study's methodology.

## Student Response C — Achieving Level 7-8

> [Includes everything from Response B, plus:]
>
> **Extended analysis:** The IQR for weekday screen time is 3.0-6.5 hours, meaning the newspaper's claim of 7 hours falls just outside our middle 50%. However, 22% of our sample reported 7+ hours on weekdays, so the claim is not impossible — it depends heavily on the population sampled.
>
> I also found that Grade 9 students averaged 5.8 hours on weekdays vs. 4.1 hours for Grade 7, suggesting screen time increases with age. If the newspaper surveyed 16-18 year olds rather than 12-15 year olds, 7 hours becomes very plausible.
>
> **Evaluation:** A key question is whether the newspaper's "average" means the mean or the median. If screen time is right-skewed (which my data suggests — a few very high users pull the mean up), the mean could be 7 hours even if most teenagers use less. The median would be a more representative measure.

### Commentary

This response demonstrates genuine statistical sophistication. The IQR analysis evaluates the claim probabilistically rather than as true/false. The age-trend analysis shows pattern recognition across subgroups. The discussion of mean vs. median skewness shows deep understanding of how statistical measures can be manipulated — exactly the kind of critical thinking Criterion B targets.

---

## Teaching Notes

This task works best when the newspaper claim is real — find one before the assessment. The authenticity of evaluating a genuine media claim elevates the task from "doing statistics" to "using statistics to think critically about the world."

The three annotated responses are designed for calibration: show all three in class and have students identify what makes each stronger.
