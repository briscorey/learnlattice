## The Hook

Here's an election result:

- **Blue party:** 60% of votes
- **Red party:** 40% of votes
- **Result:** Red wins 70% of the seats

No ballot stuffing. No voter fraud. No hacking. Everything was counted correctly.

**How is this possible?**

The answer is geometry.

---

## Part A: The Grid Challenge (Criterion B)

### Setup

Here is a 5×5 grid representing a region with 25 voters:

```
B B R B B
B R R B B
R B B R B
B R B B R
R R B R B
```

**B = Blue voter (15 total = 60%)**
**R = Red voter (10 total = 40%)**

You need to divide this grid into **5 districts of exactly 5 cells each.** Every cell must belong to exactly one district. Districts must be connected (each cell touches at least one other cell in its district by an edge, not just a corner).

### Challenge 1: Fair Result

Draw district boundaries so that **Blue wins 3 districts and Red wins 2** — proportional to the vote share.

### Challenge 2: Blue Landslide

Draw district boundaries so that **Blue wins all 5 districts.**

*(Hint: spread the Red voters so they're a minority in every district.)*

### Challenge 3: Red Upset

Draw district boundaries so that **Red wins 3 out of 5 districts** — a majority of seats despite having only 40% of voters.

*(This should feel impossible. Try it. When you succeed, you've just discovered gerrymandering.)*

### Reflection

How did you make Challenge 3 work? Describe your strategy.

You should have used one or both of these techniques:
- **Packing:** Cramming Blue voters into one or two districts where they win by huge margins (5-0 or 4-1), wasting their extra votes
- **Cracking:** Splitting remaining Blue voters across districts so they're always outnumbered 3-2

**These are the two fundamental tools of gerrymandering, and they are both about geometry.**

## Part B: Measuring Fairness with Compactness (Criterion C)

If gerrymandered districts have weird shapes, can we measure "weirdness" mathematically?

### The Polsby-Popper Score

This score compares a district's shape to a perfect circle (the most compact shape):

$$PP = \frac{4\pi \times A}{P^2}$$

Where A = area of the district and P = perimeter of the district.

- **PP = 1.0:** Perfect circle (maximally compact)
- **PP = 0.5:** Roughly square
- **PP < 0.2:** Very irregular — suspicious

### Practice

Calculate the Polsby-Popper score for these shapes:

| Shape | Area | Perimeter | PP Score | Compact? |
|-------|------|-----------|----------|----------|
| Circle | 78.5 cm² | 31.4 cm | | |
| Square | 100 cm² | 40 cm | | |
| Long rectangle (2×50) | 100 cm² | 104 cm | | |
| L-shape (calculate from grid) | | | | |

### Apply to Your Grid Districts

Go back to your Challenge 3 solution (where Red wins despite fewer voters).

For each of your 5 districts:
1. Count the area (number of cells)
2. Count the perimeter (number of exposed edges)
3. Calculate the Polsby-Popper score

**Are your gerrymandered districts less compact than your fair districts from Challenge 1?**

## Part C: The Efficiency Gap (Criterion B + D)

Compactness measures shape. But the **efficiency gap** measures something deeper — whether votes are being wasted.

### How It Works

In every district election, some votes are "wasted":
- **Losing votes:** Every vote for the losing candidate is wasted
- **Surplus votes:** For the winning candidate, every vote beyond the minimum needed to win is wasted

The efficiency gap = (Party A's wasted votes - Party B's wasted votes) / Total votes

An efficiency gap above **7%** is considered strong evidence of partisan gerrymandering (based on research by political scientists).

### Calculate It

Using your Challenge 1 (fair) and Challenge 3 (gerrymandered) maps:

**For each district, count:**

| District | Blue votes | Red votes | Winner | Blue wasted | Red wasted |
|----------|-----------|-----------|--------|-------------|------------|
| 1 | | | | | |
| 2 | | | | | |
| 3 | | | | | |
| 4 | | | | | |
| 5 | | | | | |
| **Total** | | | | | |

**Efficiency gap = |Blue wasted - Red wasted| / 25 × 100%**

Compare the efficiency gap for your fair map vs. your gerrymandered map. What do you notice?

## Part D: Real Districts (Criterion D)

### Analyse Real Maps

Your teacher will provide images of real electoral districts from different countries.

For each district:
1. Estimate its Polsby-Popper score (exact calculation not needed — compare to reference shapes)
2. Does it look compact or gerrymandered?
3. Research: Is there a historical or geographic reason for its shape?

### The Big Question

Write a response to:

> **"Should mathematical formulas determine how electoral boundaries are drawn?"**

Consider:
- **For:** Mathematics is objective. Politicians drawing their own boundaries is a conflict of interest. Compactness scores and efficiency gaps provide measurable, non-partisan criteria.
- **Against:** Mathematics doesn't understand communities. A river, a highway, a cultural boundary — these create natural districts that aren't compact. Minority representation may require non-compact districts. Who chooses the formula? That's still a political decision.
- **The nuance:** Maybe mathematics should inform boundaries without determining them. An independent commission using mathematical measures as guidelines, not rigid rules.

Your response must:
- Use specific mathematical evidence from Parts A, B, and C
- Consider perspectives from at least two different countries or contexts
- Acknowledge the limitations of mathematical approaches to political problems
- Arrive at a justified position — not "it depends" without substance

## Extension: Design Your Own Fairness Metric

The Polsby-Popper score and efficiency gap each capture something important but incomplete.

**Design your own mathematical measure of district fairness.** It should:
- Be calculable from publicly available data
- Produce a number that allows comparison between maps
- Account for at least one weakness of existing measures

Explain your metric with a formula, worked example, and justification for why it captures fairness better than (or differently from) existing measures.

## Teaching Notes

### Why This Investigation Is Unique

There is no MYP-level investigation on gerrymandering anywhere. This takes geometry — area, perimeter, ratios — and applies it to one of the most consequential real-world problems in democratic governance. Students who have never cared about perimeter calculations will care when the perimeter of a district determines who represents them.

### Navigating Political Sensitivity

This investigation is explicitly designed to be **non-partisan.** The mathematical techniques of gerrymandering are used by parties across the political spectrum and in countries worldwide (USA, UK, France, India, Malaysia). Use fictional parties (Blue/Red) for the grid activities. When analysing real districts, include examples from multiple parties and countries. The investigation question is "Can mathematics detect unfairness?" — not "Who is cheating?"

If students ask about specific political situations, redirect to the mathematics: "That's an interesting question — let's see what the efficiency gap tells us about that map."

### Cross-Disciplinary Connections

**Social Studies / Individuals & Societies:** Electoral systems, representation, democracy, minority rights. This investigation could be co-taught with a social studies colleague.

**Science (Criterion D):** The methodology of quantitative analysis applied to social problems — how do you operationalise a concept like "fairness" into a measurable quantity?

### International Context

Gerrymandering is not just a USA problem. Discuss:
- **UK:** Constituency boundaries are drawn by independent commissions, but the criteria they use still involve trade-offs
- **Australia:** Independent Electoral Commission draws boundaries using mathematical population targets
- **France:** Historical "découpage" controversies
- **Malaysia / Singapore:** Malapportionment and boundary manipulation in Southeast Asian democracies (relevant to your students' regional context)

### Adaptation for EAL Learners

- Provide the 5×5 grid pre-printed on card for physical manipulation (students can cut and rearrange)
- Create a visual vocabulary card: packing = squeezing together (diagram), cracking = spreading thin (diagram)
- Use colour-coded district overlays on transparent sheets rather than requiring students to draw boundaries
- For Part D, allow students to present their evaluation as an annotated poster with labelled diagrams rather than continuous prose

### Equipment Needed

- Printed 5×5 grids (multiple copies per student — they'll need several attempts)
- Coloured pencils or markers
- Calculator
- Grid paper for Polsby-Popper practice
- Printed real district maps (teacher-provided)
- Optional: scissors and coloured card for physical district manipulation
