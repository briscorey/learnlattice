## The Hook

Two students stand 50 metres apart on your school campus.

One stands on the field. One stands on the car park.

They hold up their thermometers at the same time.

**The readings differ by 8°C.**

Same moment. Same school. Same air.

*So why?*

---

## Background: What Is the Urban Heat Island Effect?

Cities are hotter than the countryside around them. Not by a little — by **2 to 10°C** on a typical summer day. At night, the difference can be even larger.

This happens because:

| Factor | Natural surface | Urban surface |
|--------|----------------|---------------|
| **Albedo** (reflectivity) | Grass reflects ~25% of sunlight | Asphalt reflects only ~5% |
| **Thermal mass** | Soil releases heat quickly | Concrete stores heat for hours |
| **Evapotranspiration** | Plants release water, cooling the air | No evaporation from concrete |
| **Geometry** | Open fields allow air flow | Buildings trap heat between them |

Your school is a miniature city. It has all of these surfaces. Today, you're going to map its heat islands.

---

## Part A: Prediction and Planning (Criterion B)

### Before You Go Outside

Here are 8 locations around the school:

1. Centre of the car park (asphalt, no shade)
2. Grass field (open, no trees nearby)
3. Under a large tree on grass
4. Concrete path between buildings
5. Next to the south-facing wall of a building
6. Shaded courtyard between buildings
7. Artificial turf / sports surface
8. Near the school entrance (mixed surface)

**Rank these from hottest (1) to coolest (8).** Write your prediction and your reasoning.

### Design Your Method

Before collecting data, your group must agree on:

- **What will you measure?** (Air temperature? Surface temperature? Both?)
- **How will you control variables?** (Height of thermometer? Time between readings? Shading the thermometer from direct sun?)
- **How many readings per location?** (Why is one reading not enough?)
- **How will you record data?** (Design your data table before going outside)

*Your teacher will not tell you the answers to these questions. Discuss in your group and justify your choices.*

---

## Part B: Fieldwork — Collect Your Data (Criterion B + C)

### Data Collection Protocol

At each location, record:

| Location | Surface type | Shade? | Reading 1 (°C) | Reading 2 (°C) | Reading 3 (°C) | Mean (°C) |
|----------|-------------|--------|----------------|----------------|----------------|-----------|
| | | | | | | |

**Important:**
- Wait 60 seconds between readings at each location (thermometer needs time to equilibrate)
- Hold thermometer at consistent height (~1 metre)
- Shield thermometer bulb from direct sunlight (you're measuring air temperature, not sun intensity)
- Record the time you visit each location

### Pool Your Class Data

When you return, your teacher will collect means from every group. If 6 groups each measured 4 locations, you now have **24 data points** — far more reliable than any single group's data.

Calculate the **class mean** for each location.

---

## Part C: Analysis and Representation (Criterion C)

### Create a Temperature Map

Using a sketch map of your school campus, colour-code each measurement location:

- **Red/orange:** Hottest locations (above class mean)
- **Blue/green:** Coolest locations (below class mean)
- **Yellow:** Near the mean

Add temperature values and a colour key.

### Statistical Summary

For the full class dataset, calculate:
- **Mean** temperature across all locations
- **Range** (highest — lowest)
- **Temperature difference** between the hottest surface type (asphalt) and coolest (shaded grass)

Create a **bar chart** comparing mean temperatures by surface type.

### Compare to Predictions

Look at your ranking from Part A.

- How many locations did you rank correctly?
- Which prediction was most wrong? Why do you think your intuition failed there?
- What does this tell you about the value of measurement vs. assumption?

---

## Part D: The Equity Question (Criterion D)

### From School to City

Your school's heat map is a miniature version of a real city.

Research question: **Do all neighbourhoods in a city experience the same temperature?**

Consider these facts:
- Wealthier neighbourhoods typically have **2-3x more tree canopy** than low-income areas
- In many cities, formerly "redlined" neighbourhoods (historically denied investment) are now the hottest areas — by up to **7°C**
- Heat-related illness disproportionately affects elderly people, outdoor workers, and those without air conditioning

### Your Reflection

Write a response to:

> **"Based on your investigation, what recommendations would you make to your school (or city) about managing urban heat — and who benefits most from these changes?"**

Your response must:
- Reference specific data from your investigation
- Explain the science behind your recommendations (albedo, evapotranspiration, thermal mass)
- Consider who is most affected by heat and why
- Acknowledge at least one limitation of your investigation
- Be supported by evidence, not just opinion

---

## Extension: The Redesign Challenge

Choose one area of your school campus that your data identified as a "heat island."

Design a modification to reduce its temperature by at least 3°C. Your proposal must include:
- A labelled sketch showing the current and proposed design
- The scientific reasoning for why your changes would reduce temperature
- A rough cost estimate (research the cost of trees, shade structures, reflective paint)
- An explanation of who would benefit and how

## Teaching Notes

### Why This Investigation Is Special

This is the rare investigation where students generate their own primary data through genuine fieldwork, then connect local findings to global patterns. The school campus becomes a living laboratory. Students who discover that the car park is 10°C hotter than the garden don't need to be told about climate — they've measured it with their own hands.

### The Nanjing Context

For schools in Nanjing (or any city with hot summers), this investigation has immediate personal relevance. Nanjing's summer temperatures regularly exceed 38°C, and the urban heat island effect can push city centre temperatures 5-8°C above surrounding rural areas. Students can connect their school data to the city they live in.

### Cross-Disciplinary Connections

**Mathematics (Criterion C):** Students calculate means, ranges, and create multiple representations. The statistical analysis is authentic — they need statistics to make sense of noisy real-world data, not because the teacher told them to calculate a mean.

**Mathematics (Criterion D):** The equity analysis uses real demographic and temperature data to explore proportional reasoning and correlation.

### Equipment Needed

- Simple thermometers (one per group — digital or alcohol, NOT infrared for air temperature)
- Clipboards and data recording sheets
- Sketch map of school campus (prepare in advance or have students draw their own)
- Timer/stopwatch
- Optional: infrared thermometer for comparing surface temperatures alongside air temperatures

### Common Fieldwork Issues

- **Thermometers in direct sun:** Students will forget to shade the bulb. Demonstrate why this matters — put one in sun and one in shade and show the 10°C+ difference
- **Not waiting for equilibration:** If students rush between locations, readings will reflect the previous location. Enforce the 60-second wait
- **Wind effects:** Windy conditions reduce temperature differences between locations. Best results come from calm, sunny days

### Adaptation for EAL Learners

- Provide a pre-labelled school map with location names
- Create a visual fieldwork protocol card with numbered steps and diagrams
- Allow students to annotate their temperature map instead of writing continuous prose
- Pre-teach: albedo, evapotranspiration, thermal mass, urban heat island, mean, range
