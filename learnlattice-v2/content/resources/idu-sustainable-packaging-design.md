## Overview: Why This Works as an IDU

This investigation connects MYP Mathematics (geometry, measurement, optimisation) with MYP Sciences (materials science, environmental chemistry) through product packaging design. The interdisciplinary need is real: designing effective packaging requires mathematical optimisation of surface area and volume AND scientific understanding of material properties and environmental impact.

**Mathematics contributes:** Surface area and volume formulas for prisms, cylinders, and spheres; SA:V ratio calculations; optimisation (finding dimensions that maximise volume for fixed surface area); graphing and comparison.

**Science contributes:** Material properties (strength, flexibility, biodegradability); structural engineering principles; environmental impact of different materials (plastic, cardboard, glass, aluminium); lifecycle analysis.

## Unit Structure (4-5 Lessons)

### Lesson 1: Measuring Real Packaging

Students bring in or are given 5-6 commercial product packages of different shapes:
- Rectangular box (cereal, tissue box)
- Cylinder (Pringles, drink can)
- Triangular prism (Toblerone)
- Irregular shape (chip packet, bottle)

For each package, students measure dimensions, calculate surface area and volume, and compute the **SA:V ratio**. the amount of material needed per unit of product stored.

**Key discovery:** The SA:V ratio varies dramatically. A sphere has the lowest possible ratio (most efficient) but no commercial product uses spherical packaging. Why not?

### Lesson 2: The Optimisation Challenge (Mathematics Focus)

**Challenge:** You have exactly 600 cm² of cardboard. Design a container with the maximum possible volume.

Students investigate:
1. For a **rectangular box** with SA = 600 cm², what dimensions give maximum volume? (Answer: a cube with side ≈ 10 cm, volume ≈ 1000 cm³)
2. For a **cylinder** with SA = 600 cm², what radius and height give maximum volume? (Answer: r ≈ 5.6 cm, h ≈ 11.3 cm, volume ≈ 1119 cm³)
3. For a **sphere** with SA = 600 cm², what is the volume? (Answer: r ≈ 6.9 cm, volume ≈ 1382 cm³)

**MYP Maths Criterion B:** Students investigate the pattern of how shape affects efficiency, describe the SA:V relationship, and verify their findings.

**MYP Maths Criterion D:** The real-world constraints (stackability, shelf display, manufacturing cost) mean the mathematically optimal solution is not always the best solution.

### Lesson 3: Materials Science (Science Focus)

Students test the structural properties of different packaging materials and shapes:
1. **Crush test:** Which shape supports the most weight? (Cylinders typically outperform boxes of the same material)
2. **Drop test:** Which material best protects contents?
3. **Water resistance:** How long does each material last when wet?

**MYP Science Criterion B:** Students design fair tests for material properties, controlling variables and collecting reliable data.

### Lesson 4: Environmental Impact and Design Decision

Students research and evaluate the environmental impact of different packaging materials:
- Cardboard: renewable, recyclable, but uses water and energy to produce
- Plastic: light, strong, cheap, but persistent in environment
- Glass: infinitely recyclable but heavy (transport emissions) and energy-intensive to produce
- Aluminium: recyclable with low energy cost, but high initial energy cost

**MYP Science Criterion D:** Students evaluate trade-offs between mathematical efficiency, structural performance, and environmental sustainability.

### Lesson 5: Design and Present

Each group designs a final package for a specific product, justifying their choices:
- Shape (mathematical optimisation)
- Material (scientific properties and environmental impact)
- Dimensions (calculated for maximum efficiency)
- Trade-offs acknowledged (why the design is not mathematically perfect but is practically optimal)

## Assessment Pairing

**Mathematics:** Criterion B (SA:V optimisation investigation) + Criterion C (mathematical communication of calculations and graphs) + Criterion D (real-world application and evaluation of constraints)

**Science:** Criterion B (materials testing experimental design) + Criterion D (environmental impact evaluation)

## EAL Scaffolds

- Physical manipulation of real packages provides a tactile, language-light entry point
- Provide SA and volume formula reference sheets with visual diagrams
- The design challenge (Lesson 5) can incorporate visual presentation. drawings, models, and labelled diagrams reduce the writing burden
- Bilingual glossary: surface area, volume, ratio, efficiency, biodegradable, renewable, recyclable
