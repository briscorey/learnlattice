## Overview

Students discover that not all growth patterns are linear. Through systematic investigation of square numbers, triangular numbers, and other quadratic sequences, they encounter second differences and develop intuition for n² relationships.

## The Investigation

### Stimulus
Build a sequence of growing squares: 1×1, 2×2, 3×3, 4×4...
Record the total number of unit squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25...

### Phase 1 — First Differences Don't Work
- Calculate first differences: 3, 5, 7, 9...
- "The first differences aren't constant — so this isn't linear!"
- Calculate second differences: 2, 2, 2...
- "The second differences ARE constant. What does that mean?"

### Phase 2 — More Quadratic Patterns
Investigate at least two more:
- Triangular numbers: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15...
- Handshake problem: n people, how many handshakes?
- Rectangular numbers: 2, 6, 12, 20...

For each: find first differences, second differences, and attempt a general rule.

### Phase 3 — Connecting to n²
- Square numbers → n²
- Rectangular numbers → n(n+1) = n² + n
- Triangular numbers → n(n+1)/2
- "Every quadratic pattern has a constant second difference"

## Criterion B Alignment

| Strand | Evidence |

| Select a method | Use of systematic tabulation and difference methods |

## EAL Scaffolds
- **Sentence frame**: "The first differences are ___, so this is not linear. The second differences are ___, which means..."
- **Visual**: Side-by-side comparison tables with colour-coded difference rows

## Teacher Notes
The key insight is that **constant second differences = quadratic**. Students do NOT need to derive the quadratic formula. The goal is recognition and description, not symbolic manipulation.
