## The Hook

Place 9-12 different rock samples on each group's table. No labels. No names. No information.

Ask: **"Sort these into groups. Any groups you want. You decide the categories."**

Give them 5 minutes. Then ask each group to explain their system.

Some will sort by colour. Some by texture. Some by weight. Some by whether they can scratch glass. Each system reveals different properties. **None are wrong.** But one system — based on how the rocks formed — turns out to be more useful than the others. That's what they'll discover.

---

## Core Concept: Three Rock Types

- **Igneous:** Formed from cooled magma/lava. Often crystalline or glassy. Examples: granite, basalt, obsidian.
- **Sedimentary:** Formed from compressed layers of sediment. Often layered, may contain fossils. Examples: sandstone, limestone, shale.
- **Metamorphic:** Formed when existing rocks are changed by heat and pressure. Often banded or foliated. Examples: marble, slate, quartzite.

The classification system exists because the *process of formation* determines the *properties* of the rock.

## Tiered Tasks

### Part A — Criterion A (Levels 1-4)
1. Describe each of your rock samples using at least 3 observable properties (colour, texture, hardness, layering, crystal size, weight).
2. Sort your rocks into the three categories: igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic. Explain which properties helped you classify each one.

### Part B — Criterion B (Levels 3-6)
1. Create a dichotomous key that someone could use to classify any rock into one of the three types using only observable properties (no prior knowledge needed).
2. Test your key: give it to another group with a rock they haven't seen. Does it work? Refine it.

### Part C — Criterion B (Levels 5-8)
1. The rock cycle shows how rocks transform from one type to another through geological processes. Draw a complete rock cycle diagram showing all possible pathways.
2. A student finds a rock with visible crystals AND layers. They say it must be two types at once. Explain why this observation is possible and what it reveals about the rock's history.

## Teaching Notes

Rock kits can be purchased cheaply or assembled from garden centres and geological supply websites. If you don't have rocks, high-resolution photographs work — but physical rocks are dramatically more engaging.

The key pedagogical move is letting students create their OWN classification system before introducing the scientific one. When they discover that "how it formed" is a more useful organising principle than "what colour it is," they understand why scientists classify the way they do. This is genuine inquiry — not discovery learning theatre.
