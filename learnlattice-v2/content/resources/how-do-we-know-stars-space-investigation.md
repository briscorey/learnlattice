## The Hook

In 1835, the French philosopher Auguste Comte — one of the founders of modern philosophy of science — wrote this:

> *"The positive knowledge of stars will always remain inaccessible to us... their chemical composition is forever beyond our reach."*

He was wrong within 25 years. By 1859, Gustav Kirchhoff had shown that the dark lines in sunlight were the chemical fingerprints of elements — and that the Sun contained iron, sodium, calcium, and dozens of other elements, all identifiable from 150 million kilometres away.

Ask: **"How is that possible? How do you identify what something is made of without touching it?"**

Give students 90 seconds to speculate. Then: **"The answer is that light from a hot object carries the chemical information of everything it passed through. The Sun's light is not just brightness — it is a message. We just needed to learn how to read it."**

This investigation is about learning to read light.

---

## Why This Investigation Matters

Every piece of knowledge humanity has about the universe beyond our solar system comes from analysing electromagnetic radiation — light in its broadest sense. The techniques of spectroscopy developed to understand stars are the same techniques used in:

- Medical diagnostics (MRI, PET scans, spectroscopic blood tests)
- Environmental monitoring (satellite detection of atmospheric gases)
- Forensic science (identifying trace substances)
- Pharmaceutical quality control

The context is the universe. But the reasoning is transferable everywhere.

## Core Concepts

### Light as a Wave

Light is electromagnetic radiation — a self-propagating wave of oscillating electric and magnetic fields. The full electromagnetic spectrum from shortest to longest wavelength:

$$\gamma\text{-rays} \rightarrow \text{X-rays} \rightarrow \text{UV} \rightarrow \text{Visible} \rightarrow \text{IR} \rightarrow \text{Microwaves} \rightarrow \text{Radio}$$

**Visible light** occupies a narrow band: wavelengths from ~380 nm (violet) to ~700 nm (red).

$$\text{ROYGBIV: red (700nm) → orange → yellow → green → blue → indigo → violet (380nm)}$$

Key relationship: **shorter wavelength = higher energy = higher frequency**

### Temperature and Colour: Wien's Law

Every object above absolute zero (−273°C) emits electromagnetic radiation. The *peak wavelength* of that radiation depends on temperature:

$$\lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{b}{T}$$

where $b = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}$ m·K (Wien's displacement constant) and $T$ is temperature in Kelvin.

**What this means for stars:**

| Star colour | Peak wavelength | Surface temperature | Example |
|------------|----------------|-------------------|---------|
| Blue-white | ~300 nm (UV) | ~30,000 K | Rigel |
| White | ~400 nm | ~10,000 K | Sirius |
| Yellow | ~550 nm | ~6,000 K | Sun |
| Orange | ~660 nm | ~4,500 K | Arcturus |
| Red | ~750 nm | ~3,500 K | Betelgeuse |

> **Worked example:** The Sun has a surface temperature of ~5,778 K. What is its peak emission wavelength?
> $$\lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{5778} = 5.02 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m} = 502 \text{ nm}$$
> 502 nm is green — which is why solar irradiance peaks in the green part of the spectrum, and why plants (which evolved under sunlight) absorb red and blue, reflecting green.

### Spectroscopy: Reading Chemical Fingerprints

When light from a hot source passes through a cool gas, specific wavelengths are absorbed. Each element absorbs a unique set of wavelengths — its spectral "fingerprint."

**Emission spectrum:** A hot gas emits light only at specific wavelengths — bright lines on a dark background. These are the characteristic emission lines of that element's electrons jumping between energy levels.

**Absorption spectrum:** When white light passes through a cool gas, the same wavelengths are absorbed, producing dark lines (Fraunhofer lines) on a continuous spectrum.

**The key insight:** The dark lines in sunlight are absorption lines. Comparing them to laboratory spectra of known elements allows identification of every element in the Sun's atmosphere — from 150 million kilometres away.

### Doppler Shift: Motion Written in Light

If a light source moves toward or away from an observer, the wavelength appears compressed (blue-shifted) or stretched (red-shifted).

**Cosmological implication:** In 1929, Edwin Hubble discovered that almost all distant galaxies show red-shifted spectra — they are moving away from us. The farther they are, the faster they recede. This is the evidence that the universe is expanding.

**Exoplanet detection:** When a planet orbits a star, the star "wobbles" slightly. This wobble produces a rhythmic Doppler shift in the star's spectrum. By measuring this shift, astronomers can detect planets they have never directly seen — inferring mass, orbital period, and minimum distance.

## Investigation Tasks

### Part A — The Colour-Temperature Connection

Using Wien's Law ($\lambda_{\text{max}} = b/T$ where $b = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}$ m·K):

1. Calculate the peak emission wavelength for each star in the table below. Identify the colour and whether it falls in the UV, visible, or IR range.

| Star | Temperature (K) | $\lambda_{\text{max}}$ (m) | $\lambda_{\text{max}}$ (nm) | Colour/Region |
|------|----------------|--------------------------|---------------------------|--------------|
| Betelgeuse | 3,500 | | | |
| Sun | 5,778 | | | |
| Sirius | 10,000 | | | |
| Rigel | 12,000 | | | |
| Eta Carinae | 35,000 | | | |

2. Betelgeuse's peak emission is in the infrared. Why can we still see it as a red star with the naked eye?

3. Our eyes are most sensitive to green light (~555 nm). Calculate the surface temperature a star would need to have for its peak emission to be exactly 555 nm. What does this tell you about why human vision evolved at this wavelength?

### Part B — The Spectral Detective

You are given four spectra (provided on the insert sheet): two unknown stellar spectra (Star X and Star Y) and the laboratory emission spectra of hydrogen, calcium, sodium, iron, and helium.

For each unknown star:
1. Match the absorption lines in the stellar spectrum to the laboratory emission spectra.
2. List which elements are present in each star's atmosphere.
3. Star X has strong hydrogen lines and weak calcium lines. Star Y has weak hydrogen lines and strong calcium and iron lines. What does this suggest about the relative temperatures of the two stars? (Hint: hydrogen absorption lines are strongest at intermediate temperatures ~10,000 K; calcium lines strengthen at cooler temperatures.)
4. One of these stars is similar to the Sun. Which one? Justify your identification.

### Part C — The Exoplanet Wobble

The radial velocity method for detecting exoplanets works as follows: a planet's gravitational pull causes its host star to orbit the common centre of mass. This produces a measurable Doppler shift in the star's spectrum — the spectrum shifts blue when the star moves toward us, red when it moves away.

**Dataset:** You are given a table of radial velocity measurements for a star over 4 years (values in m/s, positive = moving away, negative = moving toward):

| Day | Velocity (m/s) | Day | Velocity (m/s) |
|-----|---------------|-----|---------------|
| 0 | +12 | 400 | +8 |
| 50 | -18 | 450 | -20 |
| 100 | +15 | 500 | +14 |
| 150 | -19 | 550 | -17 |
| 200 | +11 | 600 | +12 |
| 250 | -20 | 650 | -19 |
| 300 | +13 | 700 | +13 |
| 350 | -18 | 750 | -21 |

1. Plot this data. Describe the pattern.
2. What is the period of the signal (time between identical velocity values)? What does this represent astronomically?
3. The amplitude of the wobble is ~19 m/s. Jupiter causes the Sun to wobble at ~13 m/s. What does this suggest about the mass of the unseen planet relative to Jupiter?
4. This planet orbits its star every ~50 days. Using Kepler's Third Law ($T^2 \propto r^3$), compare this to Earth's 365-day orbit. Is this planet closer to or farther from its star than Earth is from the Sun?
5. Given its orbital distance and assuming a star similar to the Sun: would liquid water be possible on the surface? Explain.

### Part D — The Comte Problem (Criterion D)

Return to the Auguste Comte quote from the hook:

> *"The positive knowledge of stars will always remain inaccessible to us... their chemical composition is forever beyond our reach."*

1. What was the specific technical limitation Comte was responding to? Was his reasoning sound given what was known in 1835?
2. The discovery that proved Comte wrong — spectroscopy — came from laboratory physics, not astronomy. What does this suggest about the relationship between different scientific disciplines?
3. We now know the chemical composition, temperature, age, distance, mass, luminosity, and rotational speed of thousands of stars — all without ever leaving Earth. Choose one of these properties and explain in detail how it is determined from light analysis.
4. Reflect: what might a 2024 version of Comte's statement look like — what do we currently believe is "permanently beyond our reach" in understanding the universe? Is there a good reason to be sceptical of such claims?

## Formative Assessment Checkpoints

**Checkpoint 1 — After Wien's Law introduction:**
"A star appears distinctly blue-white. Without calculating, what can you confidently say about its surface temperature relative to our Sun?" — Students who understand Wien's Law will say: "It's hotter than the Sun." Students still treating colour as aesthetic will be uncertain.

**Checkpoint 2 — During Part A:**
"You calculated that the Sun's peak emission is at ~502 nm (green). But we describe the Sun as yellow-white. Why don't we see the Sun as green?" — This tests whether students understand the difference between peak emission and integrated perception. The Sun emits across the entire visible spectrum; our eyes integrate all of it and perceive yellow-white.

**Checkpoint 3 — Before Part C:**
"Astronomers discovered the first exoplanet using the Doppler method in 1995. Nobody has ever seen this planet directly. Is this sufficient evidence that it exists? What would 'sufficient evidence' mean in this context?" — This is the core epistemological question of the investigation.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often believe | What's accurate |
|-----------------------------|----------------|
| "We can see stars because they're bright" | We see stars because they emit electromagnetic radiation in visible wavelengths. Stars also emit far more in wavelengths we cannot see — UV, X-ray, infrared — and some are best studied outside the visible spectrum |
| "Redshift means the light is actually red" | Redshift means the light has been stretched to longer (redder) wavelengths — a cold blue star moving away from us is redshifted, but it is still blue relative to its emitted spectrum |
| "Telescopes make things look bigger" | Larger telescopes primarily collect more light (enabling detection of fainter objects) — the resolution increase is secondary |
| "We can see what stars look like right now" | Light takes time to travel. When we observe a star 100 light-years away, we see it as it was 100 years ago. The Andromeda Galaxy appears as it was 2.5 million years ago. |
| "Scientists are making assumptions about star composition — they might be wrong" | Spectroscopic identification of elements is done by matching precise wavelengths to laboratory measurements. Hydrogen in a star absorbs at exactly the same wavelengths as hydrogen in a laboratory tube on Earth. This is reproducible, universal physics. |

## Teaching Notes

**On the Comte hook:** The quote is a gift because Comte was genuinely brilliant, genuinely wrong, and wrong in a way that reveals something important: in science, "this is permanently beyond our reach" is almost never the right conclusion. It usually means "this is currently beyond our reach with existing techniques." The follow-up question — "what might a 2024 version of Comte's statement look like?" — invites students to think about the frontier of current knowledge, which is a genuinely exciting place to live intellectually.

**On spectroscopy:** If your school has a spectrometer or diffraction grating, do the lab version of this first — pass white light through a prism or grating, observe the continuous spectrum, then show a sodium lamp (sharp yellow doublet), a hydrogen lamp (series of lines), and a neon lamp. The leap from "this pattern of lines is unique to each element" to "we can identify elements in stars from their absorption lines" becomes physically intuitive rather than abstract.

**On Part C:** The wobble dataset is designed to show a clear ~50-day period with an amplitude of ~19 m/s. This is broadly similar to the parameters of a hot Jupiter (a massive planet in a close orbit) — the first class of exoplanet detected by this method. The combination of Part C with the Kepler's Third Law calculation extends into mathematics without losing the scientific context.

**On Part D:** Some students will want to claim that modern science has eliminated fundamental unknowns. Push back: we don't know what dark matter is (makes up ~27% of the universe's mass-energy). We can't directly observe the first stars or the Big Bang singularity. The interior of black holes is causally disconnected from external observation. There are deep, interesting reasons why some things may genuinely remain unreachable — and exploring those limits is as intellectually rich as the discoveries themselves.

> **The bigger idea:** Every fact we know about the universe beyond our solar system arrived as light. The Hubble Space Telescope, the James Webb Space Telescope, every radio telescope and X-ray observatory — they are all, at their core, sophisticated light-reading machines. The techniques of spectroscopy, photometry, and Doppler analysis that revealed the composition of stars are the same techniques that are discovering new exoplanets weekly and mapping the large-scale structure of the universe. To understand how we read starlight is to understand how humans have extended their senses across 13.8 billion years of cosmic history — and why that achievement is among the most remarkable things our species has ever done.
