## The Hook

Bring a candle and a digital balance to the front. Weigh the candle. Record the mass. Light it. Let it burn for exactly 5 minutes. Blow it out. Weigh it again immediately.

Ask: **"Where did the missing mass go?"**

Students will say: "It burned up." "It disappeared." "It turned into smoke." "Into air." Press on each answer: "Burned up — but into what? Disappeared — where? Into air — what does that mean, precisely?"

Then ask the harder question: **"A rusting nail gets heavier as it rusts. A burning candle gets lighter as it burns. Both are reacting with oxygen. Why does one gain mass and the other lose it?"**

This is not a trick question. The answers are both correct under conservation of mass — but they require understanding where the matter goes in each case. The rusting nail's reaction products (iron oxide) stay on the nail. The candle's reaction products (CO₂ and H₂O) escape as gases. The candle doesn't lose mass — the *system* loses mass because gases leave.

## Why This Investigation Matters

Conservation of mass is the cornerstone of all quantitative chemistry. Without it, stoichiometry is impossible, industrial chemistry is uncontrollable, and environmental accounting of carbon emissions is meaningless.

But more fundamentally, it requires students to make the particle model *real* — to actually believe that what looks like disappearance (a burning candle, dissolving sugar, evaporating water) is actually matter changing form, not matter ceasing to exist. This conceptual shift — from "matter vanishes" to "matter rearranges" — is the central move of chemistry at every level from Grade 6 to university.

## Core Concepts

### The Particle Model of Matter

All matter is made of particles (atoms or molecules). These particles:
- Are in constant motion
- Have spaces between them
- Interact with each other through forces
- Cannot be created or destroyed in ordinary chemical reactions

**Three states as particle arrangements:**

| State | Particle arrangement | Energy | Shape | Volume |
|-------|---------------------|--------|-------|--------|
| Solid | Closely packed, regular lattice | Low | Fixed | Fixed |
| Liquid | Closely packed, irregular | Medium | Flows | Fixed |
| Gas | Widely spaced, disordered | High | Fills container | Fills container |

### Conservation of Mass

**Antoine Lavoisier's Law (1774):** In any chemical reaction, the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.

$$m_{\text{reactants}} = m_{\text{products}}$$

This is *always* true in closed systems. When mass appears to be lost (burning candle) or gained (rusting nail), it is because the system is not closed — gases enter or leave.

**The candle reaction (simplified):**

Candle wax is approximately \\(C_{25}H_{52}\\) (a long-chain hydrocarbon). Combustion in oxygen:

$$C_{25}H_{52} + 38O_2 \rightarrow 25CO_2 + 26H_2O$$

The carbon and hydrogen atoms in the wax recombine with oxygen atoms from the air to form carbon dioxide (gas) and water vapour (gas). These gases rise into the air and dissipate. The candle loses mass because the products *leave the system as gases*. But if you could capture all the CO₂ and H₂O produced, you would find that their total mass equals the mass of wax lost plus the mass of oxygen consumed.

### Step-by-step: Balancing the Mass

**Worked example — Burning methane (CH₄):**

$$CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$$

Count atoms on each side:
| Atom | Reactants | Products |
|------|-----------|---------|
| C | 1 (in CH₄) | 1 (in CO₂) ✓ |
| H | 4 (in CH₄) | 4 (in 2H₂O) ✓ |
| O | 4 (in 2O₂) | 4 (2 in CO₂ + 2 in 2H₂O) ✓ |

The equation is balanced. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed.

**Calculating mass:**
If 16 g of CH₄ burns (one mole), it reacts with 64 g of O₂ to produce 44 g of CO₂ and 36 g of H₂O.
- Reactants: 16 + 64 = 80 g
- Products: 44 + 36 = 80 g ✓

> **Formative checkpoint:** "If 16 g of candle wax burns and the balance shows the candle lost 12 g — did mass disappear? What question would you need to answer to account for the missing 4 g?" Students should reach for: "some products were already on the candle (water condensation, soot), or some oxygen was consumed from the air."

### Chemical vs Physical Change

| Type | What changes | What stays the same | Reversible? |
|------|-------------|-------------------|------------|
| Physical change | Shape, state, arrangement | Chemical identity, mass | Usually yes |
| Chemical change | Chemical bonds, identity of substances | Total mass (atoms) | Usually no |

**Indicators of chemical change:**
- Gas produced (bubbling without heating)
- Colour change
- Temperature change (exothermic or endothermic)
- Light produced
- Precipitate formed (solid from two liquids)
- Irreversibility

## Investigation Tasks

### Part A — The Closed System Experiment

**Setup:** A candle sits on a balance inside a glass jar with a tight-fitting lid. Record the total mass (candle + jar + air inside). Light the candle via a small port, then seal immediately. Record mass every 2 minutes until the candle goes out on its own.

**Data table:**

| Time (min) | Mass (g) | Change from start |
|-----------|---------|------------------|
| 0 | | — |
| 2 | | |
| 4 | | |
| 6 | | |
| 8 (out) | | |

**Questions:**
1. Plot mass vs time. Describe the pattern.
2. Why does the candle go out in the sealed jar? (What has been used up?)
3. Did the total mass of the system change? Why or why not?
4. Compare: if you repeated the experiment with the jar open to air, how would the balance reading change? Explain using the particle model.

### Part B — The Rusting Nail Comparison

You have two nails: one bare iron, one painted. You place both on a balance in a sealed plastic bag with a small pad of steel wool (finely divided iron — rusts faster than nails).

**Prediction task:** Predict the mass reading after 7 days for:
- The sealed bag containing the steel wool (rusting occurs inside)
- An open dish containing the same amount of steel wool (rusting occurs in open air)

Write your predictions and justifications before collecting data.

**After 7 days of data collection:**
1. Did either system gain or lose mass? Explain using the particle model.
2. The sealed bag showed [data to be recorded]. The open dish showed [data to be recorded]. Were your predictions correct?
3. Why does the rusting nail behave differently from the burning candle, if both are reacting with oxygen?

### Part C — The Balancing Challenge

Balance each equation and calculate the mass of the missing product:

**Equation 1:** Magnesium burns in oxygen:
$$\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{MgO (unbalanced)}$$
If 24 g of magnesium reacts with 16 g of oxygen, what mass of magnesium oxide is produced?

**Equation 2:** Iron reacts with oxygen to form iron oxide (rust):
$$\text{Fe} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \text{ (unbalanced)}$$
If a 56 g iron nail rusts completely, how much mass does it gain?

**Equation 3:** Hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water:
$$\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O (unbalanced)}$$
This reaction powers hydrogen fuel cells in some vehicles. If a fuel cell uses 2 g of hydrogen, how much oxygen does it consume? What mass of water is produced?

**Reflection:** In Equation 1, you made a solid that weighed more than the starting magnesium. Where did the extra mass come from?

### Part D — The Mass Balance Investigation (Criterion B)

Design and conduct an investigation to determine whether mass is conserved when a soluble solid (e.g. a tablet of effervescent vitamin C) dissolves in water.

Your investigation must include:
- A testable hypothesis
- A method that accounts for the gas produced by the reaction
- Identification of variables (independent, dependent, controlled)
- A prediction of what you would observe if mass is conserved vs if it is not conserved
- A description of at least one source of error and how you would minimise it

**After conducting the investigation:**
1. Was your hypothesis supported?
2. If your balance showed a mass loss, was mass actually not conserved? Explain.
3. Redesign the experiment to give a more reliable result.

### Part E — The Criterion D Task

> *In 2021, global CO₂ emissions from fossil fuel combustion reached approximately 36.3 billion tonnes.*

The main components of fossil fuels are hydrocarbons (compounds of carbon and hydrogen). When they combust: \\(\text{fuel} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\\).

1. Every carbon atom in a fossil fuel molecule produces one CO₂ molecule when burned. Carbon has an atomic mass of 12; CO₂ has a molecular mass of 44. What mass of CO₂ is produced per gram of carbon burned?

2. If the atmosphere currently contains approximately \\(8.2 \times 10^{17}\\) kg of CO₂ (a figure that increases by about 2 ppm per year), express the annual 36.3 billion tonnes of emissions as a percentage of atmospheric CO₂.

3. A news article claims: "Burning coal releases stored carbon back into the atmosphere — it was there before, we're just returning it." Is this statement chemically accurate? Is it an accurate argument against concern about emissions? Explain the distinction.

4. Conservation of mass means that every carbon atom burned as fossil fuel *must* end up somewhere. List the possible "sinks" where carbon atoms from combustion could end up. Which sinks are temporary and which are effectively permanent on human timescales?

## Formative Assessment Checkpoints

**Checkpoint 1 — After the hook:**
"The candle lost 0.8 g in 5 minutes. How much total mass (candle + air) was lost from the open system?" — Most students will say 0.8 g. The correct answer requires them to account for oxygen consumed: total mass loss from the open system depends on where the products go. In a closed system, no mass is lost.

**Checkpoint 2 — During Part C:**
"You said burning magnesium gains mass. But combustion is when something burns — doesn't burning mean losing mass?" — This directly confronts the candle-vs-rusting-nail confusion. The difference is whether the products are gases (which escape) or solids (which stay).

**Checkpoint 3 — After Part D:**
"What is the difference between saying 'mass is approximately conserved' and 'mass is conserved'? Does your experimental error mean the law is only approximately true?" — Students should distinguish between experimental error and the theoretical law. The law is absolute; their measurement has uncertainty.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often believe | What's accurate |
|-----------------------------|----------------|
| "Burning destroys matter" | Burning converts matter from solid/liquid form to gases — the atoms persist, they just change molecular arrangement and leave the local system |
| "A rusting nail gains mass, so matter was created" | Iron atoms in the nail combine with oxygen atoms from the air. The total mass of iron + oxygen is conserved. The system appears to gain mass because oxygen atoms from outside the system join the nail |
| "Dissolving means a substance disappears" | Dissolved particles remain in the solution — they are distributed between solvent molecules but not destroyed. Evaporating the solvent recovers the original solute |
| "Balancing a chemical equation means adding more atoms to make the numbers work" | Balancing adjusts the *coefficients* (how many molecules), not the subscripts (which would change the substance). You cannot add or remove atoms — you can only adjust how many molecules of each substance react |
| "Conservation of mass is only an approximation" | In nuclear reactions, a tiny mass is converted to energy (E=mc²), so mass is technically not conserved. In all ordinary chemical reactions, mass is conserved exactly. This is a meaningful distinction. |

## Teaching Notes

**On the hook:** The rusting-vs-burning comparison is the most productive cognitive conflict in this investigation. Students who say "combustion means losing mass" are not wrong — the system loses mass because gaseous products escape. But students need to articulate *why* the nail does the opposite. The answer — solid products vs gaseous products — is the particle model applied to reaction products.

**On Part A:** The closed system experiment is the key experiment. In a sealed jar, total mass should not change (the candle goes out, but all CO₂ and H₂O remain inside). In practice, the balance may drift slightly due to temperature effects on the jar — this is a productive source of error discussion.

**On Part D:** The effervescent tablet investigation is designed specifically because students almost always see "mass loss" when they don't seal the system — and this is real, measurable evidence that gas escapes. The redesign challenge is where the real learning happens: students must propose a method (sealed bag, balloon over container, etc.) that captures the gas.

**On Part E:** The climate context is not decoration — it is genuine applied conservation of mass. The 44/12 ratio is real and teachable at this level. Carbon accounting in climate science is literally applied stoichiometry. Students who compute "every 12 g of carbon produces 44 g of CO₂" have understood something genuinely useful about how the world works.

## Teacher Moves

### Launch (Minutes 0–3)

Light a candle. Place it on a digital balance. Ask: **"If I leave this burning for 10 minutes, will the mass go up, down, or stay the same?"**

Have students vote. Record the votes visibly. Then: **"Where does the candle go?"**

This question — "where does the mass go?" — drives the entire investigation into conservation of mass, combustion, and the particle model.

**EAL move:** Point at the candle. Point at the balance reading. Mime "smaller" with hands. Point at the air with a questioning gesture. The question is entirely physical.

### During the Investigation (Minutes 3–30)

**Watch for:** Students who say the mass "disappears." Push back: "Can mass disappear? Where would it go?" The idea that mass is conserved is counterintuitive when the candle is visibly shrinking, which is exactly why this investigation works.

**Watch for:** Students who know "it turns into CO₂" but can't explain *how*. The particle model is the bridge: wax molecules + oxygen molecules → carbon dioxide molecules + water molecules. Every atom in the reactants is in the products. Nothing disappears — it changes form.

**Do not say:** "Mass is conserved." This is what students need to discover. If they weigh the candle and see the mass decrease, they need to reason about where the missing mass went (into the air as gas). The conservation principle emerges from the evidence, not from a textbook statement.

**Key moment:** If possible, burn the candle in a sealed container on a balance. The mass stays constant. Open container: mass appears to decrease. Sealed container: mass stays the same. Ask: "Why the difference?" The gas products are escaping in the open system — that's the missing mass.

### Pulling It Together (Minutes 30–40)

Draw the particle model of combustion on the board. Count atoms on each side. Ask: "Are all the atoms accounted for?" Students should be able to verify that every carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atom in the reactants appears in the products.

**EAL move:** Use molecular model kits or coloured balls. Students physically rearrange "atoms" from reactants to products. The conservation becomes tactile — you can't create or destroy a ball.

### What You Are Listening For

- "The candle didn't disappear — it turned into gas" — the conservation insight
- "But it weighs less!" — the open-system confusion, which is the best teaching moment
- Students counting atoms on both sides — this is balancing equations through understanding, not procedure

> **The bigger idea:** Conservation of mass is not just a chemistry principle. It is a philosophical commitment: the universe does not create matter from nothing, and does not allow it to vanish. Every atom that ever existed still exists somewhere — rearranged, perhaps bonded differently, perhaps distributed across an ocean or atmosphere, but present and accountable. This principle is what makes quantitative chemistry possible, what makes environmental accounting possible, and what gives the concept of "waste" a rigorous meaning: waste is matter that is not where we want it. It has not disappeared. It is somewhere.
