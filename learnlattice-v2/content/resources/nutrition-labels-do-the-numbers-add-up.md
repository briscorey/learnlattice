## The Hook

Pick up your favourite snack. Look at the back.

It says **150 calories per serving.**

Now look at the serving size: **30 grams.**

That's about **6 crisps.** Or **3 biscuits.** Or **half a muesli bar.**

When was the last time you ate exactly 6 crisps and stopped?

**The numbers on nutrition labels are mathematically correct — but are they mathematically honest?**

---

## Part A: Decode the Label (Criterion A + C)

### What's Actually on a Nutrition Label?

Every nutrition label tells you the same core information, but different countries present it differently:

| Information | What it means |
|-------------|--------------|
| **Energy** (kcal or kJ) | Total energy the food provides |
| **Carbohydrates** (g) | Sugars and starches — your body's quick fuel |
| **— of which sugars** (g) | The simple carbohydrates (fast energy, sweet taste) |
| **Fat** (g) | Energy storage, cell membranes, vitamin absorption |
| **— of which saturated** (g) | Solid fats (mainly from animal products) |
| **Protein** (g) | Building blocks for muscles, enzymes, hormones |
| **Fibre** (g) | Indigestible plant material — keeps digestion healthy |
| **Salt/Sodium** (g) | Mineral for nerve function — too much raises blood pressure |

### The Energy Equation

Each macronutrient provides a different amount of energy:

- **Carbohydrates:** 4 kcal per gram
- **Protein:** 4 kcal per gram
- **Fat:** 9 kcal per gram

This means: **Fat provides more than double the energy of carbohydrates or protein, gram for gram.**

### Your First Calculation

Choose any packaged food you have (or use the data your teacher provides).

**Step 1:** Write down the per-serving values for carbohydrates, protein, and fat.

**Step 2:** Calculate the energy from each:
- Energy from carbs = ___ g × 4 kcal/g = ___ kcal
- Energy from protein = ___ g × 4 kcal/g = ___ kcal
- Energy from fat = ___ g × 9 kcal/g = ___ kcal
- **Total calculated energy = ___ kcal**

**Step 3:** Compare to the energy listed on the label. Do the numbers add up?

*(They usually don't match exactly. Why? Because fibre, alcohol, and rounding all affect the total. But they should be close.)*

## Part B: The Serving Size Investigation (Criterion C)

### The Comparison Problem

Here are two cereals:

| | Cereal A | Cereal B |
|---|---------|---------|
| Serving size | 30g | 55g |
| Sugar per serving | 9g | 12g |
| Energy per serving | 120 kcal | 210 kcal |

**Which cereal has more sugar?**

If you said Cereal B (12g > 9g), you fell for the serving size trap.

**Per 100g:**
- Cereal A: 9 ÷ 30 × 100 = **30g sugar per 100g**
- Cereal B: 12 ÷ 55 × 100 = **21.8g sugar per 100g**

Cereal A actually has **38% more sugar** than Cereal B when measured on the same scale.

### Your Turn

Collect nutrition labels from **at least 4 products** (bring from home, or use teacher-provided data).

For each product, create a standardised comparison table:

| Product | Serving (g) | Energy /100g | Sugar /100g | Fat /100g | Protein /100g | % Sugar | % Fat | % Protein |
|---------|------------|-------------|-------------|-----------|---------------|---------|-------|-----------|
| | | | | | | | | |

**Calculate the percentage composition** of each product:
- % Sugar = (Sugar per 100g ÷ 100) × 100
- % Fat = (Fat per 100g ÷ 100) × 100
- % Protein = (Protein per 100g ÷ 100) × 100

### Represent Your Findings

Create:
1. A **grouped bar chart** comparing all 4 products on sugar, fat, and protein (per 100g)
2. A **pie chart** for the product with the most surprising composition
3. A written comparison: Which product is highest in sugar? Which has the best protein-to-sugar ratio?

## Part C: The International Comparison (Criterion D)

### Labels Around the World

Nutrition labels look different in every country:

| Feature | China (中国) | USA | EU/Australia | Japan |
|---------|-------------|-----|-------------|-------|
| Energy unit | kJ | kcal | Both | kcal |
| Base | Per 100g + per serving | Per serving only | Per 100g + per serving | Per serving |
| % Daily Value | ✓ (NRV) | ✓ (%DV) | Sometimes | ✓ |
| Front-of-pack rating | No | No | Traffic light (UK) / Health Stars (AU) | No |

**The conversion:** 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ

### The Questions

Using labels from at least 2 different countries (your teacher will provide these, or research online):

1. Convert all values to the same units so you can compare
2. Are serving sizes the same across countries for the same type of product?
3. Which country's labelling system makes it easiest for a consumer to make healthy choices? Why?

### The Biology Connection

Now explain **why** the numbers matter biologically:

For each macronutrient on the label, explain:
- What your body uses it for
- What happens if you consume too much
- What happens if you consume too little
- Which foods are good sources

Use the data from your labels as examples:

> "This product provides 15g of protein per 100g. Protein is used by the body to build and repair muscle tissue, produce enzymes, and support immune function. For a teenager, approximately 0.9g of protein per kg of body weight per day is recommended, meaning a 50kg student needs about 45g per day. This single serving provides about 33% of that requirement."

## Part D: Design a Better Label (Extension)

Current nutrition labels are designed by governments and food companies. 

**Design your own nutrition label** that you think would help teenagers make better food choices.

Your design must:
- Include the key nutritional information
- Use at least one mathematical representation (chart, ratio, percentage, colour-coding)
- Be understandable to someone who hasn't studied nutrition
- Fit on a food package (realistic size)
- Include a brief written justification for your design choices

Consider: Would a traffic-light system (green/amber/red) be more effective than numbers? Would a simple "health score" out of 10 be useful, or would it oversimplify? Should labels show what the food gives you (energy, protein) or warn you about what it has too much of (sugar, salt)?

## Teaching Notes

### Why This Investigation Works for MYP Year 1-2

This investigation requires only basic mathematical skills — percentages, ratios, multiplication, division — but applies them to a context every student engages with daily. The low floor (read a label, calculate a percentage) and high ceiling (cross-country comparison, label design, biological justification) make it genuinely accessible for Grade 6 students while challenging for advanced Grade 7s.

### Handling Food Sensitivity

**Be careful.** Some students have eating disorders, restrictive eating patterns, or anxiety about food. Frame this investigation around **understanding** food, not **judging** food choices. Avoid language like "good food" / "bad food" or "junk food." Instead: "What does this food provide? When might someone choose this? What are the trade-offs?"

If a student seems uncomfortable, offer an alternative focus: comparing pet food labels, comparing sports drink labels, or analysing labels from non-food products (cosmetics, cleaning products).

### Preparation

**Option 1 (best):** Ask students to bring 2-3 packaged food items from home the day before. This creates personal investment.

**Option 2:** Collect 10-15 labels yourself and photocopy them. Include: a cereal, a chocolate bar, a sports drink, fruit juice, yoghurt, bread, crisps, a "health" bar, a ready meal, and a can of soup. Try to include labels from different countries.

**Option 3:** Use the data table provided in Part B and supplement with online label databases.

### Cross-Disciplinary Connections

**Science (Criterion A):** Macronutrients, digestion, energy transfer in biological systems. This investigation naturally introduces (or reinforces) the concept that food is a chemical energy source that the body converts to kinetic, thermal, and stored energy.

**Science (Criterion D):** Food labelling regulations vary globally. Why? Who benefits from clear labelling? Who benefits from confusing labelling? This connects to health equity, corporate responsibility, and consumer rights.

### Adaptation for EAL Learners

- Provide a bilingual label (Chinese + English) as the starting point — many products in Nanjing have both
- Create a visual vocabulary card with diagrams: carbohydrate → bread/rice image, protein → meat/egg image, fat → oil/butter image
- Use colour-coding: always show carbohydrates in orange, protein in red, fat in yellow (consistent with many label colour systems)
- Allow students to present their label design with annotations in their first language alongside English

### Equipment Needed

- Packaged food items (or photocopied labels)
- Calculator
- Coloured pencils/markers
- Graph paper or graphing software
- Optional: kitchen scale (to demonstrate what serving sizes actually look like)
