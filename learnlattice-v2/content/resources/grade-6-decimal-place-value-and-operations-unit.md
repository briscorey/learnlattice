## The Hook

In the 1999 Mars Climate Orbiter mission, NASA lost a $327 million spacecraft because one engineering team used metric units (Newton-seconds) and another used imperial units (pound-force-seconds). The navigation software read numbers like 4.45 when it expected 1.0. The orbiter entered the Martian atmosphere at the wrong angle and burned up.

The mission failed not because of complex mathematics — but because of a decimal place value error.

This unit builds the precision with decimals that scientists, engineers, pharmacists, and economists rely on every day.

- **MYP Big idea:** The base-10 place value system extends infinitely in both directions, connecting whole numbers and decimals through consistent structure.

## Inquiry Questions

**Factual:** How does the position of a digit determine its value?

**Conceptual:** Why does multiplying by 10 shift every digit one place to the left — and dividing by 10 shift it right?

**Debatable:** When is an approximate decimal answer more useful than an exact one?

## Core Concepts: Step by Step

### 1. Place Value: The Structure of Decimals

The base-10 system works the same on both sides of the decimal point — each position is 10 times larger than the one to its right.

| Hundreds | Tens | Ones | **·** | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths |
|----------|------|------|---|--------|------------|-------------|
| 100 | 10 | 1 | | 0.1 | 0.01 | 0.001 |

**Reading decimals correctly:**
- 3.4 → "three and four tenths" (not "three point four")
- 0.07 → "seven hundredths"
- 12.305 → "twelve and three hundred and five thousandths"

The word "and" marks the decimal point. Using it anywhere else is a mathematical error.

**Step-by-step: expand a decimal**

Write 47.263 in expanded form:
1. 4 is in the tens place → 40
2. 7 is in the ones place → 7
3. 2 is in the tenths place → 0.2 (or 2/10)
4. 6 is in the hundredths place → 0.06 (or 6/100)
3 is in the thousandths place → 0.003 (or 3/1000)

So: **47.263 = 40 + 7 + 0.2 + 0.06 + 0.003**

### 2. Comparing and Ordering Decimals

**The most common error:** students treat decimals like whole numbers. They think 0.9 < 0.72 because 72 > 9. This is wrong.

**Step-by-step: compare two decimals**

Compare 0.9 and 0.72:
1. Write both with the same number of decimal places: 0.90 and 0.72
2. Compare digit by digit from left to right.
3. Tenths place: 9 vs 7 → 9 is larger.
4. Therefore **0.9 > 0.72**.

> **Real-world context:** Timing in competitive swimming. At the 2008 Olympics, Michael Phelps won gold in the 100m butterfly by 0.01 seconds — one hundredth of a second. His time: 50.58 s. Second place: 50.59 s. Place value decided the gold medal.

### 3. Adding and Subtracting Decimals

**The rule:** line up decimal points. This keeps place values aligned.

**Worked example:**
Find 14.7 + 8.36.

```
  14.70
+  8.36
------
  23.06
```

Note: write 14.7 as 14.70 — adding a zero after the last decimal digit doesn't change the value but makes alignment clear.

**Common error:** students write 14.7 + 8.36 = 22.106 by treating them as whole number addition (147 + 836 = 983, insert decimal). Always line up the decimal points first.

### 4. Multiplying Decimals

**Method:** multiply as whole numbers, then count total decimal places.

**Step-by-step: 3.4 × 0.7**
1. Ignore decimals: 34 × 7 = 238
2. Count decimal places in each factor: 3.4 has 1, 0.7 has 1 → total = 2
3. Place decimal 2 places from the right: **2.38**

**Checking with estimation:** 3.4 ≈ 3, 0.7 ≈ 1, so the answer should be around 3. ✓ 2.38 is reasonable.

**Worked example:** A piece of fabric costs $12.50 per metre. You need 3.4 metres.

12.50 × 3.4:
1. 1250 × 34 = 42,500
2. Total decimal places: 2 + 1 = 3
3. Answer: 42.500 = **$42.50**

### 5. Dividing Decimals

**Dividing by a whole number:** divide normally, keep the decimal point in the same position.

**Worked example:** 9.6 ÷ 4
```
  2.4
4)9.6
  8
  --
  16
  16
  --
   0
```
Answer: **2.4**

**Dividing by a decimal:** multiply both numbers by a power of 10 to make the divisor a whole number.

**Step-by-step: 4.8 ÷ 0.6**
1. Multiply both by 10: 48 ÷ 6
2. Solve: 48 ÷ 6 = **8**

> **Why it works:** Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number doesn't change the quotient. It's the same as writing 4.8/0.6 = 48/6.

## Learning Flow (3–4 Weeks)

**Week 1 — Place Value Structure**
- Open with the Mars Orbiter story. Ask: what would have happened if the decimal was off by one more place?
- Place value slider activity: students physically move a digit card left or right and state what happens to the value (×10 or ÷10 each step).
- Ordering challenge: rank a set of 8 messy decimals on a number line (include numbers like 0.6, 0.59, 0.605, 0.6050).
- Connect decimals to fractions: show that 0.3 = 3/10 and that the place value names mirror fraction denominators.

**Week 2 — Addition and Subtraction**
- Real data context: compare world record times in athletics (100m sprint, marathon) — differences are always small decimals.
- Column addition and subtraction with decimals. Students first estimate, then calculate, then check against estimate.
- Error analysis: teacher presents 5 worked problems, some correct, some with the classic misalignment error. Students identify and fix.

**Week 3 — Multiplication and Division**
- Shopping context: unit pricing. Which is better value — 250g for $3.49 or 400g for $5.60? (Requires ÷ and comparison.)
- Multiplication with area: a room is 4.8 m × 3.5 m. How much carpet? How much skirting board?
- Division with decimals: splitting costs, converting units (km to m requires ×1000; m to km requires ÷1000).

**Week 4 — Summative Assessment**
- A real-data task: students receive a set of nutritional panel data (always decimals), answer comparison questions, calculate total intake across a day's meals, and determine if a person met/exceeded a target. Written justification required.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often think | What's accurate |
|--------------------------|----------------|
| 0.9 < 0.72 because 72 > 9 | Compare digit by digit from left; 0.90 > 0.72 |
| Adding zeros after a decimal changes the value | 3.5 = 3.50 = 3.500 — trailing zeros don't change value |
| Multiply decimals: add the decimal points | Count total decimal *places*, then insert once |
| The decimal point moves when you multiply | The digits move relative to the decimal point; the point stays fixed |

---

> **Teaching Tip:** Estimation is the most powerful self-check tool for decimal operations. If students estimate first and their calculated answer is wildly different, they know to check their decimal placement. Build the habit of estimating before calculating from day one.
