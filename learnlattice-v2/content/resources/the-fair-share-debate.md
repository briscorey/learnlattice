## The Hook

Two friends share a pizza.

- Friend A cuts it into **6 pieces** and takes **3 pieces**
- Friend B cuts it into **8 pieces** and takes **4 pieces**

Both say: "I took exactly half."

**Are they both right? How can 3/6 and 4/8 be the same amount?**

---

## The Investigation

### Part A. Paper folding

Take 4 identical strips of paper.

1. Fold Strip 1 in half. Colour 1 part → **1/2**
2. Fold Strip 2 into quarters. Colour 2 parts → **2/4**
3. Fold Strip 3 into sixths. Colour 3 parts → **3/6**
4. Fold Strip 4 into eighths. Colour 4 parts → **4/8**

**Line them up. The coloured lengths are identical.** Different fractions, same amount.

> 1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 = 4/8 = 50/100

### Part B. Finding equivalents

For each fraction, find 3 equivalents using paper folding or diagrams:
- 1/3 = ?/? = ?/? = ?/?
- 2/5 = ?/? = ?/? = ?/?
- 3/4 = ?/? = ?/? = ?/?

### Part C. The comparing challenge

Which is bigger: **3/5 or 2/3**?

You cannot compare them directly because the denominators are different. But if you convert both to the same denominator...

$$\frac{3}{5} = \frac{9}{15} \qquad \frac{2}{3} = \frac{10}{15}$$

Now it is obvious: 10/15 > 9/15, so 2/3 > 3/5.

---

## Teaching Notes

Start with paper folding, not numbers. Students who SEE that 2/4 and 3/6 cover the same length understand equivalent fractions in a way that "multiply top and bottom by the same number" never achieves.

This is Bruner's CPA at its best: concrete (paper folding) → pictorial (fraction strips) → abstract (the rule). Do not skip to the rule.
