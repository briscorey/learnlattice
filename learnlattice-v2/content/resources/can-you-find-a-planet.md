## The Hook

TRAPPIST-1 is a small, cool star 40 light-years away. It is too dim to see with the naked eye.

In 2017, astronomers announced that **seven Earth-sized planets** orbit this star. Some might have liquid water. Some might have atmospheres.

**Nobody has ever seen these planets.** Not even through the most powerful telescopes. They are too small and too far away.

So how do we know they exist?

---

## The Transit Method

When a planet passes in front of its star (from our perspective), it blocks a tiny fraction of the star's light. This is called a **transit**.

For Earth-sized planets around Sun-sized stars, the dimming is about **0.01%** — one part in ten thousand. Incredibly small. But detectable.

### The Simulation

Use a lamp, a ping-pong ball on a stick, and a light sensor (phone with a light meter app).

1. Place the lamp 2 metres from the sensor
2. Record the baseline light reading
3. Slowly pass the ball between the lamp and sensor
4. Record the minimum light reading during transit

> **Calculate the percentage dimming:**
> $$\text{Dimming} = \frac{\text{baseline} - \text{minimum}}{\text{baseline}} \times 100\%$$

### What Can You Learn?

From the transit data alone, astronomers can determine:
- **Planet size** (bigger planet = more dimming)
- **Orbital period** (time between transits)
- **Distance from star** (from the period, using Kepler's third law)

---

## The Wobble Method

A planet's gravity pulls on its star, causing a tiny wobble. We detect this wobble by measuring shifts in the star's light spectrum (Doppler effect).

> **Think of it this way:** A parent spinning a child on a merry-go-round also moves slightly. The child has a big orbit, the parent a tiny wobble. But both are real.

## Teaching Notes

This investigation connects proportion, percentages, and area calculations to cutting-edge astronomy. The transit simulation with a lamp and ping-pong ball makes the method concrete and testable — students see their light sensor dip and understand exactly what Kepler and TESS detect from space.

The key pedagogical insight: we are teaching indirect evidence. Most of science relies on inferring things we cannot directly see. This is true for atoms, genes, black holes, and exoplanets. The habit of reasoning from evidence to conclusion is more important than any specific fact about TRAPPIST-1.
