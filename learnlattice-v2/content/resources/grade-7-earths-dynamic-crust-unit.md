## The Hook

On 26 December 2004, a magnitude 9.1 earthquake struck off the coast of Sumatra. It lasted nearly 10 minutes — the longest ever recorded. The seafloor along a 1,200 km fault shifted by up to 15 metres in seconds. The energy released was equivalent to 23,000 Hiroshima bombs. The resulting tsunami killed over 230,000 people across 14 countries.

What caused it? The same slow, unstoppable process that built the Himalayas, opened the Atlantic Ocean, and makes Iceland volcanically active: plate tectonics.

This unit asks students to think like geologists — using multiple lines of evidence to build and defend an explanation for why Earth's surface looks the way it does, and why it occasionally moves with devastating force.

## Inquiry Questions

**Factual:** How do scientists measure plate movement if it happens at about the speed your fingernails grow?

**Conceptual:** Why does the same tectonic process produce completely different outcomes in different locations?

**Debatable:** Given that earthquake location is predictable but timing is not, how much should governments spend on preparedness for low-probability, high-consequence events?

## Core Concepts: Step by Step

### 1. The Structure of Earth

Earth is layered, and the layers matter:

- **Inner core** — solid iron-nickel, ~5,100–6,370 km depth, ~5,400°C
- **Outer core** — liquid iron-nickel, generates Earth's magnetic field
- **Mantle** — solid rock that flows *very* slowly (like Silly Putty over years). Convection currents in the mantle drive plate movement.
- **Crust** — thin outer shell. *Oceanic crust* is thinner (~7 km), denser, and younger. *Continental crust* is thicker (~35 km), less dense, and older.

**Why the distinction between oceanic and continental crust matters:** at subduction zones, oceanic crust always sinks beneath continental crust because it is denser. This is why the Pacific Ocean is shrinking and the Atlantic is growing.

### 2. Plate Boundaries — Three Types, Three Outcomes

**Step-by-step guide to the three boundary types:**

| Boundary type | Plates do | Geological outcome | Real-world example |
|--------------|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Divergent | Move apart | Rift valleys, mid-ocean ridges, new seafloor | Mid-Atlantic Ridge; East African Rift |
| Convergent | Move toward each other | Mountains (continent-continent); volcanoes + trenches (ocean-continent) | Himalayas; Andes; Cascades |
| Transform | Slide past each other | Earthquakes, no mountain building or volcanism | San Andreas Fault, CA |

> **Worked example — Why does BC have both earthquakes and volcanoes?**
> The Juan de Fuca Plate (oceanic) is subducting beneath the North American Plate (continental) — a convergent boundary. As the oceanic plate descends, it melts. That magma rises, forming the Cascade volcanoes (Mt. Rainier, Mt. St. Helens, Mt. Garibaldi). The grinding between plates also generates major earthquakes. BC sits directly in this zone.

### 3. Evidence for Plate Motion

Scientists use multiple independent lines of evidence — this is what makes plate tectonics one of the most well-supported theories in science:

1. **Fit of the continents** — South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces. Alfred Wegener noticed this in 1912.
2. **Matching fossils** — Identical fossils of *Mesosaurus* (a freshwater reptile) are found on both sides of the South Atlantic. It could not have swum across an ocean.
3. **Matching rock formations** — Mountain ranges in eastern Brazil and western Africa share the same age, rock type, and mineral composition.
4. **Seafloor magnetic striping** — As new seafloor forms at mid-ocean ridges, iron minerals align with Earth's magnetic field. Earth's field periodically reverses. This creates symmetrical magnetic stripes on either side of a ridge — a "tape recording" of seafloor spreading.
5. **GPS measurement** — Modern GPS confirms that North America and Europe are moving apart at ~2.5 cm per year — about the rate your fingernails grow.

### 4. Earthquakes and Seismic Waves

An earthquake occurs when stress built up along a fault releases suddenly, sending seismic waves outward from the **focus** (underground point of rupture). The **epicentre** is directly above the focus on the surface.

**Two main wave types:**
- **P-waves** (primary/pressure waves): compress and expand rock in the direction of travel. Travel through solids *and* liquids. Arrive first.
- **S-waves** (secondary/shear waves): move rock perpendicular to travel direction. Travel only through solids. Arrive second.

**Step-by-step: How seismologists locate an earthquake**
1. Measure the time gap between P-wave and S-wave arrival at a seismograph station. A bigger gap = the station is farther from the epicentre.
2. Use the time gap to calculate distance from that station.
3. Repeat for at least 3 stations.
4. Draw a circle around each station with radius = calculated distance.
5. Where all 3 circles intersect = the epicentre.

### 5. Volcanoes

Volcanoes form where magma reaches the surface. Two main tectonic settings:

- **Subduction zones:** water from the descending oceanic plate lowers the melting point of surrounding mantle rock. Magma rises explosively. These volcanoes (e.g. Mt. St. Helens) tend to be steep, violent, and dangerous.
- **Hotspots:** a plume of abnormally hot mantle rock melts through the plate above it, regardless of plate boundary. Hawaii sits over a hotspot. As the Pacific Plate moves northwest, new volcanic islands form. The older islands (now extinct volcanoes) trail behind — producing the Hawaiian island chain as a record of plate motion direction.

## Learning Flow (4–5 Weeks)

**Week 1 — Earth as a Layered System**
- Build a scale model of Earth's layers using clay (different colours per layer, scaled to actual depth ratios).
- Introduce the convection current mechanism using food colouring in water above a heat source.
- Hook: show the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami footage (1–2 min clip). Question: what caused this, and could it happen on BC's coast?

**Week 2 — Plate Boundaries and Evidence**
- Students map global earthquake and volcano data (USGS data sets work well) and identify patterns.
- Introduce the three boundary types with cross-section diagrams. Students match real locations to boundary type.
- Evidence lineup activity: present each piece of evidence (fossil maps, rock age data, magnetic stripe data, GPS readings) one at a time. Students rate the strength of each piece and justify their rating.

**Week 3 — Earthquakes**
- Seismic wave demonstration: students use a slinky to model P-wave and S-wave motion.
- Triangulation activity: use simulated seismograph arrival times from 3 stations to locate a mystery earthquake's epicentre on a map of BC.
- Analyse real seismograph data from a past BC/PNW earthquake.

**Week 4 — Volcanoes and BC Risk**
- Compare subduction zone volcano (Mt. St. Helens, 1980) vs hotspot volcano (Kīlauea, ongoing) using eruption data, lava type, and hazard profiles.
- Research task: What is the Cascadia Subduction Zone? What does evidence suggest about the frequency and magnitude of past megathrust earthquakes?
- Students produce a risk communication product (infographic, explainer video script, or public advisory document) for a BC audience.

**Week 5 — Summative Assessment**
- Novel scenario: students receive a map with geological data (rock ages, fossil distribution, earthquake locations, magnetic stripes) for a fictional region and must identify the plate boundary type, explain the geological history, and evaluate two scientists' competing interpretations.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often think | What's accurate |
|--------------------------|----------------|
| Earthquakes and volcanoes are random | They cluster at plate boundaries in predictable patterns |
| Continental plates float on liquid rock | The mantle is solid; it flows like a very slow viscous fluid |
| The "Ring of Fire" is one single fault | It is a series of subduction zones and transform faults around the Pacific |
| Wegener's theory was rejected because it was wrong | It was initially rejected because he couldn't explain the mechanism. Seafloor spreading evidence in the 1950s–60s confirmed it. |

---

> **Teaching Tip:** The Cascadia Subduction Zone is right on BC's doorstep and has produced magnitude 9+ earthquakes roughly every 200–500 years, the last in January 1700 (confirmed by Japanese tsunami records and BC First Nations oral histories). Making the hazard local and real sharpens students' engagement with the risk communication task dramatically.
