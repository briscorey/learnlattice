## The Hook

Galileo supposedly watched a chandelier swinging in Pisa Cathedral and noticed something extraordinary: no matter how far it swung, it always took the same time to complete one swing.

He was right. And the variable that controls the speed is not what most people guess.

**Three suspects:** Mass. Angle. Length. Only one changes the period. Design experiments to find which one.

---

## The Investigation

### Setup
A simple pendulum: string, a weight (washers or a bag of coins), a clamp or tape to hang it from.

**Measure the period:** Time 10 complete swings and divide by 10. This gives you an accurate average.

### Experiment 1: Does mass matter?
- Keep length and angle the same
- Change the mass (1 washer, 2 washers, 5 washers)
- Measure the period for each

### Experiment 2: Does angle matter?
- Keep length and mass the same
- Release from different angles (10°, 20°, 30°, 40°)
- Measure the period for each

### Experiment 3: Does length matter?
- Keep mass and angle the same
- Change the string length (20cm, 40cm, 60cm, 80cm, 100cm)
- Measure the period for each

---

## Results

> Mass: **No effect.** A heavy pendulum swings at the same rate as a light one.
>
> Angle: **Almost no effect** (for small angles under 30°).
>
> Length: **Strong effect.** Longer pendulums swing slower.

Plot length vs period. The relationship is not linear — it is a **square root**:

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$$

To double the period, you need to make the pendulum **four times** longer.

---

## The Galileo Connection

Galileo did not have a stopwatch. He used his own pulse to time the swings. Despite this crude measurement, he discovered one of the most elegant relationships in physics.

Every grandfather clock uses this principle. The pendulum length is precisely calibrated so that one complete swing takes exactly one second. If the clock runs fast, you make the pendulum slightly longer.

---

## Teaching Notes

This investigation teaches experimental design better than almost anything else. Students must change one variable while controlling the others — and the result is genuinely surprising. Most students (and many adults) predict that mass will matter. The reveal that it does not is a powerful moment.

This is a Dan Meyer "be less helpful" investigation. Give students the three suspects and let them design the experiments themselves. Do not tell them the answer. Do not tell them the formula. Let the data speak.
