## The Hook

Pick up any rock. It looks ordinary. But inside it is a story millions of years long.

That pebble might have started as lava, cooled into crystals, been buried under kilometres of sediment, heated and squeezed until its minerals rearranged, then pushed to the surface by tectonic forces and worn smooth by a river.

**Every rock is a time capsule. Can you read it?**

---

## The Investigation

### Part A. Rock Detectives

Examine 6 rock samples (3 igneous, 2 sedimentary, 1 metamorphic).

For each rock, observe and record:
- **Colour and pattern**
- **Crystal size** (large, small, none visible)
- **Texture** (rough, smooth, layered, glassy)
- **Hardness** (does it scratch with a fingernail? A coin? A nail?)
- **Special features** (fossils, layers, crystals, bubbles)

### Part B. Sort and Classify

Without being told the categories, sort your rocks into groups based on what you observed. Explain your grouping to another team.

> The scientific groups are: **igneous** (from cooling magma), **sedimentary** (from compressed layers), **metamorphic** (changed by heat and pressure). Do your groups match?

### Part C. Write the Story

Choose one rock. Write its biography. from its origin to how it ended up on your desk. Use the rock cycle to trace at least three stages in its journey.

---

## Teaching Notes

Buy a cheap rock collection kit (available online for under $15). Label them A-F and do NOT tell students the rock types. Let them discover the categories through observation. this is classification as a thinking skill, not a vocabulary exercise.

The biography writing is powerful for EAL students. It transforms technical knowledge into narrative, which is far more accessible than filling in a diagram. "I was born in a volcano..." is more memorable than "igneous rocks form from cooling magma."
