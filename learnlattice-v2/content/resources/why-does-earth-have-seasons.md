## The Hook

Ask every student to write on a sticky note: **"Why does Earth have seasons?"** Collect them. Do not read them aloud yet.

Now ask: **"When it is summer in London, what season is it in Sydney?"**

Winter. They know this.

**"If seasons were caused by Earth being closer to the Sun in summer, would London and Sydney have opposite seasons? Or would the whole planet be hot at the same time?"**

Silence. The whole planet would be hot at the same time. But that is not what happens. Opposite hemispheres have opposite seasons. Distance cannot be the explanation.

Read out the sticky notes. Most will say "distance from the Sun." The class has just discovered that the most common answer is wrong — and they have already figured out why.

Now: **"If it is not distance, what is it?"**

## Why This Investigation Matters

The seasons misconception is one of the most studied in science education research. A famous 1987 Harvard study showed that graduating Harvard seniors — some of the best-educated people on the planet — could not correctly explain why Earth has seasons. The distance misconception is deeply intuitive and resistant to instruction.

This investigation works because it does not tell students the answer. It gives them the logical tools to disprove the wrong answer themselves, and the physical model to construct the right one. By the end, they own the understanding — it was not handed to them.

## Core Concepts

### Axial tilt

Earth's axis is tilted 23.4° from perpendicular to its orbital plane. This tilt stays constant as Earth orbits — the North Pole always points toward Polaris. As Earth moves around the Sun, this fixed tilt means that sometimes the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun (June) and sometimes away (December).

### Light angle and energy concentration

When sunlight hits a surface directly (perpendicular), the energy is concentrated in a small area. When it hits at an angle, the same energy is spread over a larger area. This is why noon is warmer than dawn — the Sun is higher, so the light is more concentrated. The same principle explains why tilted hemispheres receive less energy per square metre.

### Day length

Tilt also affects how many hours of sunlight each hemisphere receives. In summer, days are longer because the tilted hemisphere is exposed to more of the Sun's path. Longer days mean more total energy received — a second mechanism reinforcing the temperature difference.

## Investigation Tasks

**Part A — Disproving the distance hypothesis**

Research: When is Earth closest to the Sun? (January — perihelion.) When is it furthest? (July — aphelion.) If distance caused seasons, which hemisphere should be warmest in January? Is it? What does this tell you?

**Part B — The tilt model**

Using a torch and globe, demonstrate the four seasons. Photograph the globe at each position. For each position, describe the light angle hitting London, Nairobi, and Sydney. Explain why Nairobi (equator) has minimal seasonal variation.

**Part C — The energy concentration experiment**

Shine a torch perpendicular to graph paper. Trace the lit area and count squares. Tilt the torch 23° and repeat. Tilt 45° and repeat. Calculate the energy per square (assume the torch output is constant). Plot energy concentration against angle.

**Part D — Counterfactual reasoning**

What would happen if Earth's tilt were 0°? What about 45°? What about 90° (like Uranus)? Use the model to predict, then research what Uranus's seasons actually look like.
