## The Assessment Task

### The Bouncing Ball Investigation

A student claims: "The higher you drop a ball, the higher it bounces." Your task is to design and carry out an investigation to test this claim.

**Part (i): Research question and hypothesis**
State a clear, testable research question. Formulate a hypothesis that includes a scientific reason.

**Part (ii): Experimental design**
Describe your method. Identify the independent variable, dependent variable, and at least three controlled variables. Explain how you will ensure reliability.

**Part (iii): Data collection and processing**
Collect data for at least 5 different drop heights. Present your data in an appropriate format. Process your data to show the relationship.

**Part (iv): Conclusion and evaluation**
State a conclusion supported by your data. Evaluate the method — what were the sources of error? How could the investigation be improved?

---

## Student Response A — Achieving Level 3-4

> **Hypothesis:** If I drop the ball from higher, it will bounce higher because of more energy.
>
> **Method:** I dropped a tennis ball from 5 heights and measured how high it bounced. I did it 3 times each.
>
> **Data:** [Table with drop heights 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 cm and bounce heights]
>
> **Conclusion:** My data shows that higher drop = higher bounce. My hypothesis was correct.

### Commentary

The hypothesis is testable but the scientific reasoning ("because of more energy") is vague. The method identifies the correct variable relationship but doesn't name the variables formally or list controlled variables. Data is collected with repeats (good), but no averages are calculated. The conclusion restates the hypothesis without analysing the data quantitatively.

**What would move this to 5-6:** Name IV, DV, and controlled variables explicitly. Calculate averages. Describe the pattern using data values ("for every 20 cm increase in drop height, bounce height increased by approximately 12 cm").

## Student Response B — Achieving Level 5-6

> **Research question:** How does drop height affect the bounce height of a tennis ball on a tile floor?
>
> **Variables:**
> - Independent: Drop height (cm)
> - Dependent: Bounce height (cm)
> - Controlled: Same ball, same surface, same person measuring, ball dropped (not thrown)
>
> **Method:** 5 drop heights (20, 40, 60, 80, 100 cm), 5 trials each, filmed on phone to measure bounce height accurately from slow-motion playback.
>
> **Data:** [Table with averages and ranges. Calculated efficiency for each height.]
>
> **Conclusion:** Bounce height increases proportionally with drop height. The efficiency is approximately 62% at all heights, suggesting a constant proportion of energy is lost to heat and sound at each bounce. The relationship is linear: \\(\text{bounce} \approx 0.62 \times \text{drop height}\\).
>
> **Evaluation:** The main error was judging the top of the bounce. Using slow-motion video reduced this but didn't eliminate it. I could improve by using a motion sensor instead of visual measurement.

### Commentary

Strong experimental design with clearly identified variables and a smart methodology choice (slow-motion video). Data processing includes averages, ranges, AND efficiency calculations — showing mathematical thinking applied to scientific data. The conclusion identifies a linear relationship and connects it to energy conservation. The evaluation identifies a genuine limitation and proposes a realistic improvement.

Falls short of 7-8 because the efficiency calculation, while excellent, doesn't explore whether efficiency is truly constant or varies slightly with height. A 7-8 response would test this assumption.

## Student Response C — Achieving Level 7-8

> [Includes everything from Response B, plus:]
>
> **Extended analysis:** I plotted drop height vs. bounce height and drew a line of best fit. The \\(R^2\\) value is 0.997, confirming a very strong linear relationship. However, the efficiency decreases slightly at higher drop heights (64% at 20cm, 61% at 100cm), suggesting that energy losses increase at higher impact speeds — possibly because greater deformation of the ball produces more thermal energy.
>
> **Evaluation:** My results assume the ball returns to the same point each bounce, but in reality the ball drifts horizontally. At higher drop heights, this horizontal drift is larger, which could slightly reduce the measured vertical bounce height. A controlled improvement would be to drop the ball inside a transparent tube to eliminate horizontal movement.
>
> **Extension:** I predict that on a softer surface (carpet), the efficiency would be lower because more energy would be absorbed by the surface. I could test this by repeating the experiment on 3 different surfaces.

### Commentary

This response demonstrates all strands at the highest level. The extended analysis identifies a subtle secondary pattern (decreasing efficiency) that most students miss, and provides a physically plausible explanation. The evaluation identifies a specific, non-obvious source of error (horizontal drift at higher speeds) and proposes a practical solution (tube constraint). The extension question is testable, relevant, and demonstrates genuine scientific thinking.
