## The Hook

Show a photograph of the Moon. Ask: **"What shape is the Moon right now — tonight?"**

Most students won't know. Some will guess.

**"For the next 28 days, you're going to look at the Moon every clear night and draw exactly what you see. At the end, we'll see if anyone can explain the pattern."**

---

## The Investigation

### The Moon Journal

Each student receives a Moon Journal sheet with 28 circles (one per night). Each evening, they:
1. Go outside and find the Moon (if visible)
2. Shade the circle to match what they see
3. Note the time and direction (East/West)
4. If cloudy or Moon not visible, mark an X

After 28 days, lay out all the drawings in sequence.

### The Pattern

The Moon cycles through: new moon → waxing crescent → first quarter → waxing gibbous → full moon → waning gibbous → third quarter → waning crescent → new moon. This cycle takes approximately 29.5 days.

### The Explanation

The Moon doesn't change shape. It's always a sphere, always half-lit by the Sun. What changes is how much of the lit half we can see from Earth as the Moon orbits.

## Tiered Tasks

### Part A — Criterion C (Levels 1-4)
1. Present your 28-day Moon Journal with labelled drawings.
2. Name each phase you observed.
3. How many days does one complete cycle take?

### Part B — Criterion B (Levels 3-6)
1. Using your data, predict what the Moon will look like 7 days from today. 14 days. 21 days.
2. Check your predictions against actual observations.
3. How accurate were your predictions? What pattern allowed you to predict?

### Part C — Criterion B (Levels 5-8)
1. Explain why the Moon's appearance changes using a diagram showing the Sun, Earth, and Moon positions for each major phase.
2. Lunar eclipses only happen during full moons, but not every full moon. Explain why, using the 5° tilt of the Moon's orbital plane.
3. Ancient civilisations used lunar cycles to create calendars. The Islamic calendar is still lunar. Research: how many days are in a lunar year? Why is it different from a solar year?

## Teaching Notes

This investigation requires 28 days of homework — one observation per night. Start it on a Monday and collect it a month later. Compliance will be imperfect: clouds, forgetfulness, and indoor evenings mean most students will have 15-20 observations. That's enough to see the pattern.

The power of this investigation is that students DISCOVER the pattern themselves from their own data, rather than being told "the Moon has 8 phases." The journal makes the learning personal — it's their sky, their drawings, their pattern.

**EAL scaffold:** Drawing requires no English. The circles, shading, dates, and times are universal. The pattern emerges visually.
