## The Hook

Place a bar magnet under a sheet of paper. Scatter iron filings on top.

Tap the paper gently. The filings snap into curved lines radiating from pole to pole.

Say nothing. Just point.

Ask: **"What are you looking at?"**

---

## Core Concept: Magnetic Fields

A magnetic field is the region around a magnet where a magnetic force can be detected. We cannot see the field, but we can map it using iron filings or a compass.

### Key ideas:
- Field lines run from **North to South** outside the magnet
- Field lines are **closer together** where the field is stronger (near the poles)
- Field lines **never cross**
- Like poles repel; unlike poles attract

### Why fields matter:
The concept of a "field" is one of the most powerful ideas in physics. It explains how objects can exert forces on each other without touching. Gravity, electricity, and magnetism all use field models. If students understand magnetic fields, they have the conceptual foundation for understanding gravitational and electric fields later.

## Tiered Tasks

### Part A — Criterion A (Levels 1-4)

1. Sketch the iron filing pattern you observed. Label the North and South poles.
2. Use a compass to trace the field lines around a bar magnet. Draw at least 6 field lines.
3. Where is the magnetic field strongest? How can you tell from the field line pattern?

### Part B — Criterion B (Levels 3-6)

Map the magnetic field of three different arrangements:
1. A single bar magnet
2. Two magnets with like poles facing each other (N-N)
3. Two magnets with unlike poles facing each other (N-S)

Sketch each field pattern. Describe how the patterns differ. State a general rule about how the arrangement of poles affects the shape of the field.

### Part C — Criterion B (Levels 5-8)

An electromagnet is a magnet created by running electric current through a coil of wire.
1. Research: how does increasing the current affect the strength of the magnetic field?
2. Design an experiment to test the relationship between current and field strength (you could use the number of paper clips an electromagnet can hold as a measure of field strength)
3. Predict the shape of the graph you would expect if field strength is proportional to current. Justify your prediction.

## Teaching Notes

Iron filings are the single best investment for teaching magnetism. The visual is immediate, stunning, and requires zero reading. For EAL students, the demonstration IS the instruction — the field lines speak for themselves.

Safety: iron filings are difficult to clean up if they stick to the magnets directly. Always use a sheet of paper or plastic between the filings and the magnet. Have students work in pairs so one holds the paper while the other taps.

Photograph the field patterns — they make excellent material for student work galleries and proof-of-practice documentation.
