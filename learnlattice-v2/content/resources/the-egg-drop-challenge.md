## The Hook

A raw egg is fragile. Drop it from waist height and it shatters.

But the egg inside is not the problem. **The shell can withstand enormous compression** — try squeezing one end-to-end in your palm (over a bowl). It is surprisingly strong.

What breaks the egg is not the fall. It is the **sudden stop**. The shell hits a hard surface and all the kinetic energy converts to force in a fraction of a second.

**Your mission:** Build a container from paper, card, tape, and string that allows a raw egg to survive a 3-metre drop onto concrete.

---

## The Physics: Force, Time, and Impulse

When the egg hits the ground:

$$F \times t = m \times v$$

The **impulse** (F × t) equals the **momentum** (m × v). The momentum at impact is fixed — it depends only on the height and the egg's mass.

But you can choose HOW to absorb that impulse:
- **Short time, high force** → egg breaks
- **Long time, low force** → egg survives

Every safety device uses this principle: airbags, crumple zones, bike helmets, bubble wrap. They all **increase the time of impact** to reduce the peak force.

## The Challenge

### Materials (per team)
- 5 sheets of A4 paper
- 50cm of sticky tape
- 1 metre of string
- 1 raw egg
- 30 minutes to design and build

### Rules
- The egg must be accessible after the drop (you must prove it survived)
- No pre-made packaging (no bubble wrap, no foam, no cotton wool)
- Maximum container size: fits inside a shoebox

### The Drop
From a 3-metre height (balcony, stairwell, or teacher standing on a chair).

---

## Debrief

After the drops, analyse the survivors:
- What design features worked?
- How did they increase the TIME of impact?
- What would you change for a 10-metre drop?

The best designs typically use one of three strategies:
1. **Crumple zones:** Paper structures that compress slowly on impact
2. **Suspension systems:** Egg hangs inside the container on string, slowing gradually
3. **Parachutes:** Reducing speed before impact

---

## Teaching Notes

This is the ultimate Papert investigation — students learn impulse by building, testing, and failing. The emotional stakes (a raw egg!) create urgency and engagement that no worksheet can match.

Do this outdoors. Lay a tarp on the ground for easy cleanup. Film the drops in slow motion on a phone — students will watch their designs hit and analyse the deformation frame by frame.

The connection to real engineering is direct: ask students to research crumple zones in cars, airbag deployment times, and spacecraft heat shields. The physics is identical at every scale.
