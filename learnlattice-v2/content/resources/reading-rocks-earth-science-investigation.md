## The Hook

Write these three facts on the board. Ask students to sit with them quietly.

> **Fact 1:** The oldest rocks ever found on the ocean floor are 200 million years old.
> **Fact 2:** The oldest rocks ever found on land are 4 billion years old.
> **Fact 3:** The oceans cover 71% of Earth's surface.

Ask: **"If the oceans cover most of Earth, and Earth is 4.5 billion years old — why is all the *old* rock on land?"**

Give thinking time. Most students will be genuinely puzzled. A few will guess "the ocean wears it away." This is close but not quite the mechanism — and exploring *why* it's not quite right is the entry to the whole investigation.

The answer — that the ocean floor is continuously created at mid-ocean ridges and destroyed at subduction zones, cycling entirely every ~200 million years — is one of the most striking predictions of plate tectonic theory, and one of its most powerful confirmations.

## Why This Investigation Matters

Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of solid Earth science, but most students learn it as vocabulary: "plates move, earthquakes happen, volcanoes exist." This misses the epistemological heart of the story.

Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912 with compelling evidence. He was ridiculed and largely rejected for decades — not because the evidence was weak, but because he couldn't explain *how* continents could move through solid rock. The mechanism (mantle convection + seafloor spreading) wasn't established until the 1960s. The story of plate tectonics is the story of *how scientific consensus actually forms* — through accumulating independent lines of evidence that all point to the same conclusion.

That process is what this investigation teaches.

## Core Concepts

### Line of Evidence 1: Continental Fit

In 1912, Wegener noticed that South America and Africa fit together like jigsaw puzzle pieces — not just in coastline shape, but in geology. Mountain ranges stop at one coastline and continue at the same elevation on the facing coast. Matching rock strata. Matching fossil species (Mesosaurus, a freshwater reptile, found only in South America and West Africa — it could not have swum across an ocean).

**The inference:** These landmasses were once joined.

**The limitation:** A fit alone proves nothing. Coincidences exist. To be compelling, the fit must be accompanied by independent evidence.

### Line of Evidence 2: Paleomagnetism

When molten rock solidifies, iron minerals align with Earth's magnetic field — like tiny compass needles frozen in rock. This leaves a permanent magnetic record.

Earth's magnetic poles periodically reverse (north becomes south and vice versa). This has happened hundreds of times in Earth's history.

**The discovery:** When scientists mapped the magnetic orientation of rocks on the ocean floor, they found a remarkable pattern — symmetric stripes of normally and reversely magnetised rock, running parallel to mid-ocean ridges on both sides.

**The inference:** New ocean floor is being created at the ridge, moving outward in both directions, like a conveyor belt. Each magnetic stripe represents a period of one polarity, frozen in the rock as it solidified and moved away. This is seafloor spreading.

**Step-by-step: Reading a paleomagnetic map**
1. Find the mid-ocean ridge (the central line where the symmetry is centred)
2. Identify matching stripes on both sides — same width, same magnetic polarity
3. Measure the width of each stripe — wider stripes = longer polarity periods
4. Calculate spreading rate: if a stripe that represents a 1-million-year polarity period is 25 km wide, the spreading rate is 25 km/million years = 2.5 cm/year

> **Formative checkpoint:** Show students a paleomagnetic stripe map. Ask: "The stripes get wider as you move away from the ridge. What does this tell us about spreading rate over time?" Students who understand the model will recognise that wider stripes mean the same polarity lasted longer — not necessarily that spreading was faster.

### Line of Evidence 3: Earthquake and Volcano Distribution

Plot every earthquake above magnitude 5.0 from the past 50 years. Plot every active volcano. The result is not random. Earthquakes cluster in narrow bands. Volcanoes cluster near those same bands. The pattern precisely outlines the edges of tectonic plates.

**Plate boundary types and their signatures:**
| Boundary Type | Plates Moving | Earthquakes? | Volcanoes? | Topography |
|--------------|--------------|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| Divergent | Apart | Yes, shallow | Yes (rift volcanoes) | Rift valleys, mid-ocean ridges |
| Convergent (ocean-ocean) | Together | Yes, deep | Yes (island arcs) | Oceanic trenches |
| Convergent (ocean-continent) | Together | Yes, deep | Yes (Andes type) | Mountains + trenches |
| Convergent (continent-continent) | Together | Yes, deep | No | High mountain ranges |
| Transform | Past each other | Yes, shallow | No | Fault scarps, valleys |

### Line of Evidence 4: GPS Measurement

Since the 1980s, GPS satellites have measured plate movements directly. The Pacific Plate moves ~7 cm per year northwest. The Indian Plate moves ~5 cm per year northward into Eurasia. The Atlantic Ocean widens by ~2.5 cm per year.

These measurements match the rates predicted from paleomagnetic data — confirming what the rock record inferred over 50 million years, now watched in real time by satellite.

## Investigation Tasks

### Part A — The Evidence Builder

For each of the four lines of evidence below, complete the analysis table.

| Evidence | What it shows directly | What it allows us to infer | What it cannot prove alone |
|----------|----------------------|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| Matching fossils across oceans | | | |
| Paleomagnetic stripes on ocean floor | | | |
| Earthquake distribution maps | | | |
| GPS measurement of plate velocity | | | |

**Discussion:** Why does each piece of evidence alone not "prove" plate tectonics? What is it about the *combination* of evidence that is more powerful than any single piece?

### Part B — The Wegener Problem

Read the following account:

> *Alfred Wegener presented his theory of continental drift in 1912 with the following evidence: matching coastlines, matching rock types, matching fossils, and matching mountain ranges. He was rejected by most geologists of the time, with one British geologist calling his hypothesis "utter, damned rot."*
>
> *Wegener could not explain the mechanism — how could continents move through the solid rock of the ocean floor? Without a mechanism, his evidence was interesting but insufficient.*
>
> *By 1965, the discovery of seafloor spreading provided the missing mechanism. By 1970, plate tectonics was scientific consensus. Wegener had been dead for 35 years.*

1. Was Wegener's evidence in 1912 sufficient to establish continental drift as fact? Explain using the concept of "convergent evidence."
2. Why does a proposed mechanism matter, even if the evidence for an effect is strong?
3. Wegener was right, but the scientific community was sceptical for decades. Does this represent a failure of science, or is it an example of science working correctly? Explain.
4. Design a thought experiment: if you were a geologist in 1950, before seafloor spreading was discovered, what evidence would you need to see to change your mind and accept Wegener's theory?

### Part C — The Subduction Investigation

The Cascadia Subduction Zone runs off the coast of Washington, Oregon, and British Columbia. Here, the Juan de Fuca Plate is diving beneath the North American Plate. Geological evidence from buried coastal forests and tsunami deposits in lake sediments indicates a magnitude 9+ earthquake occurred on January 26, 1700 — the date known from Japanese tsunami records.

Scientists estimate this zone is "locked" — building up stress — and will eventually produce another megathrust earthquake. Risk estimates suggest a 37% probability within the next 50 years.

**Tasks:**
1. Using the plate boundary types table, identify what type of boundary exists at Cascadia and predict what geological features you would expect to find there.
2. The Juan de Fuca Plate moves at approximately 4 cm/year. Since 1700, how far has it moved? Does this increase or decrease the likelihood of another rupture?
3. Compare Cascadia to the Himalayas: both are convergent zones. Why does Cascadia produce volcanoes and the Himalayas do not?
4. A city planner asks: "Should we build earthquake-resistant infrastructure in Portland, 200 km from the subduction zone?" Using your knowledge of seismic waves and plate tectonics, write a justified recommendation.

### Part D — The Evidence Assessment (Criterion D)

> *In 2021, a research team published seismic tomography images of the Earth's interior — using earthquake waves to "image" the mantle the way CT scans image the human body. The images showed cold, dense slabs of former ocean floor sinking into the mantle, some as deep as 2,900 km.*

1. Explain how seismic waves can be used to image the interior of the Earth — what property of waves makes this possible?
2. Cold, sinking slabs are exactly what subduction theory predicts. Does this evidence *prove* that subduction occurs? Explain the difference between evidence that confirms a prediction and evidence that proves a theory.
3. A geologist friend claims: "With GPS and seismic tomography, we now have complete proof of plate tectonics — there's nothing left to learn." Write a response challenging this claim, while also acknowledging what is solidly established.
4. Reflect on the investigation as a whole: how did your confidence in the plate tectonic model change as you added each line of evidence? What does this tell you about how scientific knowledge is built?

## Formative Assessment Checkpoints

**Checkpoint 1 — After the hook:**
"The ocean floor is younger than the continents. What does this tell you about the ocean floor — what must be happening to it?" Students should reach for "it's being destroyed somewhere" before you introduce subduction.

**Checkpoint 2 — After Line of Evidence 2 (paleomagnetism):**
"If the Atlantic Ocean is widening at 2.5 cm per year, how wide was it 100 million years ago? What does your calculation tell you about when North America and Europe were joined?" — Connects the present measurement to the deep past.

**Checkpoint 3 — End of Part A:**
"You've seen four lines of evidence. Which one do you find most convincing? Which is weakest? Would a scientist care about your personal ranking — why or why not?" — Introduces the idea that evidence quality is assessed by reproducibility, precision, and independence, not personal persuasiveness.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often believe | What's accurate |
|-----------------------------|----------------|
| "Continents float on water" | Plates float on the *asthenosphere* — a zone of partially molten rock that behaves plastically over long timescales. There is no liquid water layer under the continents. |
| "Earthquakes happen where plates crash into each other" | Earthquakes occur at ALL plate boundaries — divergent, transform, and convergent — and at some intraplate hotspots. The mechanism differs at each. |
| "Volcanic mountains form wherever plates collide" | Only ocean-continent and ocean-ocean convergent zones produce volcanic activity. Continent-continent collision (like India-Eurasia) produces fold mountains with almost no volcanism. |
| "Plate movement is too slow to matter in human lifetimes" | GPS measures it in real time. Mumbai has moved ~25 cm toward Tokyo in the last 5 years. The cumulative effect over millions of years is what creates the surface we see. |
| "Wegener's theory was rejected because scientists were stupid or closed-minded" | It was rejected because the evidence, while suggestive, lacked a proposed mechanism — and without a mechanism, even compelling pattern evidence is vulnerable to alternative explanations. This is rigorous scientific practice, not closed-mindedness. |

## Teaching Notes

**On the hook:** The old-ocean-floor-vs-old-continental-rock contrast is genuinely counterintuitive and never fails to produce real puzzlement. Do not rush to the explanation. Let students speculate. "The ocean wears away the rock" is close — erosion is real — but the timescale doesn't work (erosion rates are too slow to remove 3.8 billion years of ocean floor). The correct answer — that ocean floor is continuously recycled — needs to emerge through the investigation.

**On Wegener:** The history of science here is genuinely important and often misrepresented. Wegener was not a lone genius ignored by incompetent authorities. He was a rigorous scientist who lacked a crucial piece of the puzzle. The scientific community's resistance was not anti-science — it was appropriate scepticism about a mechanism-free theory. The lesson: evidence + mechanism + reproducibility = scientific consensus. Any two without the third is insufficient.

**On Part C (Cascadia):** This is deliberately chosen to be personally relevant to students in the Pacific Northwest context, but it works globally as a case study. The 37% probability in 50 years is a real estimate (USGS, updated 2022). The January 26, 1700 date is known with remarkable precision from the time of tsunami arrival in Japan, cross-referenced with coastal evidence in the Pacific Northwest. The precision of that historical reconstruction is itself a powerful argument for the value of Earth science.

**On the word "proof":** Science does not prove in the mathematical sense. Part D deliberately asks students to think about this distinction — "evidence that confirms a prediction" vs "proof." This is a foundational epistemological point that most students have never been asked to consider.

> **The bigger idea:** Every line of evidence in this investigation was produced by someone who had never seen a plate move, never touched the mantle, and never visited the Jurassic seafloor. What they had was method: measure what you can reach, infer what you cannot, check whether independent measurements converge on the same answer. This is not a special method for geology. It is the method of all science that investigates things beyond direct observation — particle physics, cosmology, medicine, archaeology. Understanding how plate tectonics was established is understanding how science works at its best.
