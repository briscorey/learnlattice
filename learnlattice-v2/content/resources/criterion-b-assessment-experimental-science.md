## The Assessment Task

### Context
A student notices that sugar dissolves faster when stirred. They wonder whether breaking the sugar into smaller pieces would also speed up dissolving.

### Questions

**(a)** Write a hypothesis predicting how surface area (size of sugar pieces) affects the rate of dissolving. *[Criterion B strand i]*

**(b)** Design an experiment to test your hypothesis. Include: independent variable, dependent variable, controlled variables, method, and how you will measure the dependent variable. *[Criterion B strand i]*

**(c)** Carry out your experiment (or use the provided sample data). Record your results in a table and create an appropriate graph. Describe the pattern in your results. *[Criterion B strand ii]*

**(d)** Evaluate your hypothesis. Was it supported by the evidence? Discuss the reliability of your results and suggest improvements to your experimental design. *[Criterion B strand iii]*

---

## Annotated Student Response: Levels 3–4

> **Hypothesis:** "Smaller pieces dissolve faster."
>
> **Design:** "I will put sugar in water and time it. I will use big pieces and small pieces."
>
> **Results:** A table with two rows: "Big pieces: 180 seconds" and "Small pieces: 95 seconds"
>
> **Conclusion:** "Small pieces dissolved faster so my hypothesis was right."

### Why this is Level 3–4:

The hypothesis is reasonable but lacks scientific reasoning (no "because"). The experimental design is vague — how much sugar? How much water? What temperature? What counts as "dissolved"? Only two data points with no repeats. The conclusion restates the finding without evaluating reliability.

---

## Annotated Student Response: Levels 5–6

> **Hypothesis:** "If the surface area of sugar is increased (by crushing it into smaller pieces), then the rate of dissolving will increase, because smaller pieces expose more surface to the water."
>
> **Design:** Independent variable: sugar particle size (whole cube, halved, quartered, crushed). Dependent variable: time to dissolve completely. Controlled variables: volume of water (200mL), temperature (room temp, measured), mass of sugar (4g each trial), no stirring. Three trials per condition.
>
> **Results:** Clear table with all 12 data points plus averages. Bar chart of average dissolving time per condition. "The crushed sugar dissolved in an average of 47 seconds, while the whole cube took 195 seconds. The pattern shows that smaller pieces dissolve faster."
>
> **Conclusion:** "My hypothesis was supported. Increasing surface area decreases dissolving time. The results are fairly reliable because I did 3 trials, but the range for the crushed sugar was quite large (38-62 seconds), which suggests my measurement method could be improved."

### Why this is Level 5–6:

The hypothesis includes a scientific reason ("because smaller pieces expose more surface"). The experimental design is systematic with 4 conditions, controlled variables, and 3 trials. The data is well-organised with appropriate graphing. The conclusion is evidence-based and identifies a reliability concern. However, the student doesn't explain the mechanism in depth or suggest specific improvements.

## Annotated Student Response: Levels 7–8

> [Includes everything from 5-6, plus:]
>
> **Extended justification:**
>
> "The results show a clear inverse relationship between particle size and dissolving time. This is consistent with particle theory: dissolving occurs when water molecules collide with the surface of the solute. Smaller particles have a greater total surface area exposed to water (a cube cut in half has more exposed surface than the original cube), so more water-solute collisions occur per second, increasing the rate of dissolving.
>
> **Reliability:** My three trials showed reasonable consistency for whole and halved cubes (ranges of 12s and 18s), but wider variation for crushed sugar (range of 24s). This is likely because 'crushed' is harder to standardise — the particle size varies within each trial. To improve: I would use a sieve to create uniform particle sizes, use a colorimeter instead of visual observation to determine when dissolving is complete (removing subjective judgment), and increase to 5 trials.
>
> **Extension:** This principle explains why powdered medicine dissolves faster than tablets, and why mortar-and-pestle grinding is used in chemistry to speed up reactions. The general principle — increasing surface area increases reaction rate — applies beyond dissolving to combustion, corrosion, and catalysis."

### Why this is Level 7–8:

The justification connects the results to particle theory (scientific explanation, not just observation). The reliability evaluation is specific — identifying which condition was least reliable and explaining why. The improvements are concrete and scientifically justified (sieve, colorimeter, more trials). The extension connects the finding to a general scientific principle with real-world examples.

## Using This Resource

This task works for MYP Year 2-3 Chemistry. The sugar dissolving experiment requires only sugar cubes, water, cups, and a timer. For classes without lab access, provide the sample data set and focus on the analysis and evaluation skills.

The three annotated responses can be used as a calibration activity: show students all three anonymised responses and ask them to identify which demonstrates the strongest investigation. The discussion reveals what "justify" means in practice.
