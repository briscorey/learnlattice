## The Hook

Would you rather receive **¥1,000,000 today** or **¥1 that doubles every day for 30 days**?

Most people take the million. But the doubling penny reaches **¥5,368,709** by Day 30.

On Day 20, it was only ¥5,243. The final 10 days added over 5 million. That is exponential growth — it starts slowly, then explodes.

Compound interest works the same way. And understanding it might be the most valuable mathematics you ever learn.

---

## The Investigation

### Part 1: Build the Table

Two students each deposit **¥10,000** at **8% annual interest**. One account uses simple interest (8% of the original ¥10,000 every year). The other uses compound interest (8% of whatever is currently in the account).

| Year | Simple Interest Balance | Compound Interest Balance |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | ¥10,000 | ¥10,000 |
| 1 | ¥10,800 | ¥10,800 |
| 2 | ¥11,600 | ¥11,664 |
| 3 | | |
| ... | | |
| 10 | | |
| 20 | | |

Complete the table. What do you notice about the gap between the two columns?

### Part 2: Graph Both Curves

Plot both balances on the same set of axes. Use years on the x-axis and balance on the y-axis.

- The simple interest line should be **straight** (linear).
- The compound interest line should **curve upward** (exponential).

When does the gap become noticeable? When does it become dramatic?

### Part 3: The Formula

Simple interest: $A = P + Prt = P(1 + rt)$

Compound interest: $A = P(1 + r)^n$

Where P = principal, r = rate (as decimal), t or n = time in years.

Use the formula to calculate the balance after 30 years, 40 years, 50 years. At what point is the compound balance **double** the simple balance?

---

## What You Should Find

After 10 years: simple = ¥18,000, compound = ¥21,589. A 20% difference.

After 30 years: simple = ¥34,000, compound = ¥100,627. **Triple** the simple amount.

After 50 years: simple = ¥50,000, compound = ¥469,016. **Nine times** the simple amount.

The lesson: time is the most powerful ingredient. Small differences compound into massive gaps.

---

## The Real-World Connection

This is not abstract. Every bank account, every student loan, every mortgage uses compound interest.

A credit card charging 18% APR on an unpaid ¥5,000 balance:
- After 1 year: ¥5,900
- After 5 years: ¥11,436
- After 10 years: ¥26,147

The same mathematics that builds wealth in a savings account **destroys** wealth in debt.

---

## Teaching Notes

This investigation works best when students build the table by hand for the first 5 years before using the formula. The manual calculation builds understanding of what the formula actually does — each year you multiply the current balance by 1.08, not add a fixed amount.

The strongest extension is the financial literacy connection. Have students research actual savings account rates and credit card rates in their country. The asymmetry is powerful: savings accounts offer 1-3%, credit cards charge 15-25%. The system is designed so that compound interest works much harder against borrowers than for savers.

For Nanjing context: use RMB and Chinese bank rates. Have students compare Yu'e Bao rates with traditional bank savings rates.
