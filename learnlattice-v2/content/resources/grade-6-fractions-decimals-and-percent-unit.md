## The Hook

A recipe calls for ¾ of a cup of sugar. You want to make 2½ times the recipe for a party. How much sugar do you need?

¾ × 2½ = ¾ × 5/2 = 15/8 = **1⅞ cups**

Now try the same calculation as a decimal: 0.75 × 2.5 = 1.875 cups. Both are correct — but notice something: **fractions give you exact answers**. 1.875 is exact too, but with messier numbers like ⅓ × 2⅔, fractions stay clean (⅓ × 8/3 = 8/9) while decimals loop forever (0.333... × 2.666... = 0.888...).

This unit builds fluency with fractions as a number system — not just a pizza metaphor — and connects them to the decimals and percentages that represent the same quantities in different forms.

## Inquiry Questions

**Factual:** How do you know when two fractions represent the same quantity?

**Conceptual:** Why does multiplying the numerator and denominator by the same number not change a fraction's value?

**Debatable:** Is it always better to give an exact fractional answer, or are decimals sometimes more useful?

## Core Concepts: Step by Step

### 1. What a Fraction Means

A fraction a/b means: divide 1 whole into **b equal parts**, then take **a** of those parts.

- The **denominator** (bottom) names the size of each part.
- The **numerator** (top) counts how many parts you have.

This means ¾ is not "three out of four" in a vague sense — it is precisely **three parts, each of size one-quarter**.

**Fractions on a number line:**
Place ⅗ on a number line between 0 and 1:
1. Divide the space between 0 and 1 into 5 equal sections.
2. Each section = ⅕.
3. Count 3 sections from 0 → mark ⅗.

### 2. Equivalent Fractions

Two fractions are equivalent if they represent the same point on the number line.

**Rule:** multiply or divide both numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.

½ = 2/4 = 3/6 = 4/8 = 50/100

**Step-by-step: find an equivalent fraction for ⅔ with denominator 12**
1. What did 3 become? 3 × ? = 12 → **× 4**
2. Do the same to the numerator: 2 × 4 = **8**
3. Answer: ⅔ = **8/12** ✓

**Why it works:** multiplying numerator and denominator by 4 is the same as multiplying the whole fraction by 4/4 = 1. Multiplying by 1 never changes a value.

### 3. Comparing and Ordering Fractions

**Method 1 — Common denominator:**
Compare ⅗ and ⅔.
1. Find LCM of 5 and 3 → **15**
2. ⅗ = 9/15; ⅔ = 10/15
3. 10/15 > 9/15, so **⅔ > ⅗**

**Method 2 — Cross multiplication (quick check):**
Compare a/b and c/d: compare a×d with b×c.
⅗ vs ⅔: 3×3=9, 5×2=10. Since 9 < 10, **⅗ < ⅔** ✓

**Common misconception:** larger denominator does not mean larger fraction. ⅛ < ½ even though 8 > 2. The denominator tells you how *small* each piece is — more pieces means each piece is smaller.

### 4. Adding and Subtracting Fractions

**Same denominator — just add the numerators:**
3/7 + 2/7 = 5/7

**Different denominators — find a common denominator first:**

**Step-by-step: ½ + ⅓**
1. LCM of 2 and 3 = **6**
2. ½ = 3/6; ⅓ = 2/6
3. 3/6 + 2/6 = **5/6**

**Step-by-step: ¾ − ⅝**
1. LCM of 4 and 8 = **8**
2. ¾ = 6/8; ⅝ stays ⅝
3. 6/8 − 5/8 = **1/8**

**Mixed numbers:** convert to improper fractions first.
2¼ + 1⅔:
1. 2¼ = 9/4; 1⅔ = 5/3
2. LCM of 4 and 3 = 12
3. 9/4 = 27/12; 5/3 = 20/12
4. 27/12 + 20/12 = 47/12 = **3 11/12**

### 5. Fractions ↔ Decimals ↔ Percentages

All three forms describe the same quantity — the choice of form depends on context.

| Fraction | Decimal | Percentage | Best used for… |
|---------|---------|-----------|---------------|
| ½ | 0.5 | 50% | Splitting evenly |
| ¼ | 0.25 | 25% | Quarters, discounts |
| ¾ | 0.75 | 75% | Three-quarters |
| ⅓ | 0.333… | 33.3% | Division into thirds |
| ⅕ | 0.2 | 20% | Fifths, tax rates |
| 1/8 | 0.125 | 12.5% | Eighths |

**Converting fraction → decimal:** divide numerator by denominator.
¾ = 3 ÷ 4 = **0.75**
⅜ = 3 ÷ 8 = **0.375**

**Converting decimal → percentage:** multiply by 100.
0.375 = 37.5%

**Converting percentage → fraction:** write over 100 and simplify.
35% = 35/100 = **7/20**

## Learning Flow (4 Weeks)

**Week 1 — Meaning, Equivalence, and Comparison**
- Open with: "Which is bigger — ⅗ or ⅔?" Students vote, then justify. Most intuitions fail here; this creates productive dissonance.
- Fraction strip activity: students fold paper strips into halves, thirds, quarters, sixths, eighths, twelfths — physically comparing them.
- Number line placement race: teams place a set of fractions on a shared number line. Disagreements trigger discussion.
- Equivalent fraction patterns: build a chart of equivalent fractions, notice the multiplicative structure.

**Week 2 — Addition and Subtraction**
- Cooking context: scaling recipes up and down requires adding/subtracting fractions.
- Introduce LCM as a tool — not a rule to follow blindly. Ask: why does a common denominator make addition possible?
- Mixed number addition/subtraction with real measurement contexts (lengths of wood, fabric, ingredients).

**Week 3 — Connections: Fractions, Decimals, Percent**
- Benchmark fluency: daily drill on the table above until students know ¼ = 0.25 = 25% by feel.
- Data context: a class survey. Express results as fractions, then decimals, then percentages. Which form communicates best to different audiences?
- Shopping task: 30% off a $45 item — is it easier as 30/100 × 45 or 0.3 × 45? Students compare approaches.

**Week 4 — Summative Assessment**
- Students receive a "recipe scaling + budgeting" task: scale a recipe requiring fractional ingredients, convert quantities to decimals to calculate costs, and recommend whether to buy in bulk (requiring percent comparison of unit prices). Written justification required at each step.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often think | What's accurate |
|--------------------------|----------------|
| ⅛ > ½ because 8 > 2 | Larger denominator = smaller pieces. ⅛ < ½ |
| ½ + ⅓ = 2/5 (add tops and bottoms) | Must find common denominator first: ½ + ⅓ = 5/6 |
| Fractions must be less than 1 | Improper fractions (7/4) and mixed numbers (1¾) are valid and common |
| ⅓ = 0.33 exactly | ⅓ = 0.333… (repeating) — the decimal is approximate; the fraction is exact |

---

> **Teaching Tip:** The "add tops and bottoms" misconception (½ + ⅓ = 2/5) is incredibly persistent. The most effective counter is to ask students to draw both fractions on the same number line, then add the lengths. The visual makes it immediately obvious that 2/5 is *less* than ½ alone — so the answer can't possibly be right.
