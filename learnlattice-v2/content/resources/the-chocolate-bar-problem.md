## The Hook

You have 2 chocolate bars. 3 friends want to share equally.

**How much does each person get?**

The answer is 2/3 of a bar. But the interesting question is: **how do you actually cut them?**

---

## The Four Methods

### Method 1: Cut each bar into thirds
Cut both bars into 3 pieces (6 pieces total). Give each person 2 pieces. Each person has 2/3.

### Method 2: Give each person half, then share what is left
Give each person half a bar (using 1.5 bars). The remaining half bar splits into 3 pieces. Each person gets 1/2 + 1/6 = 2/3.

### Method 3: Stack and cut
Stack the bars. Make 2 cuts to create 3 equal columns. Each person takes one column from each layer.

### Method 4: The fraction calculation
2 ÷ 3 = 2/3. Done.

> **All four methods give the same answer.** This is not a coincidence. It is the definition of a fraction: 2/3 means "2 divided into 3 equal parts."

---

## The Investigation

### Part A: Physical cutting
Use paper rectangles as chocolate bars. For each problem, find at LEAST two different ways to cut:
- 3 bars shared among 4 people
- 5 bars shared among 2 people
- 1 bar shared among 5 people
- 4 bars shared among 6 people (simplify!)

### Part B: The general rule
When you share *a* bars among *b* people, each person gets **a/b** of a bar.

> This is what a fraction IS. It is a division that might not work out evenly. 2/3 is the answer to "what is 2 ÷ 3?" — frozen mid-calculation.

---

## Teaching Notes

Jo Boaler's research shows that students who connect fractions to physical division — actual cutting, actual sharing — develop far stronger fraction sense than those who learn symbolic rules. The chocolate bar is concrete, motivating, and universal.

Use paper rectangles. Let students cut and rearrange. The physical manipulation creates fraction sense that no amount of symbolic practice can replace.
