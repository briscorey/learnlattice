## The Hook

Show the live position of the International Space Station. It is moving at 28,000 kilometres per hour. It orbits Earth every 92 minutes. Six humans live on it.

Now say: **"The ISS is falling toward Earth. Right now. It has been falling since 1998. It never stops falling. Why has it not hit the ground?"**

Let students discuss for 60 seconds. Most will say "no gravity in space" or "it is too high." Both are wrong. There IS gravity at ISS altitude — about 89% of surface gravity. And it is not too high — it is only 400 km up, about the distance from Beijing to Jinan.

The real answer: **the ISS falls toward Earth, but it is also moving sideways so fast that the ground curves away at the same rate it falls. An orbit is not about escaping gravity. It is about falling and missing.**

## Why This Investigation Matters

Understanding orbits is essential for comprehending satellites, the ISS, GPS, weather prediction, space exploration, and even the Moon's behaviour. The misconception that "there is no gravity in space" is one of the most pervasive in science education, and it directly contradicts the understanding needed for orbital mechanics.

This investigation builds the concept from a physical demonstration (two balls hit the ground simultaneously) through a thought experiment (Newton's cannonball) to a quantitative calculation (orbital speed from ISS data). Each step is grounded in evidence students can see or compute.

## Core Concepts

### Projectile motion

A horizontally thrown object falls at the same rate as a dropped object. Horizontal velocity and vertical acceleration are independent. This principle — the independence of perpendicular motions — is one of the most important in physics.

### Orbital velocity

At any given altitude, there is exactly one speed at which an object will maintain a circular orbit. At ISS altitude (400 km), this speed is about 27,600 km/h. At GPS altitude (20,200 km), it is about 14,000 km/h. Higher orbits require lower speeds.

### Free fall

Everything in orbit is in free fall — falling toward Earth continuously. Astronauts float not because gravity is absent, but because they and their spacecraft are falling at the same rate. There is no contact force pushing them against the floor, so they experience what we call "weightlessness."

## Investigation Tasks

**Part A — The simultaneous fall**

Drop one ball from a measured height. Simultaneously launch another ball horizontally from the same height. Time both falls. What do you observe? Repeat with different horizontal speeds. Does horizontal speed affect the time to hit the ground?

**Part B — Newton's cannonball on whiteboards**

Draw the Earth (circle) with a mountain. Show the trajectory of a cannonball at increasing speeds. At what point does the trajectory become an orbit? What would happen at even faster speeds? (Elliptical orbit, then escape velocity.)

**Part C — ISS orbital calculation**

The ISS orbits at approximately 400 km altitude with a period of 92 minutes. Calculate: the orbital radius, the orbital circumference, and the orbital speed. Compare your calculated speed to published values.

**Part D — Satellite comparison**

Research three real satellites at different altitudes: ISS (400 km), GPS (20,200 km), and geostationary (35,786 km). For each, find the orbital period and speed. What pattern do you notice? Why do higher satellites orbit more slowly?
