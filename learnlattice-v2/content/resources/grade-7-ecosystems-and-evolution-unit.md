## The Hook

In 1995, wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park after a 70-year absence. What happened next surprised almost everyone: rivers changed course. Elk, no longer able to graze freely near riverbanks without predator risk, moved to higher ground. Riverside vegetation recovered. Tree roots stabilised banks. Rivers narrowed and meandered less. One predator reshaped an entire landscape — not just by eating prey, but by changing how prey *behaved*.

This unit uses that story — and others like it — to build a full picture of how ecosystems work, how populations change over time, and why some traits persist while others disappear.

**Debatable:** Should humans intervene when human activity has already altered natural selection pressures on a species?

## Core Concepts: Step by Step

### 1. Ecosystem Interactions

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living environment (water, soil, temperature, sunlight).

**Food web basics:**
- *Producers* (plants, algae) capture energy from sunlight via photosynthesis.
- *Primary consumers* (herbivores) eat producers.
- *Secondary consumers* eat primary consumers.
- *Decomposers* (fungi, bacteria) break down dead matter, returning nutrients to the soil.

**Trophic cascade** — what students need to grasp: removing or adding one species can ripple through multiple levels of a food web, changing population sizes of species that never directly interact.

> **Real-world example:** Sea otters eat sea urchins. When otters were hunted to near-extinction, urchin populations exploded. Urchins ate kelp forests down to bare rock. Fish populations that depended on kelp crashed. One predator; entire ecosystem restructured.

### 2. Variation and Inheritance

Not all members of a species are identical. Individuals vary in traits like colour, size, speed, and disease resistance. Key ideas:

- **Inherited variation** is passed from parent to offspring through genes. It is the raw material natural selection acts on.
- **Environmental variation** (e.g. a scar, muscle gained from exercise) cannot be passed on.

**Step-by-step: how to distinguish them**
1. Ask: did the trait come from the organism's DNA?
2. If yes → inherited variation (e.g. fur colour, blood type, petal shape).
3. If no → environmental variation (e.g. a tan, a broken bone that healed, a learned skill).

### 3. Natural Selection — The Four-Step Logic

Charles Darwin's mechanism of natural selection follows a clear logical sequence:

1. **Overproduction:** Organisms produce more offspring than can survive.
2. **Variation:** Individuals in a population differ in heritable traits.
3. **Competition:** Resources (food, mates, shelter) are limited, so individuals compete.
4. **Differential survival:** Individuals with traits better suited to the current environment survive longer and reproduce more. Their traits become more common in the next generation.

> **Worked example — Peppered Moths (England, 1800s):**
> Before industrialisation, most peppered moths were pale — camouflaged against lichen-covered tree bark. A rare dark variant existed but was quickly eaten by birds.
> After factories coated trees in soot, pale moths became *visible* and dark moths *hidden*. Within decades, dark moths dominated industrial areas. Pale moths remained common in rural areas.
> Nothing changed in the moths' DNA by choice. Soot changed the environment, which changed which variant survived.

### 4. Adaptation

An **adaptation** is a heritable trait that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its particular environment. Adaptations are not *designed*; they accumulate over many generations of selection.

**Categories of adaptation:**
- *Structural:* physical features (e.g. the duck's webbed feet, the cactus's waxy skin)
- *Physiological:* internal processes (e.g. desert animals producing highly concentrated urine to conserve water)
- *Behavioural:* actions that improve survival (e.g. meerkats posting sentinels while the group forages)

**Common misconception to address:** Students often say "the giraffe grew a longer neck to reach leaves." Correct framing: giraffes with slightly longer necks in a population where food was scarce had a survival advantage. Those individuals reproduced more. Over thousands of generations, neck length increased across the population.

## Learning Flow (4–5 Weeks)

**Week 1 — The Ecosystem as a System**
- Map a local or regional food web (e.g. Pacific Northwest forest or BC coastal marine).
- Introduce the Yellowstone wolf reintroduction scenario. Ask: what changed, and why?
- Students predict what happens to elk, willow trees, and riverbanks if wolves are removed again.

**Week 2 — Variation and Inheritance**
- Seed dispersal variation activity: students measure variation in wingspan of maple samaras from the same tree. Plot data. Ask: why aren't they all identical?
- Distinguish inherited vs environmental variation using case cards.
- Introduce simple Punnett square logic (dominant/recessive) if curriculum time allows.

**Week 3 — Natural Selection in Action**
- Simulation: bird predation on a mixed-colour "population" of paper moths on different backgrounds. Run 5 generations. Graph changes in colour frequency.
- Case study analysis: Galápagos finch beak data — beak depth vs seed hardness across drought and non-drought years.
- Students write a 4-step natural selection explanation for one case.

**Week 4 — Ecosystem Change and Human Impact**
- Data analysis: population graphs showing predator-prey cycles (e.g. lynx and snowshoe hare from Hudson's Bay Company records, 1845–1935).
- Introduce conservation dilemma: the Vancouver Island marmot (fewer than 200 remain in the wild). Should captive breeding programs reintroduce individuals to areas with altered predator landscapes?
- Students write a position paper applying natural selection reasoning.

**Week 5 — Summative Assessment**
- Students receive a novel ecosystem scenario with population data, apply food web and natural selection reasoning, and produce a written explanation + recommendation.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often think | What's accurate |
|--------------------------|----------------|
| Animals adapt *on purpose* to survive | Variation already exists; selection acts on it over generations |
| Evolution means individual organisms change during their lifetime | Evolution is change in *population* allele frequencies over generations |
| Predators are bad for ecosystems | Apex predators often *increase* biodiversity by controlling dominant herbivores |
| More biodiversity is always better | Ecosystem stability depends on the *right* mix of species in context |

---

> **Teaching Tip:** The Yellowstone wolf story is powerful precisely because students don't expect a river to change. Lead with the river change, then work backwards through the chain of causation. It gives them a felt sense of trophic cascades before you name them.
