## The Hook

In 2012, Felix Baumgartner jumped from a helium balloon at 39,045 metres — the edge of space. During freefall, he reached 1,357.6 km/h, breaking the sound barrier. His support team tracked not just his speed, but his **rate of descent**: how many metres per second he was falling, and whether that rate was increasing or decreasing.

Rate, ratio, and proportion are the mathematical tools behind every comparison that involves *how much per how much* — speed, fuel efficiency, population density, exchange rates, ingredient ratios, and map scales. This unit builds the multiplicative thinking that makes those comparisons precise.

## MYP Curriculum Focus

- **MYP Big idea:** Proportional relationships describe multiplicative comparisons between quantities — scaling, rates, and ratios all share the same underlying structure.
- MYP content links: ratios, unit rates, proportional reasoning, percent increase and decrease, and scale in measurement and map contexts.

## Inquiry Questions

**Factual:** How do you decide whether two quantities are in a proportional relationship?

**Conceptual:** Why is multiplicative comparison (ratio) more useful than additive comparison (difference) in many situations?

**Debatable:** When does expressing something as a percentage make a comparison clearer — and when does it obscure what's actually happening?

## Core Concepts: Step by Step

### 1. Ratio — Comparing Two Quantities Multiplicatively

A **ratio** compares two quantities of the same kind by division.

The ratio of boys to girls in a class of 12 boys and 16 girls:
- As a fraction: 12/16 = **3/4**
- As a colon: **3:4**
- In words: "3 to 4"

This means for every 3 boys, there are 4 girls — regardless of class size. A class with 150 boys and 200 girls has the same ratio.

**Part-to-part vs part-to-whole:**
- 3:4 is a part-to-part ratio (boys to girls)
- Boys represent 3/(3+4) = **3/7 of the whole class**

### 2. Unit Rate — The Most Useful Form of a Rate

A **rate** compares two quantities of *different* kinds (e.g. km and hours).
A **unit rate** expresses how much of one quantity per *one* unit of the other.

**Step-by-step: find the unit rate**
A car travels 270 km in 3 hours.
1. Divide both quantities by 3: 270 ÷ 3 = **90**
2. Unit rate: **90 km per hour** (90 km/h)

Unit rates make comparison easy:
- Car A: 270 km on 30 L → 9 km/L
- Car B: 400 km on 42 L → 9.52 km/L
- Car B is more fuel efficient even though Car A used less fuel total.

> **Real-world example:** Supermarket unit pricing. A 500g box of cereal costs $4.20; a 750g box costs $5.85. Which is better value?
> - 500g: $4.20 ÷ 500 = $0.0084/g
> - 750g: $5.85 ÷ 750 = $0.0078/g
> The larger box is cheaper per gram — but only by a tiny amount. A proportional thinker sees this instantly.

### 3. Proportions — Scaling Ratios

A **proportion** is an equation stating that two ratios are equal: a/b = c/d.

**Step-by-step: solve a proportion**
A map has a scale of 1:50,000. A road on the map is 4.6 cm long. How long is the real road?

1. Set up: 1/50,000 = 4.6/x
2. Cross multiply: 1 × x = 4.6 × 50,000
3. x = **230,000 cm = 2.3 km**

**Testing for proportionality:**
A relationship is proportional if the ratio y/x is constant for all pairs.

| x | y | y/x |
|---|---|-----|
| 2 | 6 | 3 ✓ |
| 5 | 15 | 3 ✓ |
| 8 | 24 | 3 ✓ |

This is proportional (y = 3x). If y/x varied, it's not proportional.

**Non-proportional trap:** A taxi charges $3.50 flat fee plus $2.20 per km.
- 1 km: $5.70 → ratio = 5.70
- 2 km: $7.90 → ratio = 3.95
- Not proportional — the flat fee breaks the constant ratio.

### 4. Percent Change

Percent change measures how much a quantity has grown or shrunk *relative to its original size*.

$$\text{Percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}} \times 100$$

**Worked example — percent increase:**
A jacket costs $80. It goes on sale for $68. What is the percent decrease?

1. Change = 68 − 80 = −12 (decrease of $12)
2. Percent change = −12/80 × 100 = **−15%**
3. The jacket is **15% off**.

**Worked example — percent increase:**
A town's population was 24,000 in 2010. It is 27,600 in 2025.
1. Change = 27,600 − 24,000 = 3,600
2. Percent change = 3,600/24,000 × 100 = **15% increase**

**The common trap — percent increase vs decrease are not symmetric:**
- A price increases 20%: $100 → $120
- Then decreases 20%: $120 × 0.80 = **$96** (not $100!)
- A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease gives a net 4% loss.

### 5. Scale — Ratios in Space

Scale is proportion applied to size. Maps, models, and blueprints all use it.

**Reading a scale:** 1:200,000 means 1 unit on the map = 200,000 units in reality.
- 1 cm on map = 200,000 cm = 2 km in reality.

**Step-by-step: a building blueprint at 1:50**
A wall on the blueprint measures 8.4 cm. How long is the real wall?
1. Real length = 8.4 × 50 = **420 cm = 4.2 m**

## Learning Flow (4–5 Weeks)

**Week 1 — Ratio and Multiplicative Thinking**
- Open with Baumgartner's jump data. Students calculate his average rate of descent in different units. Is 1,357.6 km/h the same as ~377 m/s? (It is — unit conversion is proportion.)
- Recipe ratio challenge: a smoothie recipe for 2 people. Scale to 5, then to 50. What changes and what stays the same?
- Distinguish additive vs multiplicative comparison: "Class A grew from 20 to 25 students; Class B grew from 30 to 36. Which grew more?" Students debate whether +5 or +6 students, or 25% vs 20% growth, is the more meaningful comparison.

**Week 2 — Unit Rates and Value Comparison**
- Supermarket challenge: teams receive real (or realistic) product data and must identify best value using unit rates. Present findings.
- Speed, fuel efficiency, and population density problems — all require identifying and computing unit rates.
- Proportional vs non-proportional relationships: students graph data sets and identify which produce straight lines through the origin (proportional) vs those that don't.

**Week 3 — Percent and Percent Change**
- Discounts, tax, tips: students calculate final prices in consumer contexts.
- Percent change with population and economic data (real data from World Bank or similar).
- The symmetry trap: students work through the 20% increase / 20% decrease problem, then generalise: what does it mean that percentages are always relative to the current base?

**Week 4 — Scale and Maps**
- Students use a real map (Google Maps screenshot or printed map with scale bar) to calculate actual distances.
- Blueprint activity: students draw a scale version of the classroom or a simple room layout.
- Culminating problem: a hiking route on a 1:25,000 topographic map. Students calculate total distance, compare two routes, and justify which is faster given also-provided average hiking speeds.

**Week 5 — Summative Assessment**
- A novel proportional reasoning task: students receive a data set (could be sports statistics, population data, or a consumer comparison) and must identify whether a proportional relationship exists, calculate relevant rates or percentages, and write a justified recommendation.

## Common Misconceptions

| What students often think | What's accurate |
|--------------------------|----------------|
| The bigger absolute change = bigger growth | Must compare relative to original (% change) for fair comparison |
| A 20% increase then 20% decrease = back to start | Net result is a 4% decrease (the base changes) |
| All relationships with two quantities are proportional | Only when y/x is constant (graph passes through origin) |
| Larger scale number = larger map | 1:25,000 shows *more* detail than 1:250,000 — smaller scale number = zoomed in |

---

> **Teaching Tip:** The most powerful moment in this unit is when students discover that proportional reasoning lets them solve problems they couldn't solve with arithmetic alone. Don't rush to the algorithm — let the struggle with the recipe scaling problem (for a non-whole number of people) motivate the need for ratio thinking.
