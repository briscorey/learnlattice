## The Hook

Drop an Alka-Seltzer tablet into ice water. Start your timer.

It fizzes... slowly... gently... for nearly **two minutes**.

Now drop one into hot water.

**Fifteen seconds.** Violent fizzing, then done.

Same tablet. Same chemical reaction. Just heat. Why does temperature make such an enormous difference?

---

## The Investigation

### Setup

Prepare five beakers with 100 mL of water at different temperatures:

| Beaker | Target Temperature | How to Prepare |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ≈ 5°C | Ice water |
| 2 | ≈ 15°C | Cold tap water |
| 3 | ≈ 22°C | Room temperature water (left out) |
| 4 | ≈ 40°C | Mix hot and cold tap water |
| 5 | ≈ 60°C | Hot tap water or heated |

### Method

1. Measure the **exact** temperature of each beaker
2. Drop one whole Alka-Seltzer tablet into Beaker 1
3. Start the stopwatch immediately
4. Stop when **fizzing completely stops**
5. Record the time
6. Repeat for Beakers 2-5

### Results Table

| Beaker | Temperature (°C) | Reaction Time (s) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | | |
| 2 | | |
| 3 | | |
| 4 | | |
| 5 | | |

### Graph

Plot temperature (x-axis) against reaction time (y-axis). Draw a smooth curve of best fit.

**The curve is the discovery.** It is not a straight line. The reaction time drops sharply at first, then levels off. This tells you the relationship between temperature and rate is non-linear.

---

## The Explanation

At higher temperatures, particles have more **kinetic energy**. This causes two effects:

1. **More frequent collisions** — particles move faster, so they meet each other more often
2. **More energetic collisions** — particles hit each other harder, so more collisions have enough energy to break bonds and start the reaction

Both effects combine to dramatically increase the rate. This is **collision theory**.

A useful rule of thumb: for every **10°C increase**, the reaction rate approximately **doubles**. This is why the graph curves — it follows an exponential pattern, not a linear one.

## Teaching Notes

This is one of the cleanest investigations in MYP Chemistry. The results are reliable, the equipment is cheap, and the pattern is clear. It maps perfectly to Criterion B (designing the method) and Criterion C (processing data and drawing conclusions).

The most common student error is measuring temperature AFTER the reaction starts, by which time the tablet has changed the water temperature. Measure temperature just before adding the tablet.

Use whole Alka-Seltzer tablets, not halves. If tablets are broken unevenly, surface area becomes a confounding variable. For the same reason, do not crush them.

The extension to everyday life is rich: refrigeration, cooking, glow sticks (cool them to make them last longer), hand warmers, and engine design.
