## The Hook

Two pizzas. Same thickness. Same toppings.

- **12-inch pizza:** \\(12 (that is \\)1 per inch, seems fair)
- **16-inch pizza:** \\(16 (also \\)1 per inch)

Which is the better deal?

---

## The Mathematics

Pizza is measured by **diameter**, but you eat **area**.

$$A = \pi r^2$$

| Pizza | Diameter | Radius | Area | Price | Price per sq inch |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small | 12 in | 6 in | 113.1 sq in | \\(12 | \\)0.106 |
| Large | 16 in | 8 in | 201.1 sq in | \\(16 | \\)0.080 |

The 16-inch pizza has **78% more food** but costs only **33% more**. It is dramatically better value.

> **The 16-inch pizza is almost TWO 12-inch pizzas.** But it does not look like it, because our eyes judge diameter, not area.

---

## Why This Matters

This is not just about pizza. The same principle applies everywhere:

- A pipe with double the diameter carries **four times** the water
- A lens with double the diameter collects **four times** the light
- A parachute with double the diameter has **four times** the air resistance

> When linear dimensions double, area **quadruples**. When they triple, area goes up by a factor of **nine**.

$$\text{Area scale factor} = (\text{length scale factor})^2$$

---

## Teaching Notes

Open with a genuine debate: "Which pizza should we order for the class?" Students instinctively go for two smalls or argue about toppings. The mathematical reveal is genuinely surprising and creates a "wait, really?" moment that anchors the concept.

This investigation makes area scaling concrete and personally relevant. Every student will think about this the next time they order pizza — and that is exactly the point.
