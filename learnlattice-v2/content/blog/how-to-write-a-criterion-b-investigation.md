## The Problem with Most Criterion B Investigations

Let me describe a Criterion B investigation you have probably seen. Maybe you have taught it. Maybe you wrote it.

There is a table of values. The student is told to "investigate the pattern." They calculate first differences. They write a general rule. They verify it. They justify it. They get a score between 1 and 8.

The problem is not that this format fails to assess Criterion B. It does assess it. The problem is that no student has ever walked into a classroom excited to investigate a table of values. The investigation *works*. it just does not *matter* to anyone in the room.

After designing MYP Criterion B investigations for years. and redesigning them after watching students disengage from each one. I have landed on a set of principles that produce investigations students actually want to complete. Not because the stakes are high, but because the question is interesting.

## What Criterion B Actually Asks For

Before redesigning the format, it helps to read the criterion with fresh eyes. MYP mathematics Criterion B. Investigating Patterns. asks students to:

**i.** Select and apply mathematical problem-solving techniques to discover complex patterns

**ii.** Describe patterns as general rules consistent with findings

**iii.** Prove, or verify and justify, general rules

That is it. Three strands. Notice what is *not* specified: the context, the format, the difficulty level, or whether the pattern has to emerge from a table. A student who notices that every earthquake epicentre in the last century falls along the same curved lines. and who then describes the mathematical pattern of those curves. is investigating patterns just as rigorously as a student completing first differences on a number sequence.

The criterion is more open than most of us teach it.

## Five Principles for Criterion B Investigations That Work

### 1. Start with a question, not a table

The single most effective change I have made to my Criterion B investigations is structural: I no longer start with data. I start with a question that makes students *want* data.

In [The Staircase Problem](/resources/the-staircase-problem-algebra-investigation/), the question is: *How many blocks would you need to build a staircase 100 steps tall?* Students can visualise it. They can estimate. They have a prediction before they have done any mathematics. The table of values emerges from the problem. it is a tool they reach for, not a worksheet I hand them.

Compare this to: "Investigate the pattern in the following table: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15..." Same mathematics. Completely different energy in the room.

### 2. Use a hook that works before reading

This matters in every classroom, but it matters especially if you teach multilingual learners. If the first thing a student must do is decode a paragraph of English instructions, you have placed a language barrier between them and the mathematics.

In [The Million Dollar Question](/resources/the-million-dollar-question-number-sense/), the hook is six words on the board: *$1,000,000. One dollar per second. How long?* Every student in the room can access this. regardless of their English proficiency. The investigation begins with estimation and mental arithmetic, not reading.

This is not just an EAL accommodation. It is better pedagogy for everyone. Dan Meyer calls it Act 1: *"Impose as few demands as possible. of language or of math."* The first moment of a Criterion B investigation should recruit curiosity, not test literacy.

### 3. Build the investigation around a misconception worth breaking

The investigations students remember are the ones that proved them wrong.

[Is This Die Cheating?](/resources/is-this-die-cheating-probability-investigation/) works because students arrive with the conviction that 30 rolls is enough to prove a die is unfair. They are wrong, and the investigation is the process of discovering *how* they are wrong. how sample size, expected variation, and statistical reasoning interact to make the question harder than it appears.

The misconception is the engine. Without it, you have a calculation exercise. With it, you have an investigation.

When designing a new Criterion B investigation, I ask: *What do students currently believe about this topic that is wrong or incomplete? Can I build a problem that surfaces that belief and then requires mathematical investigation to resolve it?*

### 4. Make the justification genuinely necessary

Strand (iii) of Criterion B asks students to "prove, or verify and justify, general rules." In most investigations, this becomes a formality: students check their rule against two more values and write "therefore my rule works."

That is verification, not justification. And students know it is a hoop to jump through.

In [0.999... = 1? Prove It.](/resources/zero-point-nine-repeating-decimals-investigation/), justification is the entire point. Students must construct at least two different proofs that 0.999... equals 1. algebraic, geometric, or limit-based. There is no moment where justification feels like an afterthought, because the question itself demands proof. You cannot answer "does 0.999... equal 1?" with a table of values.

The design principle: choose questions where the justification *resolves a genuine tension*, not where it confirms something already obvious.

### 5. Plan the teacher moves, not just the student tasks

A Criterion B investigation is not a handout. It is a classroom event that requires facilitation. The difference between an investigation that produces mathematical thinking and one that produces silence is almost never the task itself. it is what the teacher says and does while students are working.

For every investigation I write, I plan:

- **What I will say** in the first 90 seconds (the exact language of the hook)
- **What I expect students to try** that will not work (and how I will respond)
- **What I am listening for** that signals understanding or misconception
- **When to stop the room** for a whole-class moment
- **What the EAL scaffold is**. the visual or simplified language alternative

These are what I call [Teacher Moves](/resources/the-million-dollar-question-number-sense/), and they are published alongside every flagship investigation on this site. They are the layer that turns a good task into a great lesson.

## Common Mistakes I Have Made (And How I Fixed Them)

### Mistake 1: Too many parts

My early investigations had five or six sections, each with sub-parts. Students spent more time navigating the document than doing mathematics. Now I aim for three sections maximum, with clear escalation: *notice → describe → prove*.

### Mistake 2: Front-loading the vocabulary

I used to define every term before the investigation started. "Recall that a sequence is..." The result was that students were reading definitions instead of investigating patterns. Now I let the vocabulary emerge from the work. If a student needs the word "quadratic" to describe what they have found, I give it to them *after* they have found it.

### Mistake 3: Identical difficulty across all strands

Criterion B is assessed on achievement levels 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, and 7-8. If every part of my investigation targets levels 7-8, half the class cannot access it. If every part targets levels 3-4, nobody is stretched.

Now I design investigations with a low floor and a high ceiling. The opening question is accessible to every student. The extension requires genuine mathematical sophistication. The rubric clarifies which level of response corresponds to which level of complexity. and students can see that continuum in the task itself.

### Mistake 4: Forgetting that investigation is a skill, not a talent

Some students arrive in MYP with years of practice in open-ended mathematical thinking. Others have only ever done procedural mathematics. The first time you hand the second group a Criterion B investigation, they will stare at it and ask "but what am I supposed to do?"

This is not a failure of the task. It is a signal that investigating patterns is a skill that needs to be taught explicitly. I now spend the first two weeks of each year on low-stakes formative investigations. problems where the mathematical content is easy but the *process* of investigation (testing, organising, conjecturing, proving) is the learning goal.

Peter Liljedahl's [Building Thinking Classrooms](https://www.buildingthinkingclassrooms.com/) research calls these "non-curricular thinking tasks". problems designed to build a classroom culture of thinking before you introduce curricular content. I have found them essential for preparing students to do genuine Criterion B investigations later in the year.

## A Template That Works

Here is the structure I use for every Criterion B investigation:

**Page 1: The Hook**
A visual, a question, or a provocation. No instructions yet. No definitions. Just something that makes students wonder.

**Page 2: The Investigation**
Three to four carefully sequenced prompts that move from specific cases to a general pattern. Each prompt uses MYP command terms (describe, explain, justify) at increasing complexity. Space for student work. not lines to write on, but open space that says "show me your thinking however you want to."

**Page 3: The Justification Challenge**
A specific prompt that requires students to prove or justify their general rule. This is not "check your answer". it is "convince someone who does not believe you." The framing matters: justification as persuasion produces better mathematical reasoning than justification as compliance.

**Alongside: Teacher Moves**
A separate section. not given to students. that includes the pacing guide, the anticipated student responses, the EAL scaffolds, and the "what to listen for" cues.
