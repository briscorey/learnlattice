## What MYP Sciences Criterion B Assesses — and Why It Is Not the Same as Math Criterion B

MYP Sciences Criterion B — Inquiring and Designing — assesses whether students can formulate testable research questions, design controlled experiments, and explain scientific methodology. This is fundamentally different from Mathematics Criterion B (Investigating Patterns), even though they share a letter. In science, Criterion B is about the *design* of an investigation, not the analysis of results (that is Criterion C).

The four strands are: explaining a problem or question to be tested by a scientific investigation, formulating a testable hypothesis and explaining it using scientific reasoning, explaining how to manipulate the variables and design a method that allows for the collection of sufficient relevant data, and designing a logical and complete method.

## The Achievement Levels in Plain Language

**Levels 1-2 (Limited):** The student states a vague question ("What happens when you heat water?") and a prediction without scientific reasoning. The method is incomplete — missing key steps, equipment, or safety considerations.

**Levels 3-4 (Adequate):** The student writes a research question that identifies variables, though it may not be fully testable. The hypothesis includes a prediction with some scientific reasoning. The method outlines the main steps but lacks detail on repetition, data collection, or variable control.

**Levels 5-6 (Substantial):** The research question clearly identifies independent, dependent, and controlled variables. The hypothesis is testable and supported by relevant scientific reasoning. The method is detailed enough for another student to replicate, with attention to fair testing and sufficient data collection.

**Levels 7-8 (Excellent):** The research question is focused and sophisticated. The hypothesis includes detailed scientific reasoning that explains *why* the prediction is expected. The method is comprehensive: another scientist could replicate the experiment exactly, with justified choices about sample size, repetitions, range of independent variable, and data collection techniques. The student explains *why* each design choice was made, not just *what* they will do.

### The Critical Jump: Outlining vs. Explaining a Method

At levels 3-4, a student *outlines* a method: "1. Get a beaker. 2. Add water. 3. Heat it. 4. Measure the temperature."

At levels 5-6, a student *describes* a method with controlled variables: "Measure 200mL of water into a 250mL beaker. Place on a hot plate set to medium. Record the temperature every 30 seconds using a digital thermometer. Repeat three times."

At levels 7-8, a student *explains* the method with justified design choices: "I will use 200mL because this provides enough volume for accurate temperature measurement while heating quickly enough to observe within one lesson. I will record every 30 seconds because preliminary tests showed significant temperature change within this interval. Three repetitions allow calculation of a mean to reduce the effect of random errors, though five would be preferable if time permitted."

## Teaching the Research Question Hierarchy

Students struggle most with formulating research questions that are genuinely testable and specific. Use this hierarchy to scaffold the skill:

**Level 1 — Topic question:** "How does temperature affect plants?" (Too broad — not testable)

**Level 2 — Variable question:** "Does higher temperature make plants grow faster?" (Identifies variables but lacks specificity)

**Level 3 — Testable question:** "How does water temperature (20°C, 30°C, 40°C, 50°C) affect the germination rate of cress seeds over 7 days?" (Specific, measurable, defines range and timeframe)

**Level 4 — Justified question:** The Level 3 question plus reasoning: "I chose cress seeds because they germinate quickly, allowing data collection within one week. The temperature range spans from room temperature to the point where enzyme denaturation is expected."

## Common Criterion B Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Confusing hypothesis with prediction.** "I think the ball will bounce higher" is a prediction. "I hypothesise that increasing drop height will increase bounce height because gravitational potential energy is proportional to height, and more energy is available to convert to kinetic energy during the bounce" is a hypothesis. The difference is scientific reasoning.

**Mistake 2: Not requiring variable identification.** Students should explicitly state the independent variable (what I change), dependent variable (what I measure), and controlled variables (what I keep the same) before writing their method. This is a non-negotiable scaffold at levels 3-4 and becomes internalised by levels 7-8.

**Mistake 3: Accepting "repeat 3 times" without justification.** At levels 5-6, students state they will repeat. At levels 7-8, they explain why a specific number of repetitions is appropriate and acknowledge the limitations of their sample size.
