## The Short Version

The IB is making the biggest changes to the MYP since the 2014 "Next Chapter" revision. Beta schools have been piloting since December 2024. Full mandatory implementation is **September 2027**. The first eAssessment under the new framework will be **May 2030**.

The changes affect four areas: how we use concepts, how we frame inquiry, how we connect to global contexts, and how we approach service learning. Assessment criteria are **not changing** in this phase — Criteria A through D remain the same for mathematics and sciences.

If you teach the current MYP well, most of what you do will still work. The changes are designed to make the programme more flexible, not more restrictive. But there are genuine shifts in language and expectation that are worth understanding now, especially if you are applying to new schools that may already be piloting the beta.

## Change 1: Key and Related Concepts Become "Specified Concepts"

**What is changing:** The current two-tier system of 16 prescribed key concepts (shared across all subjects) plus discipline-specific related concepts is being replaced by a single list of **specified concepts** for each subject group. Each subject's list contains roughly 25 or more concepts that schools can choose from and add to.

**What this means in practice:** You no longer need to identify a separate "key concept" and "related concept" for every unit. Instead, you select from the specified concepts list for your subject group. The distinction between key and related is gone. Schools can also add their own concepts to the list if the specified ones do not capture something important in their context.

**What stays the same:** Concept-based teaching remains central to the MYP. You still need to identify concepts that drive your unit. The pedagogical approach — using concepts as a lens for inquiry rather than as content to be memorised — is unchanged.

**What to do now:** Nothing dramatic. If you currently use key and related concepts thoughtfully in your units, the transition is mostly a relabelling exercise. The important shift is philosophical: you have more freedom to choose concepts that genuinely fit your unit rather than forcing a prescribed key concept into a unit where it does not naturally belong.

For mathematics teachers, concepts like *patterns*, *relationships*, *change*, *equivalence*, *representation*, *generalisation*, *measurement*, *models*, and *space* are likely to appear on the specified list. For sciences, expect *systems*, *change*, *relationships*, *evidence*, *models*, *energy*, *patterns*, *interaction*, *form*, and *transformation*.

## Change 2: Statement of Inquiry Becomes "Inquiry Question" or "Inquiry Statement"

**What is changing:** The Statement of Inquiry — that sentence teachers have been crafting (and agonising over) since 2014 — is being replaced with greater flexibility. Teachers can now express the unit's central inquiry as either an **inquiry question** or an **inquiry statement**. The requirement to reference specific concepts in the wording has been removed.

**What this means in practice:** This is the change that will generate the most relief in staffrooms worldwide. The Statement of Inquiry was widely acknowledged as one of the most confusing and frustrating elements of MYP unit planning, particularly for teachers working in their additional language. Sentences like "The relationship between form and function determines how systems process change" were technically compliant but often pedagogically meaningless.

Under the new guidance, you could instead write: "How does the shape of a container affect how much it holds — and why does nature seem to prefer certain shapes?" This is clearer for students, easier for teachers, and more genuinely inquiry-driving.

**What stays the same:** Units still need a central inquiry focus. The purpose — giving students a meaningful question to investigate across the unit — has not changed. What has changed is that you have freedom to express it in whatever form works best for your students and your context.

**What to do now:** Start experimenting. Take your existing Statements of Inquiry and try rewriting them as questions. If the question is genuinely interesting to students — something they would actually want to investigate — you are on the right track. If it sounds like something only an IB coordinator would care about, rewrite it until a 13-year-old would want to know the answer.

**Example conversions for mathematics:**

Current SOI: "Logical relationships between variables can be represented in multiple forms to reveal patterns and make predictions."

Better inquiry question: "If you know how one thing changes, can you always predict what happens to something else? When does the prediction break down?"

Current SOI: "Generalisation through pattern recognition allows us to make claims about infinite cases from finite observations."

Better inquiry question: "You have checked five examples. Is that enough to be sure? How many examples do you need before you can say 'always'?"

## Change 3: Global Contexts Gain Three "Contextual Lenses"

**What is changing:** The six MYP Global Contexts (Identities and Relationships, Orientation in Space and Time, Personal and Cultural Expression, Scientific and Technical Innovation, Globalization and Sustainability, Fairness and Development) remain unchanged. What is new is the addition of three **contextual lenses**: individual, local, and global. Teachers are encouraged to place their unit in one or a combination of these lenses alongside the global context.

**What this means in practice:** This addresses a genuine problem in the current framework: global contexts were sometimes applied so broadly that they lost meaning. A unit on fractions tagged "Fairness and Development" because fractions relate to fair sharing is technically compliant but pedagogically hollow.

The contextual lenses encourage specificity. A statistics unit with the global context "Fairness and Development" might be framed:
- **Individual lens:** "How do I evaluate whether a claim in my social media feed is statistically valid?"
- **Local lens:** "Is the distribution of green space in our city equitable across different neighbourhoods?"
- **Global lens:** "How do countries use GDP per capita to compare wealth — and what does the number hide?"

Each lens generates a different inquiry question, different data sources, and different Criterion D discussions. The same mathematical content (measures of central tendency, data representation) serves all three.

**What stays the same:** Global contexts are not being removed or renamed. You still select a global context for each unit. The contextual lenses are an additional layer, not a replacement.

**What to do now:** For each existing unit, identify which contextual lens you are already using (most teachers default to one level without realising it). Then consider whether shifting the lens would create a more engaging inquiry. In my experience, the local lens produces the strongest student engagement because students can collect their own data and see the results in their own community.

## Change 4: Service as Action Becomes "Community Engagement"

**What is changing:** "Service as Action" is being rebranded as "Community Engagement." The shift is intended to broaden the scope beyond traditional service activities and give schools more flexibility in how they connect learning to community.

**What this means in practice:** For most mathematics and science teachers, this change has minimal impact on daily classroom practice. The requirement for students to engage with their community is not new — what is new is the explicit framing that community engagement can take many forms, including advocacy, awareness-raising, investigation, and action, not just traditional volunteer service.

A mathematics unit where students analyse local traffic data and present findings to the city council is community engagement. A science unit where students test school air quality and share results with the administration is community engagement. These were always valid under the MYP framework, but the new language makes this explicit.

**What stays the same:** The MYP Community Project (Year 3 or 4) remains. The expectation that students demonstrate community engagement across the programme remains. The change is primarily linguistic and philosophical — broadening what "counts."

## What About Assessment Criteria?

Assessment criteria (A through D) for mathematics and sciences are **not changing** in this phase of the enhancement. The descriptors, the 1-8 achievement levels, and the four strands per criterion remain as they are. This is significant — it means your existing assessment tasks, rubrics, exemplars, and moderation practices remain valid.

The IB has signalled that assessment enhancement will come in a later phase, but for September 2027, the focus is entirely on unit planning elements.

## Timeline and What to Do When

**Now – August 2027:** Familiarise yourself with the changes. Experiment with inquiry questions in your current planning. Consider joining the beta trial if your school has not already. No action is required yet.

**September 2027:** All MYP schools must implement the enhanced framework. This means using specified concepts, inquiry questions/statements, and contextual lenses in unit planning.

**May 2030:** First eAssessment under the new framework. This is the deadline for eAssessment schools to have fully transitioned their Year 4-5 curriculum.

**For job applicants:** If you are applying to Tier-1 international schools in the 2027-2028 hiring cycle, demonstrating awareness of the Enhanced MYP is a significant differentiator. Being able to discuss specified concepts, inquiry questions, and contextual lenses in an interview signals that you are current, engaged, and ready to lead the transition in your new school.

## The Bottom Line

The Enhanced MYP is not a revolution. It is a thoughtful loosening of constraints that were widely experienced as too rigid. The core of the programme — concept-based inquiry, criterion-referenced assessment, global contexts, approaches to learning — remains intact. What changes is the *how*: more flexibility in how you express concepts, more freedom in how you frame inquiry, more specificity in how you connect to context.

If you teach the MYP well now, you will teach the Enhanced MYP well. The changes reward the teachers who were already asking good questions, choosing meaningful contexts, and using concepts as genuine thinking tools rather than compliance checkboxes.
