The worst differentiation advice I ever received was "make an easy version, a medium version, and a hard version." Three tasks means three sets of marking, three sets of feedback, and — worst of all — students who know exactly which group they've been sorted into.

Here's what actually works: **one task with built-in stretch.**

## The Structure That Works

Every well-designed MYP investigation already has differentiation built into the criterion descriptors. Level 1-2 asks students to identify simple patterns. Level 7-8 asks them to justify general rules. That's not three different tasks — it's one task with different ceilings.

The trick is making the entry point low enough that every student can start, while leaving the ceiling high enough that the strongest students are still thinking at the end of the lesson.

## A Concrete Example

Take the [Staircase Problem](/resources/the-staircase-problem-algebra-investigation/). The prompt is:

> A staircase with 1 step uses 1 block. A staircase with 2 steps uses 3 blocks. A staircase with 3 steps uses 6 blocks. How many blocks for 10 steps? For 100 steps? For *n* steps?

**Every student** can build the first few staircases and count. That's the entry point.

**Most students** will find the pattern in the numbers and predict the 10th staircase correctly. That's Level 3-4.

**Some students** will derive the formula T(n) = n(n+1)/2 and verify it. That's Level 5-6.

**A few students** will prove why it works — perhaps by showing that two staircases fit together to make a rectangle. That's Level 7-8.

Same prompt. Same classroom. Same 60 minutes. The differentiation happens naturally through how far each student pushes.

## Three Practical Techniques

### 1. The "Convince Me" Escalator

After students find a pattern, ask them to convince you it's correct. The levels of convincing map directly to Criterion B:

- "I checked it for 5 cases" → Level 3-4
- "I can explain why the formula makes sense" → Level 5-6
- "I can prove it works for ALL cases" → Level 7-8

Students self-select their level of justification. You don't need to assign groups.

### 2. Extension Questions, Not Extension Tasks

Instead of creating a separate "extension" worksheet, add one question to the original task:

> "What if the staircases went in two directions (a pyramid)? How would the formula change?"

This extends the investigation without creating a separate task. Students who reach it explore genuinely new territory. Students who don't reach it have still completed a full investigation.

### 3. Strategic Hint Distribution

Use the ["I'm Stuck" approach](/resources/the-staircase-problem-algebra-investigation/) — progressive hints that students access when they need them. Students who need all four hints complete the task at Level 3-4. Students who need zero hints are working at Level 5-6+.

The key insight: **hints are not failure.** They're scaffolding that allows every student to experience the full investigation instead of being stuck at Step 1.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

In my Grade 8 class, I give the same investigation to everyone. The [Teacher Moves](/assessment/) I use are:

- **Random groups** at vertical whiteboards (so every group is mixed)
- **One prompt** on the board — identical for everyone
- **Progressive hints** on cards at the front of the room — students walk up and take one when they're stuck
- **A gallery walk** at the end — every group sees every other group's work

The differentiation is invisible. No student knows they're in the "easy" group because there is no easy group. There's one task, and every student goes as far as they can.

## The Assessment Implication

This approach also simplifies assessment. You're marking one task against one rubric. The [Criterion B rubric card](/assessment/criterion-b/) shows exactly what each level looks like. A student who describes the pattern without justification is Level 3-4. A student who proves it algebraically is Level 7-8. Same task, same rubric, different outcomes.

The [annotated exemplars](/resources/criterion-b-exemplar-number-patterns/) show this in action — three students respond to the same prompt, and the quality of their reasoning determines their level, not the difficulty of their prompt.
