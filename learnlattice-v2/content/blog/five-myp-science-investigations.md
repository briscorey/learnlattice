I've lost count of the number of times I've planned a science investigation that required three days of setup, a chemical I didn't have, and a piece of equipment that was broken.

Then the lesson falls flat anyway because the investigation was more about following a procedure than actually thinking.

Here are five investigations that require almost nothing, work with multilingual learners, and produce genuine inquiry. I've taught all of them. They all work.

---

## 1. The Sinking Orange

**What you need:** An orange. A container of water. That's it.

**What happens:** An unpeeled orange floats. Peel it. It sinks. Students lose their minds.

**Why it works:** You just *removed* material — it weighs less — so why does it sink? The answer (the peel contains air pockets that reduce the orange's average density) requires students to think about density as a *relationship* between mass and volume, not just a property of the material.

**The investigation:** Give students five different objects and ask them to predict which will float. Then test. Then calculate densities. Then test in salt solutions of different concentrations. By the end, they can predict floating/sinking for any object-fluid combination.

**EAL note:** The demonstration requires zero English. Float. Sink. The visual is the instruction.

## 2. Why Is the Metal Seat Colder?

**What you need:** A metal spoon and a wooden spoon. An infrared thermometer (nice to have, not essential).

**What happens:** Both spoons have been sitting in the room overnight. Both are room temperature. But the metal one *feels* colder. Prove it with the thermometer — same temperature. Students are confused.

**Why it works:** The metal spoon isn't colder. It *conducts heat away from your hand faster*. Your brain interprets "losing heat quickly" as "touching something cold." This misconception is universal, deeply held, and can be resolved in 30 seconds with a thermometer.

**The investigation:** Place identical ice cubes on five different surfaces (metal, wood, plastic, ceramic, fabric). Race. Time them. Rank materials from best conductor to best insulator.

**EAL note:** Touch a spoon. Read a thermometer. Melt ice. All physical, all observable, all language-light.

## 3. Can You See Sound?

**What you need:** A small speaker, cling film, salt. A tone generator app (free on any phone).

**What happens:** Stretch cling film over the speaker. Sprinkle salt. Play a low tone. The salt jumps into patterns — Chladni figures. Change the frequency. The patterns change.

**Why it works:** Sound is invisible. Waves are abstract. But the salt patterns are concrete, visual, and immediately responsive to changes in frequency. Students can SEE the relationship between pitch and wavelength without a single equation.

**The investigation:** Test five different frequencies. Sketch the salt patterns. Count the nodes. Describe the relationship between frequency and pattern complexity.

**EAL note:** The demonstration is entirely visual. Sketch what you see. Count the lines. The patterns speak for themselves.

## 4. The Feather and the Hammer

**What you need:** A textbook. A sheet of paper. YouTube (for the Apollo 15 footage).

**What happens:** Drop a textbook and a flat sheet of paper. The book lands first. Now crumple the paper. Drop again. They land together. Show the Apollo 15 video: a feather and a hammer dropped on the Moon hit the surface at exactly the same time.

**Why it works:** Every student arrives believing "heavier things fall faster." They're both right (gravity does pull harder on heavier objects) and wrong (acceleration due to gravity is the same for all objects). Untangling this is one of the most satisfying conceptual journeys in middle school science.

**The investigation:** Drop objects of different mass but similar shape. Time them. Does mass affect fall time when air resistance is controlled?

**EAL note:** Drop things. Watch. The physics is visible. The crumpled-paper reveal is genuinely dramatic without any verbal explanation needed.

## 5. What Lives in Our Schoolyard?

**What you need:** String quadrats (or just mark out 1m squares). Magnifying glasses. Observation log sheets.

**What happens:** Students predict how many species live in their schoolyard. They usually guess 5-15. The actual number is typically 50-200+, counting plants, insects, fungi, and micro-organisms.

**Why it works:** Students walk past their schoolyard every day without noticing the ecosystem. The quadrat survey makes the invisible visible. Different micro-habitats (shade vs. sun, grass vs. concrete edge) support dramatically different communities. This is ecology they can literally step into.

**The investigation:** Each group surveys a different micro-habitat. Pool the data. Compare biodiversity across locations. Discuss what happens if one habitat is paved over.

**EAL note:** Observe. Sketch. Count. The fieldwork phase requires no reading or writing — just careful looking. The data table and graph come afterwards, and by then students have the context to understand what the numbers mean.

## The Common Thread

All five of these investigations share three properties:

**They start with a surprise.** The orange sinks. The spoons are the same temperature. The salt dances. The paper and book land together. The schoolyard is an ecosystem. In every case, the investigation exists to explain something the student has already seen and wants to understand.

**They require minimal equipment.** Not zero — you need an orange, you need some salt, you need to go outside. But nothing that costs more than a few dollars or requires a lab technician.

**They work before English does.** Every demonstration is visual and physical. Every data collection method involves observing, measuring, or counting. The scientific thinking happens in the student's first language. The English comes later, layered on top of understanding that already exists.

That last point matters enormously in international schools. If your investigation requires students to read two paragraphs of instructions before they can start, you've lost your EAL learners before the science begins.

Start with the thing they can see. Let the words follow.
