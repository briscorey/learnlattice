## What MYP Sciences Criterion C Assesses

Criterion C — Processing and Evaluating — is where scientific investigation produces meaning from data. Students collect raw data (from Criterion B's designed method), process it into forms that reveal patterns, interpret what those patterns mean scientifically, evaluate the reliability and validity of their method, and suggest improvements.

The strands are: presenting collected and transformed data, interpreting data and explaining results using scientific reasoning, evaluating the validity of a hypothesis based on the outcome, evaluating the validity of the method based on the outcome, and explaining improvements or extensions to the method.

This criterion bridges the experimental design of Criterion B and the real-world reflection of Criterion D. It is where students learn that data does not speak for itself — it must be processed, represented, interpreted, and critically evaluated.

## The Achievement Levels in Plain Language

**Levels 1-2 (Limited):** The student presents raw data in a basic table. Attempts a graph but it may be incorrectly constructed (wrong graph type, missing labels, inconsistent scale). States a conclusion that may not be supported by the data. Does not evaluate the method.

**Levels 3-4 (Adequate):** Data is presented in an organised table with headers and units. Graphs are mostly correct but may have minor errors. The conclusion states what happened and links it to the hypothesis. The student identifies that there were "some errors" but cannot specify what they were or how they affected results.

**Levels 5-6 (Substantial):** Data is processed correctly (means calculated, appropriate transformations applied). Graphs are well-constructed and the correct type is chosen. The conclusion interprets the data using scientific reasoning and evaluates the hypothesis. The student identifies specific sources of error and suggests concrete improvements.

**Levels 7-8 (Excellent):** Data processing is sophisticated (error bars, percentage uncertainty, appropriate significant figures). The student explains anomalous data points rather than ignoring them. The conclusion integrates scientific theory with experimental evidence. Evaluation of the method is specific, realistic, and explains *how* each improvement would increase reliability or validity. Extensions are scientifically justified.

### The Critical Jump: Identifying Errors vs. Evaluating Impact

At levels 3-4, a student writes: "There may have been some errors in our experiment." At levels 5-6: "The thermometer had an uncertainty of ±0.5°C, which could have affected our temperature readings." At levels 7-8: "The ±0.5°C uncertainty in temperature measurement represents a 2.5% error at 20°C but only 1.0% at 50°C. This means our lower temperature data points are proportionally less reliable, which may explain the greater scatter in Figure 1 below 25°C."

## Teaching Data Processing Skills

**Raw data tables** should include: a descriptive title, column headers with units, independent variable in the first column, dependent variable readings in subsequent columns, and repeated trials clearly labelled.

**Processed data** means calculating means from repeated trials, applying relevant transformations (rates, percentages, ratios), and determining uncertainty where appropriate. At levels 7-8, students should be able to calculate percentage error or use range/2 as a measure of uncertainty.

**Graph selection** is a key differentiator. Teach students to choose rather than default:
- Line graph: continuous independent variable, showing a trend
- Scatter plot: investigating correlation between two continuous variables
- Bar chart: categorical independent variable
- Histogram: frequency distribution of continuous data

A student who always draws a line graph regardless of data type is capped at levels 5-6. A student who explains *why* they chose a scatter plot over a line graph demonstrates level 7-8 communication about their processing choices.
