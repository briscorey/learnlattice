## The Problem Nobody Talks About

Here is the reality of teaching MYP mathematics at an international school: you walk into a room of twenty-four students and maybe six of them can follow a full English sentence at speed. Another eight can get the gist. The remaining ten are reading your face, your gestures, and the board — in that order.

If your opening move is a paragraph of verbal explanation, you have lost a third of the room before the lesson begins. Not because they cannot do mathematics. Because you chose to deliver mathematics through a channel they cannot access yet.

This is not a post about differentiation theory. It is about what I actually do in the first ten minutes of a lesson, in a real classroom, with students whose first languages include Mandarin, Korean, Japanese, Thai, and about eight others I cannot name but whose speakers I can recognise by how they squint at my handwriting.

## The Three Rules

After ten years in Australian public schools and three in international education, I have landed on three rules for opening a lesson with multilingual learners. They are not complicated. They are easy to forget.

**Rule 1: The problem comes before the language.**

If I put a problem on the board that requires reading, the students who read slowest will be the last to start thinking. That means the strongest mathematical thinkers in my room — the ones who can decompose a pattern or spot a relationship faster than I can — might be sitting idle while they decode my English.

So the problem goes up visually first. A shape. A number sequence. A photo. A diagram with one thing missing. Something that provokes the question before I have to articulate it.

One of my favourites: I project a rectangle on the screen and ask one question: *How many different ways can you cut this into four equal pieces?* That is eleven words. Every student in the room can read it or hear it translated in under thirty seconds. But the mathematics inside that question — the meaning of "equal," the difference between congruent and equal-area, the creativity of non-obvious partitions — will sustain forty minutes of investigation.

**Rule 2: The hands move before the mouths.**

I do not ask students to discuss first. I ask them to draw, cut, build, or write numbers. This is directly from Liljedahl's work on thinking classrooms, but the reason it matters even more in a multilingual setting is that physical action creates shared reference points that do not require fluent English.

When two students are standing at a vertical whiteboard and one draws a diagonal cut through a rectangle, the other student does not need the English word "diagonal" to understand what just happened. They can point, redraw, modify. The mathematics is happening in the shared visual space, not in the verbal space.

Random grouping matters here too. If I let students self-select, they group by language. That is comfortable, and sometimes comfort is what you want. But in the first ten minutes, I want productive confusion. I want a Korean speaker and a Mandarin speaker standing at the same whiteboard, forced to communicate through the mathematics itself.

**Rule 3: The teacher shuts up.**

This is the hardest one. I have a deep and abiding instinct to explain things. I went into teaching partly because I love the feeling of making something clear. But in the first ten minutes with EAL students, every word I say is a word they have to process, and processing costs cognitive load that should be going to the mathematics.

So I set the problem, I send them to the whiteboards, and I circulate silently. I look. I point at interesting work. I draw a question mark next to something I want them to reconsider. I put a checkmark next to something correct. I do not speak unless a group is stuck for more than three minutes, and even then I draw rather than explain.

The silence feels wrong the first few times you do it. Then you notice something: the room is louder than when you were talking. The students are arguing in three languages. They are pointing at each other's drawings. They are doing mathematics.

## A Specific Example: The Staircase Problem

Here is exactly how I opened a lesson last week.

I drew this on the board:

```
Step 1:  X

Step 2:  X
         X X

Step 3:  X
         X X
         X X X
```

Underneath I wrote: **How many blocks in Step 10? Step 100? Step n?**

No other instruction. No context. No learning objectives on the board (they are in my planning, not in their eyeline — that is deliberate).

I set a two-minute silent timer. Every student had to sketch Steps 4 and 5 individually before joining a group. This is critical: the silent start means that when they reach their group, every person has something to contribute. Nobody arrives empty-handed.

After two minutes, I randomised groups and sent them to whiteboards. Here is what happened:

**Group 1** (two Mandarin speakers, one Korean speaker) drew Steps 4 through 7 and started listing totals: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 28. They spotted the pattern — adding one more each time — within four minutes. By minute eight they had Step 100 through brute-force addition and were arguing about whether there was a shortcut.

**Group 2** (one Japanese speaker, one Thai speaker, one German speaker) went visual. They rearranged the staircase into a rectangle by mirroring it, noticed the rectangle was always $n \times (n+1)$, and divided by two. They had the formula $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ by minute seven — without me saying a single word to them.

**Group 3** got stuck on Step 5. They were counting wrong. I walked over and drew a careful Step 5 next to their attempt, pointed at the difference, and walked away. Forty seconds later they had self-corrected and were back in motion.

By the ten-minute mark, every group had at least Step 10 answered. Two groups had the general formula. One group had discovered Gauss's method independently. The lesson effectively taught itself, and my EAL students were the ones who got there first — because the problem was accessible through drawing, not through reading.

## What I Am Actually Doing in Those 10 Minutes

Let me be precise about the teacher moves, because "circulate silently" sounds simple but is not.

**Minutes 0–1: Launch.** Problem on the board. Read it aloud once, slowly. Point at each part of the visual as I read. No elaboration. Set the silent work timer.

**Minutes 1–3: Silent sketch.** I scan the room for students who are not drawing. I walk to them, point at the board, point at their paper, and give a small nod. That is usually enough. If someone genuinely does not understand the task, I draw Step 4 on their paper and point back at the board.

**Minutes 3–4: Grouping.** I use a random name generator projected on the screen. Students know the routine — when their name appears, they go to the numbered whiteboard. I hand each group one marker to start. (One marker forces turn-taking without me having to explain turn-taking.)

**Minutes 4–9: Circulation.** I move in a pattern: start with the group I am most worried about, then sweep through all groups, then return to any group that has stalled. My toolkit is: point, draw a question mark, draw a checkmark, draw an arrow connecting two things they have not connected yet. I speak only if a group has stopped working entirely.

**Minute 9–10: Gallery walk setup.** I ask everyone to put their marker down and step back. This is the transition to the next phase: structured sharing. But the first ten minutes are done. Every student has engaged with the mathematics. Every student has produced something. And I have a visual map of where every group is, which tells me exactly how to run the next twenty minutes.

## Why This Matters Beyond "Engagement"

I want to be clear about what this is not. This is not a "fun starter" or a "hook" in the superficial sense. The first ten minutes are the mathematical foundation of the entire lesson. The problem I choose for the opening is the problem that contains the concept I need students to construct.

When Group 2 discovered $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$, they did not need me to teach them the formula. They owned it. They built it. When I later formalise the notation and connect it to Gauss's famous story, I am annotating their discovery, not delivering new content. That is a fundamentally different power dynamic in the classroom, and it matters especially for students who have spent years in education systems where mathematics was something done to them, not by them.

For EAL students in particular, the first ten minutes send a message: *You do not need perfect English to do brilliant mathematics.* If that message lands — really lands, in the body and not just the ears — it can begin to repair mathematical identities that years of language-barrier exclusion have damaged.

## The Practical Takeaway

If you teach multilingual learners and you want to try this tomorrow, here is the minimum viable version:

Find a problem that is visual, low-floor, and requires no reading beyond one sentence. Put it on the board. Give students two minutes of silent individual work. Randomise groups. Send them to whiteboards. Do not speak for five minutes.

That is it. You will be uncomfortable. Your students will not be.

The first ten minutes are not about getting students ready to learn. They are the learning. Design them that way.
