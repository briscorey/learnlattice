After marking hundreds of Criterion B investigations across three schools, I've noticed that most students lose marks in the same five places. Not because they can't do the mathematics or science — but because nobody taught them the specific skills that Criterion B is actually assessing.

Here are the five mistakes, in order of how often I see them, with the specific fix for each.

## Mistake 1: Confusing Verification with Justification

This is the most common error and the most important one to fix. It's the difference between Level 4 and Level 6.

**What it looks like:** "I checked my formula for n = 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. It works every time. Therefore my formula is correct."

**Why it's wrong:** Checking five cases is not proof. There are infinitely many values of n. The formula might fail at n = 100 or n = 1,000.

**The fix:** Teach the sentence: *"My rule works because..."* followed by a structural explanation. Not "it works because I checked" — but "it works because the common difference is 3, and 3n increases by 3 each time n increases by 1."

See this in action: the [Number Patterns exemplar](/resources/criterion-b-exemplar-number-patterns/) shows three students responding to the same task. Student A says "I checked it." Student C proves it algebraically. The gap between them is this one skill.

## Mistake 2: Describing Instead of Analysing

**What it looks like:** "As temperature increases, the dissolving time decreases."

**Why it's limited:** This describes what happened but doesn't analyse it. It's accurate but shallow — like describing a movie by listing what happens in each scene.

**The fix:** Add the word *"because."* Force the student to connect the observation to a reason. "The dissolving time decreases because higher temperatures give particles more kinetic energy, increasing collision frequency."

## Mistake 3: No Systematic Data Collection

**What it looks like:** A student tests random values, gets some results, and tries to guess a formula.

**Why it's limited:** Without systematic data, pattern recognition is unreliable. If you test n = 1, 5, 12, and 37, it's nearly impossible to find the rule.

**The fix:** Before any investigation, teach the habit: *"Start with 1, 2, 3, 4, 5."* Consecutive values make the common difference visible. This is not about the mathematics — it's about the investigation strategy.

## Mistake 4: Ignoring Anomalous Data

**What it looks like:** Student has 5 data points that fit the pattern and 1 that doesn't. They ignore the outlier and claim perfect results.

**Why it's wrong:** Real scientific data has anomalies. Acknowledging them — and explaining possible causes — is a Level 5-6+ skill.

**The fix:** Teach students that anomalies are not embarrassments. They're evidence. "The value at 60°C was higher than expected. This may be due to inconsistent stirring, which I could control by using a magnetic stirrer."

See the [Bouncing Ball exemplar](/resources/criterion-b-exemplar-bouncing-ball/) — the Level 7 student calculates ratios for every data point and discusses the slight variation as measurement uncertainty.

## Mistake 5: No Extension or Reflection

**What it looks like:** Student answers every question correctly but stops. No "what if?" No connection to other contexts.

**Why it's limited:** The highest levels of Criterion B ask students to extend their findings — predicting beyond the data range, connecting to other mathematical or scientific ideas.

**The fix:** Always end an investigation with: "What question does this answer raise?" or "Where else might this pattern appear?" The student who asks the next question is operating at Level 7.
