I used to collect exit tickets, glance at them after school, feel vaguely informed, and then plan tomorrow's lesson the same way I would have planned it anyway. The exit tickets were theatre — I was performing formative assessment without actually being formative.

The problem was not the idea. The problem was that my exit tickets asked the wrong questions. They checked whether students could do the thing I just taught. Of course most of them could. They had just practised it for thirty minutes. What I actually needed to know was something more specific: *where will this break down tomorrow when the scaffolding is gone?*

Here are five formats I now rotate through. Each one takes students about three minutes to complete and takes me ninety seconds to sort into three piles: got it, almost, not yet. That sorting is the only analysis I do — and it is enough to change the first ten minutes of the next lesson.

## 1. The Upside-Down Example

I give students a completed solution and ask: **What question was this the answer to?**

For MYP maths, this might be an algebraic expression like $3(x + 4) = 27$ already solved to $x = 5$. Students have to reverse-engineer a word problem that fits. This tells me whether they understand the structure of the mathematics or just the procedure.

What I learn: students who can solve equations but cannot construct contexts for them are pattern-matching, not understanding. Tomorrow's lesson opens with a context-first problem, not a symbolic one.

## 2. The Deliberate Mistake

I show a worked example with one error buried in the middle and ask: **Find it, fix it, explain why it is wrong.**

I use this constantly in MYP science. A food web with an impossible energy flow. A graph with the wrong variable on the x-axis. A method section that fails to control a variable. The error has to be conceptual, not arithmetic — I want students thinking about the science, not hunting for a dropped negative sign.

What I learn: students who spot the error but cannot explain why it matters have surface-level understanding. Tomorrow I pair them with students who can articulate the reasoning and run a short structured peer-teaching activity before we move on.

## 3. The Confidence Meter

Students solve one problem and then circle a number from 1 to 4:

- **1** — I guessed or copied
- **2** — I think I got it but I'm not sure why
- **3** — I understand and could explain it to someone
- **4** — I could make up a harder version of this problem

The self-reported confidence matters as much as the answer. A correct answer with a confidence of 1 tells me the student got lucky. A wrong answer with a confidence of 3 tells me there is a misconception that feels right. Both need different interventions.

What I learn: the gap between confidence and accuracy is where misconceptions live. If half the class is confident and wrong, I am re-teaching tomorrow — and I know exactly what to target.

## 4. The Two-Sentence Summary

**Write two sentences: what did you learn today, and what is still unclear?**

This is the simplest format and the one I use most with EAL students. It works because it separates self-perceived understanding from self-perceived confusion. Most students can articulate one or the other. Together they give me signal.

I scaffold this for lower-proficiency students with a sentence starter: *Today I learned that... I am still confused about...*

What I learn: the "still confused" responses cluster around specific sticking points that I would not have predicted. Last week, eight students in Grade 7 wrote some version of "I don't understand why we use brackets." I had assumed brackets were understood. They were not. That changed the next day's opener.

## 5. The Sorting Task

I give students four or five items and ask them to sort into two categories — but I do not tell them the categories.

In science: five substances — students sort them into elements and compounds. In maths: five expressions — students sort them into linear and non-linear. The categories are the concept I just taught. If students can sort correctly without being told the rule, they have internalised it.

What I learn: sorting reveals whether students have built a mental model or are still relying on surface features. A student who sorts $y = x^2 + 1$ as linear because "it has an equals sign and letters" needs a fundamentally different intervention than one who sorts it correctly but cannot explain why.

## The Ninety-Second Sort

After collecting the tickets — and I mean paper, not digital; the physical act of sorting is faster than any app — I deal them into three piles:

**Got it:** These students open tomorrow with an extension task or a peer-teaching role.

**Almost:** These students get a targeted warm-up that addresses the specific gap. This is where the exit ticket pays off — I know the exact concept to target, not a vague sense that "some students struggled."

**Not yet:** These students get a reteach moment in the first five minutes, using a different representation than yesterday. If I taught it symbolically, I reteach it visually. If I taught it visually, I reteach it with manipulatives.

The whole process — collect, sort, plan the opener — takes less time than marking a single assignment. And it has more impact on learning than any summative assessment I have ever written.
