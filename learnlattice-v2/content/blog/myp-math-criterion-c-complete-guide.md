## What MYP Mathematics Criterion C Really Means

Criterion C. Communicating. is the criterion most often misunderstood as "presentation marks." It is not about neat handwriting or colour-coded graphs. At its core, Criterion C assesses whether a student can express mathematical thinking clearly enough that another person can follow, verify, and evaluate it.

The three strands at MYP Year 3+ are: using appropriate mathematical language (notation, symbols, terminology), using appropriate forms of mathematical representation (tables, graphs, diagrams, formulae), and communicating a complete and coherent mathematical line of reasoning. The word "appropriate" does the heavy lifting. at the highest levels, students do not just use correct formats, they *choose* the best format for the situation and explain why.

## The Achievement Levels in Plain Language

**Levels 1-2:** The student uses everyday language instead of mathematical terms. Diagrams are missing labels or units. Working may be correct but is disorganised and hard to follow.

**Levels 3-4:** The student uses some correct terminology and creates basic representations (tables, graphs), but these may lack titles, scales, or units. The line of reasoning has gaps that require the reader to fill in steps.

**Levels 5-6:** The student uses mathematical language consistently and correctly. Representations are complete (labelled axes, appropriate scales, titles, units). Working follows a logical sequence that a reader can follow from start to finish.

**Levels 7-8:** The student selects the most appropriate representation for the data and can explain why. Communication is precise and concise. Multiple representations are integrated into a coherent argument. The student acknowledges limitations of their representations.

### The Critical Jump: Choosing vs. Using

At levels 5-6, a student uses the graph format they were taught. At levels 7-8, a student explains why a scatter plot is more appropriate than a line graph for their data, or why a percentage is more meaningful than a raw number in their context. The shift is from *following communication conventions* to *making strategic communication choices*.

## Teaching Mathematical Communication to EAL Students

This is where Criterion C intersects powerfully with English language development. and where thoughtful teaching can simultaneously improve mathematical communication and English proficiency.

**Sentence frames** are the single most effective tool. Instead of asking students to "explain your reasoning," provide frames: "I chose [method] because..." or "This graph shows that as [variable] increases, [variable]..." The frame provides the English structure while requiring the student to supply the mathematical thinking.

**Mathematical vocabulary walls** with visual support help students access precise terminology. A word wall entry for "gradient" should include the word, a visual (a line on a grid), the formula, and a sentence frame: "The gradient of this line is ___, which means for every 1 unit increase in x, y increases by ___."

**Dual-language glossaries** are permitted by the IB and should be encouraged. A student who can explain a concept perfectly in Mandarin but lacks the English vocabulary has a language gap, not a mathematical one.
