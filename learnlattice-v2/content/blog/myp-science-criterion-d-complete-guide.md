## Why Criterion D Is Where Science Meets the Real World

Criterion D — Reflecting on the Impacts of Science — is the most distinctive and powerful of the four MYP science criteria. While Criteria A-C focus on understanding and practising science as a discipline, Criterion D asks students to step back and consider: What are the consequences of this scientific knowledge? Who benefits? Who is harmed? What should we do about it?

The strands are: explaining the ways in which science is applied and used to address a specific problem or issue, discussing and evaluating the various implications of using science and its application to solve a specific problem or issue, applying scientific language to communicate understanding, and documenting sources of information.

This is the criterion that connects most naturally to MYP Global Contexts, interdisciplinary learning, and the IB's mission to develop internationally minded learners who recognise that other people, with their differences, can also be right.

## The Achievement Levels in Plain Language

**Levels 1-2 (Limited):** The student states that science has impacts on society but cannot identify specific examples. May write "pollution is bad" without connecting to specific scientific processes or evidence. Sources are absent or incorrectly cited.

**Levels 3-4 (Adequate):** The student describes how a specific scientific application affects people or the environment. Identifies one or two implications (economic, environmental, ethical, social) but discusses them superficially. Uses some scientific language. Acknowledges sources but citations may be incomplete.

**Levels 5-6 (Substantial):** The student explains how science is applied to a specific problem, discusses multiple implications from different perspectives (economic AND environmental AND social), and uses scientific language accurately. Evaluates implications to reach a reasoned conclusion. Sources are correctly documented.

**Levels 7-8 (Excellent):** The student provides a detailed, evidence-based analysis of how science addresses a specific issue. Evaluates implications from multiple stakeholder perspectives, considering trade-offs and unintended consequences. Applies scientific language precisely. Proposes a justified course of action or reaches a nuanced conclusion that acknowledges complexity. Sources are comprehensively and accurately documented.

### The Critical Jump: Discussing vs. Evaluating

At levels 3-4, a student *discusses* implications: "Nuclear power reduces carbon emissions but produces radioactive waste." At levels 5-6, a student *evaluates*: "While nuclear power produces 70g CO₂/kWh compared to coal's 820g, the long-term storage of radioactive waste poses risks for thousands of years. The economic cost of decommissioning must also be considered." At levels 7-8, a student *synthesises and proposes*: "For a developing nation with high energy demand, nuclear power may represent the optimal balance of low carbon emissions and reliable baseload capacity, provided the regulatory infrastructure exists to manage waste safely. However, the high capital cost means renewable alternatives may be more appropriate where grid flexibility is less critical."

## Designing Criterion D Tasks Around Global Contexts

Criterion D tasks work best when anchored in genuine dilemmas — situations where the science is clear but the best course of action is not. The six MYP Global Contexts provide natural framing:

**Globalization and Sustainability:** Climate change policy, biodiversity conservation vs. economic development, water resource management, plastic pollution solutions.

**Fairness and Development:** Access to vaccines, genetic testing ethics, food security and GMOs, environmental justice (who bears the burden of pollution?).

**Scientific and Technical Innovation:** AI in medicine, gene editing (CRISPR), space exploration priorities, nuclear energy decisions.

**Identities and Relationships:** Mental health and neuroscience, genetic identity, body image and nutrition science.

**Orientation in Space and Time:** Evolution and cultural beliefs, geological time and resource depletion, astronomical discoveries and worldview.

**Personal and Cultural Expression:** Biomimicry in design, science and art intersections, traditional ecological knowledge.

## Common Criterion D Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Making Criterion D an essay about opinions.** Students must base their arguments on scientific evidence. "I think nuclear power is bad" is not Criterion D. "The evidence suggests nuclear power produces significantly less atmospheric carbon than fossil fuels, but the risk-benefit analysis depends on geological stability, regulatory capacity, and time horizon" is Criterion D.

**Mistake 2: Only considering one perspective.** Levels 7-8 require evaluation from multiple stakeholder perspectives. Build this into the task: "Consider the perspective of the local community, the national government, the energy company, and future generations."

**Mistake 3: Ignoring source documentation.** The IB explicitly includes "documenting sources" in the Criterion D descriptors. Teach proper citation practice early and consistently — it is both a Criterion D requirement and an ATL skill (research).
