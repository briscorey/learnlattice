I didn't go looking for any of these people. Each one found me at a moment when something in my teaching wasn't working — when I could feel a gap between what I was doing and what my students needed. I'd try something different, and only later discover that somebody had already researched it, named it, and explained why it works.

This is what I've learned from six thinkers who changed how I teach. Not their full theories — just the specific ideas that survived contact with actual thirteen-year-olds in actual classrooms.

## Peter Liljedahl: "Get them standing up."

I used to start class like every other teacher: students sitting in their assigned seats, me at the front explaining. Some students were engaged. Most were waiting. A few had checked out before I finished my first sentence.

Then I read about Peter Liljedahl's work on thinking classrooms, and one idea grabbed me: put students in random groups and send them to vertical whiteboards around the room.

I tried it on a Tuesday morning with Grade 8 algebra. I literally moved the desks to the walls, taped chart paper everywhere, and said: "Find a partner. Go to a board. Here's the problem."

The effect was immediate. Students who normally sat in the back and avoided eye contact were suddenly standing, marker in hand, arguing about whether the pattern was linear or quadratic. Students who usually dominated discussions were forced to share the pen with someone they'd never worked with. The room was loud. It looked chaotic. Every single student was thinking.

Liljedahl's research explains why this works. He studied hundreds of classrooms across years and found that when students are seated, they tend to mimic rather than think — they wait for the confident kid to start, then copy. Vertical surfaces change the dynamic because the work is visible, erasable, and shared. Standing creates a physical urgency. Random grouping prevents students from settling into familiar roles.

**What stuck in my practice:** I now start almost every investigation with random vertical grouping. It takes 90 seconds to set up and it transforms who does the thinking. The specific move: random groups of three, one marker per group, problem on the board, no explanation from me. Just go.

**Where you'll see this on LearnLattice:** The [warm-up connectors](/resources/the-staircase-problem-algebra-investigation/) on every flagship investigation are designed to be projected and solved at whiteboards. They're intentionally short (5 minutes) and slightly too easy — because the goal isn't to challenge students, it's to get everyone standing and thinking before the real investigation begins.

## Jo Boaler: "The speed trap is a lie."

I had a student — I'll call her Mei — who could not do mental arithmetic quickly. In timed tests she'd freeze. She was convinced she was bad at maths. Her parents were convinced. By Grade 7, she'd stopped trying.

Then I gave her an investigation about tessellations. No time pressure. No single right answer. She had to figure out which shapes tile a plane and prove why. Over two lessons, Mei produced the most elegant proof in the class. She drew every possibility, systematically eliminated the ones that left gaps, and wrote a paragraph explaining her reasoning that was clearer than anything her "fast" peers produced.

Mei wasn't bad at maths. She was bad at performing maths under artificial time pressure — and the education system had mistaken speed for understanding.

Jo Boaler's research at Stanford made me understand what I was seeing. She studied how the cultural emphasis on speed in mathematics classrooms actively damages students who think deeply but slowly. More importantly, she showed that mathematical ability is not fixed. The brain forms new neural connections when we struggle with problems — but only if we're given time to struggle, and only if we believe the struggle is productive.

**What stuck in my practice:** I removed timed tests from my classroom entirely. I stopped saying "quick" and "easy" when introducing problems. When a student says "I'm slow," I say: "You're thorough. That's better." And I redesigned my investigations to have entry points that every student can access, with extensions that have no ceiling.

**Where you'll see this on LearnLattice:** Every investigation has [differentiated tasks](/resources/the-million-dollar-question-number-sense/) at three levels, but they're not labelled "easy/medium/hard." They're labelled Approaching, Meeting, and Exceeding — because the goal is growth, not ranking. The [Revision Mode](/revision/) uses self-assessment ("I knew it" vs "Didn't know") rather than timed scores.

## Dylan Wiliam: "The question you ask determines the thinking you get."

For years, I asked: "Does everyone understand?" And students nodded. And then half of them got the test wrong.

Dylan Wiliam's work on formative assessment showed me that the problem wasn't my students — it was my questions. A yes/no question gets a yes/no answer. It tells you nothing about understanding. The student who nods might mean "I understood your words" or "I don't want to look stupid" or "I want you to move on."

Wiliam's core insight is that assessment should happen during the learning, not after it — and that the purpose of a question isn't to check understanding, it's to create understanding. A well-designed question forces students to think in a specific way. A badly designed question lets them avoid thinking entirely.

The technique that changed my teaching most was the "hinge question" — a question asked at the decision point of a lesson. If students get it right, you move forward. If they don't, you know exactly what to reteach. Not "do you understand percentages?" but "A shirt costs $80. It's 25% off. You have $65. Can you afford it? Show your reasoning."

**What stuck in my practice:** I now plan my lessons backward from the hinge question. I decide what I need students to understand by minute 30, then design a question that will reveal whether they do. If more than a third get it wrong, I stop and reteach. This single habit has improved my teaching more than any other.

They're designed to be shown at minute 35, answered on mini-whiteboards, and used to make a real-time decision about what to do next. ## Dan Meyer: "Be less helpful."

I used to over-scaffold everything. Every investigation had a worksheet with numbered steps, guided questions, and sentence frames. Students would follow the steps, fill in the blanks, and produce work that looked like understanding but was actually compliance.

Dan Meyer's work on patient problem-solving showed me a different approach: give students a compelling question and get out of the way. His method for lesson design is deceptively simple. Show something interesting that raises a question. Let students notice and wonder. Slowly reveal information as they ask for it. Let them solve it using whatever tools they choose.

The key insight is that the moment you give a student a formula, you've stolen their chance to discover it. The moment you give them a method, you've replaced their thinking with yours. Real mathematical thinking happens in the gap between "I don't know how to do this" and "Oh wait, what if I try..."

The hardest part for me was the silence. When a student says "I'm stuck," every instinct screams "help them." Meyer's approach says: wait. Ask "What do you know so far?" Ask "What have you tried?" Ask "What would make this easier?" But do not give the answer. Let them sit in productive struggle.

**What stuck in my practice:** I deleted half the scaffolding from my worksheets. I now start investigations with a question, not instructions. The [Staircase Problem](/resources/the-staircase-problem-algebra-investigation/), for instance, opens with "How many blocks to build a staircase 100 steps high?" — not "Complete the table below and find the formula." Same mathematical content. Completely different thinking.

**Where you'll see this on LearnLattice:** Every investigation starts with a [hook question](/resources/the-monty-hall-problem/) — one provocative line designed to create what Meyer calls perplexity. "Fire grows, eats, and responds to stimulation. Is fire alive?" Students have an opinion before they've learned anything. Now they're motivated to investigate, because they want to know if they're right.

## Seymour Papert: "You understand something best when you build it."

I used to teach density by explaining the formula, doing worked examples, and then giving practice problems. Students could calculate density. They could not explain it. They could plug numbers into d = m/v but had no intuition for why an orange floats and a grape sinks.

Seymour Papert was a mathematician at MIT who spent decades studying how children learn. His core idea was constructionism: people learn by making things. Not by listening to explanations, not by reading textbooks, but by building, testing, breaking, and rebuilding. The knowledge lives in the making.

When I redesigned the [density investigation](/resources/density-and-floating-investigation/), I threw away the formula-first approach. Instead, students get 10 objects and a tub of water. They predict which will float, test their predictions, measure mass and volume, and try to find the rule themselves. The formula emerges from their data, not from my whiteboard.

The difference in understanding is stark. Students who discovered the rule themselves can predict whether a new object will float without calculating. They've built an intuition that no amount of formula practice can create.

**What stuck in my practice:** Wherever possible, I let students build the concept before I name it. They discover the pattern, then I give it a label. They find the relationship, then I show the formula. The label and the formula become a shorthand for something they already understand — not a substitute for understanding.

**Where you'll see this on LearnLattice:** The [16 interactive games](/games/) are all built on this principle. The [Circuit Challenge](/resources/circuit-challenge-game/) doesn't teach Ohm's law and then let you build circuits. You build circuits and discover Ohm's law. The [Ecosystem Architect](/resources/ecosystem-architect-game/) doesn't explain trophic cascades. You remove a species and watch everything collapse.

## Jerome Bruner: "Concrete first. Always."

My final thinker isn't the most famous, but his idea might be the most practical. Jerome Bruner's enactive-iconic-symbolic framework (often simplified to "concrete-pictorial-abstract" or CPA) says that every concept should be introduced through physical manipulation before pictures, and through pictures before symbols.

This sounds obvious for young children — of course five-year-olds need blocks before numbers. But Bruner's insight is that CPA applies at every age, for every concept. Teenagers learning algebra benefit from physical models. Adults learning calculus benefit from visual representations. The concrete grounds the abstract, no matter how sophisticated the thinker.

I learned this the hard way. I once taught algebraic expansion — the idea that a(b + c) = ab + ac — by writing it on the board and doing examples. Half the class couldn't do it a week later. The next year, I gave them rectangles made from card. "This rectangle is a by (b + c). Cut it into two pieces. What are the areas of each piece?" Every student in the room understood the distributive law, because they could hold it in their hands.

**What stuck in my practice:** Before I teach any abstract concept, I ask: "What could students touch, move, or draw that would make this concrete?" If I can't answer that question, I don't teach the concept that day — I go and design the concrete first.

## What I'm still learning

None of these ideas work in isolation. Liljedahl's thinking classrooms need Wiliam's hinge questions to check if the thinking is productive. Boaler's growth mindset needs Meyer's patient problem-solving to give students something worth struggling with. Papert's constructionism needs Bruner's CPA sequence to ensure the making leads to understanding.

The real skill in teaching isn't knowing these theories. It's knowing which one applies at 10:47am on a Wednesday when half the class is confused and the other half is bored. That judgment — that sense of what this room needs right now — is something no book can teach. You build it lesson by lesson, year by year, by trying things that don't work and paying attention to why.

If you're a teacher reading this: pick one idea from one thinker and try it tomorrow. Not all six at once. One. See what happens. Pay attention. Then try another.

That's how practice changes. Not in leaps, but in experiments.
