## The Book That Changed My Classroom

Peter Liljedahl's *Building Thinking Classrooms in Mathematics* landed on my desk three years into international teaching. I had already spent a decade in Australian public schools. I thought I knew how to run a classroom. Then I read the research, tried the first three practices the following Monday, and watched my Grade 7 class do more genuine mathematical thinking in forty minutes than I had seen in the previous month.

That is not hyperbole. The shift was that stark.

But here is what the book does not tell you: how to implement these practices in a classroom where half your students are working in their second or third language. Liljedahl's research was conducted primarily in North American public school classrooms with English-speaking students. The practices are sound — the research is rigorous and the framework is brilliant — but the context of an international school introduces variables that require adaptation.

After two years of implementing BTC practices at Nanjing International School with MYP mathematics students, here is what I have learned about what transfers directly, what needs adjustment, and which three practices had the most impact.

## What Transfers Directly

### Visibly Random Groups

Liljedahl's research shows that when you randomise groups frequently — every class — students become agreeable to working with anyone within two to three weeks. Social barriers dissolve. Knowledge mobility increases.

This works identically in an international school. In fact, I would argue it works *better*, because random grouping breaks through the language-based social clusters that naturally form when students share a first language. When a Mandarin-speaking student, a Korean-speaking student, and a Thai-speaking student are grouped together, English becomes the necessary bridge — and mathematics becomes the shared language underneath.

I use playing cards to randomise. It takes twelve seconds. I do it every single class.

### Vertical Non-Permanent Surfaces (VNPSs)

Standing at whiteboards in groups of three is the single most transformative practice in the entire BTC framework. The research is unambiguous: students who work on vertical non-permanent surfaces think longer, start faster, and take more risks than students who work at desks on paper.

In my MYP classroom, VNPSs have an additional benefit that Liljedahl does not mention: they make mathematical thinking *visible to the teacher across languages*. When a student writes their reasoning on a whiteboard, I can assess their mathematical understanding even if I cannot follow their verbal explanation in their first language. The board is the universal translator.

I have eighteen whiteboard sections in my room. I bought them with my own money. Best investment I have ever made in teaching.

### Thinking Tasks Before Curricular Tasks

Liljedahl recommends starting the year — and often starting each class — with non-curricular thinking tasks. Problems that require thinking but do not require specific mathematical content knowledge. The purpose is to establish a *culture of thinking* before you introduce curriculum.

This is essential for MYP classrooms. When I start with a thinking task, I am telling my students: *the expectation in this room is that you think, not that you already know the answer.* For students who have come from educational systems that prioritise memorisation and correct answers, this is a radical shift. The thinking tasks give them permission to struggle — and they discover that struggle is productive, not shameful.

Some of my favourite thinking tasks for multilingual learners:
- **The locker problem**: 1,000 lockers, 1,000 students, each student toggles multiples. Which lockers are open? (Zero language required — demonstrate with 10 lockers physically.)
- **Bridge crossing**: Four people, one torch, a bridge that holds two. Cross in 17 minutes. (Draw it. No words needed.)
- **Handshake problem**: Everyone in the room shakes hands once. How many handshakes? (Stand up and do it.)

Each of these requires mathematical reasoning and produces rich discussion. None requires reading a paragraph of English instructions.

## What Needs Adapting

### Answer Only Keep-Thinking Questions

Liljedahl's research identifies three types of student questions: proximity questions (asked because the teacher is nearby), stop-thinking questions ("is this right?"), and keep-thinking questions (asked so the student can continue working). He recommends answering *only* keep-thinking questions.

In a multilingual classroom, the distinction between these categories becomes complicated. A student asking "is this right?" in their second language might actually be asking "am I understanding the problem correctly?" — which is a keep-thinking question wrapped in stop-thinking language. Their limited English forces them into simpler question structures.

My adaptation: I answer the *intent* behind the question, not the literal question. If I suspect a student is checking their understanding rather than seeking validation, I respond with a question that helps them verify their own thinking: "What would happen if you tried it with a different number?" This keeps them thinking without penalising them for limited English.

### Oral Instructions

Liljedahl recommends giving tasks orally rather than in written form. In a typical classroom, this prevents students from reading ahead and short-circuiting the thinking process.

In a multilingual classroom, purely oral instructions create an immediate equity problem. Students with stronger English comprehension hear the task clearly. Students with weaker comprehension miss critical information. I have watched students stand at whiteboards doing nothing — not because they could not do the mathematics, but because they did not catch the task.

My adaptation: **oral-first, visual-second**. I give the task verbally with deliberate pacing and repetition. Then I write the core question on the board — in as few words as possible. Often this is a single sentence or even just a number and a question mark. The oral delivery carries the energy and context. The written version carries the specifics.

For [The Million Dollar Question](/resources/the-million-dollar-question-number-sense/), the board reads: **$1,000,000. One dollar per second. How long?** Six words. Every student can access this regardless of English proficiency.

### Levelling to the Bottom

Liljedahl recommends that when every group has reached a minimum threshold, the teacher pulls the class together to consolidate learning. This is "levelling to the bottom."

In a multilingual classroom, the consolidation phase is where learning is most at risk of being lost — because consolidation is the most language-heavy part of the lesson. If I run a ten-minute whole-class discussion in English, I have lost the students who accessed the mathematics through visual and spatial reasoning at the whiteboards.

My adaptation: **level with the boards, not with words.** Instead of a verbal consolidation, I ask two or three groups to walk the class through their whiteboard. The board does the heavy lifting. I annotate their work with key vocabulary as we go — writing the mathematical terms *next to* the visual reasoning they represent. This connects new language to meaning that already exists in the student's understanding.

## The Three Practices That Changed My Teaching Most

### 1. Defronting the Classroom

Liljedahl's research shows that a "defronted" classroom — one with no designated front — produces more collaborative learning than a traditional classroom. When desks face every direction and the teacher addresses the class from different locations, the implicit message is: *learning can happen anywhere in this room.*

I removed the rows. I scattered the tables. I teach from every corner. The first week felt chaotic. By the third week, I could feel the difference. Students orient toward each other now, not toward me. Mathematical arguments happen between students, not between students and teacher.

### 2. One Marker Per Group

This is deceptively simple. Each group of three gets one whiteboard marker. The person holding the marker can only write *other people's ideas*, not their own.

In a multilingual classroom, this practice is transformative. It forces verbal contribution from every student, because the marker-holder cannot write until someone tells them what to write. Students who might stay silent in a whole-class discussion find their voice in a group of three — and the pressure to contribute is distributed equally regardless of English proficiency.

### 3. Thin-Slicing Tasks

Rather than giving one large task, Liljedahl recommends presenting a sequence of increasingly difficult problems — "thin slices" that build from accessible to challenging. Students experience flow because the difficulty matches their developing ability.

This aligns perfectly with MYP Criterion B investigations. An investigation that starts with specific cases (accessible), moves to pattern recognition (moderate), and ends with generalisation and proof (challenging) is inherently thin-sliced. The structure I describe in [How to Write a Criterion B Investigation](/blog/how-to-write-a-criterion-b-investigation/) is essentially a thin-slicing framework for MYP.

## Try It Monday

If you have never tried BTC practices, here is where to start — in this order:

1. **Random groups.** Tomorrow. Use playing cards. Do not explain why. Just do it.
2. **Whiteboards.** Buy six from a hardware store this weekend. Prop them against walls. Send groups to them.
3. **A thinking task.** Use one of the investigations from this site — [The Fairness Problem](/resources/the-fairness-problem-fractions-inquiry/) works brilliantly as a first BTC task because the entry point is a question everyone has an opinion about.

You will feel uncomfortable. Your students will look confused for about seven minutes. Then the thinking will start. And once it starts, you will not want to go back.
