## Why This Matters

The Statement of Inquiry was the MYP's most divisive planning element. In principle, it was meant to capture the conceptual understanding students would develop across a unit. In practice, it often became a compliance exercise — teachers reverse-engineering a grammatically correct sentence from a prescribed key concept, a related concept, and a global context.

The result was sentences like: "The relationship between form and function determines how systems process change through logical representations." Technically valid. Pedagogically useless. No student has ever been motivated by that sentence.

The Enhanced MYP fixes this by allowing teachers to express the central inquiry as a **question** or a **statement** — and removes the requirement to reference specific concepts in the wording. The concepts still drive the unit; they just no longer need to be grammatically embedded in a single sentence.

Here are 20 real conversions — 10 for mathematics, 10 for sciences.

## Mathematics: 10 Before-and-After Examples

### 1. Grade 6 — Number Sense and Place Value

**Before (SOI):** "The form of number systems reflects logical relationships that allow efficient representation of quantity."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "Why does every culture in history invent a number system — and why do they all look different?"

*Why this is better:* The question is genuinely interesting. A 12-year-old would want to know the answer. It leads to the same conceptual understanding about number systems and representation, but through curiosity rather than compliance.

### 2. Grade 7 — Algebra and Equations

**Before (SOI):** "Equivalence can be maintained through logical operations that preserve relationships between quantities."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "An equation is a balance. If you do the same thing to both sides, it stays balanced. But what happens when you do something different — and why does the balance break?"

### 3. Grade 7 — Ratio and Proportion

**Before (SOI):** "Proportional relationships in different forms allow us to understand and predict real-world situations."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "A recipe for 4 people uses 200g of flour. You are cooking for 7. Can you always just multiply — or does the mathematics of scaling have limits?"

### 4. Grade 8 — Linear Functions and Graphs

**Before (SOI):** "Relationships between variables can be represented through different forms to reveal patterns that predict change."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "If something changes at a constant rate, can you always predict the future? When does the straight-line model stop working?"

### 5. Grade 8 — Statistics and Probability

**Before (SOI):** "Representation and analysis of data reveals patterns that inform fair and evidence-based decisions."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "Two politicians use the same data to argue opposite conclusions. How is that possible — and how do you know who is telling the truth?"

### 6. Grade 8 — Geometry and Measurement

**Before (SOI):** "The form of three-dimensional objects can be described through spatial relationships and measurement."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "A Pringles tube uses less material per chip than a rectangular box. So why is everything sold in boxes?"

### 7. Grade 9 — Quadratics

**Before (SOI):** "Change in relationships can be modelled through different representations to make predictions about non-linear patterns."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "When you throw a ball, its path curves. The same curve appears in satellite dishes, bridge arches, and profit calculations. What makes this shape so universal?"

### 8. Grade 7 — Probability

**Before (SOI):** "Logical analysis of chance events reveals patterns in randomness that challenge personal and cultural assumptions."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "You flip a coin five times and get five heads. Is the next flip more likely to be tails? Most people think yes. The mathematics says no. Who is right — and why does our intuition fail?"

### 9. Grade 6 — Fractions

**Before (SOI):** "The form of fractions represents relationships between parts and wholes that determine equivalence and quantity."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "You get ¾ of a pizza. Your friend gets ⁵⁄₆ of a smaller pizza. Who got more? Can fractions ever lie about fairness?"

### 10. Grade 9 — Sequences and Series

**Before (SOI):** "Patterns in sequences can be generalised through logical relationships to make predictions about infinite cases."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "A sequence starts 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13... This pattern appears in sunflower seeds, pine cones, and galaxy arms. Is nature doing algebra — or is the pattern a coincidence?"

## Sciences: 10 Before-and-After Examples

### 11. Grade 6 — States of Matter

**Before (SOI):** "The form and function of matter is determined by the interaction of particles within systems."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "Ice, water, and steam are all the same substance. What is actually changing — and what stays the same?"

### 12. Grade 7 — Ecology and Ecosystems

**Before (SOI):** "The balance within systems depends on relationships and interactions that can be disrupted by change."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "In 1995, wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone. The rivers changed shape. How can 31 wolves reshape an entire landscape?"

### 13. Grade 7 — Forces and Motion

**Before (SOI):** "The interaction of forces creates patterns of movement that can be described through relationships."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "A bowling ball and a feather are dropped from the same height. On the Moon, they hit the ground at the same time. On Earth, they do not. What is the difference — and what does it reveal about forces?"

### 14. Grade 8 — Chemical Reactions

**Before (SOI):** "Transformation of matter through interaction reveals patterns that can be predicted and controlled."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "You cannot un-bake a cake. But you can un-melt ice. What makes some changes reversible and others permanent?"

### 15. Grade 8 — Human Body Systems

**Before (SOI):** "The function of body systems depends on the interaction and balance of components working as an integrated whole."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "Your heart beats 100,000 times a day without you thinking about it. What happens when one part of the system fails — and how does the body compensate?"

### 16. Grade 9 — Genetics and Inheritance

**Before (SOI):** "Patterns of inheritance reveal relationships between form and function that determine biological identity."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "You have your mother's eyes and your father's nose. But you are not identical to either parent. What determines which traits you inherit — and is it truly random?"

### 17. Grade 7 — Earth Science

**Before (SOI):** "Evidence of change over time reveals patterns in Earth systems that help us understand our environment."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "The Himalayas grow 1 cm taller every year. How do we know — and what does this tell us about forces we cannot see?"

### 18. Grade 8 — Electricity

**Before (SOI):** "The function of electrical systems depends on the interaction of components that control the flow of energy."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "You flip a switch and a light turns on instantly. But the electrons in the wire move at about 0.01 cm per second. If the electrons are barely moving, what is actually travelling at the speed of light?"

### 19. Grade 9 — Waves and Sound

**Before (SOI):** "The form and behaviour of waves determines how energy is transferred through systems."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "You can hear someone around a corner but you cannot see them. Sound bends around obstacles; light does not. Why — and what does this tell us about the nature of waves?"

### 20. Grade 6 — Space Science

**Before (SOI):** "Models of celestial systems reveal relationships of scale and movement that challenge our understanding of position."

**After (Inquiry Question):** "If Earth were the size of a marble, how far away would the nearest star be? And if space is mostly empty, why does it matter to us?"

## Three Principles for Writing Good Inquiry Questions

**1. The question should be genuinely interesting.** Test it on a student. If they shrug, rewrite it. If they argue, you have a winner.

**2. The question should not have a simple factual answer.** "What is the formula for the area of a circle?" is a recall question, not an inquiry question. "Why is π the same for every circle in the universe?" is an inquiry.

**3. The question should lead to the conceptual understanding you want.** The concepts no longer need to appear in the question, but the question must lead students to grapple with those concepts through investigation.
