## Why Criterion D Is the Most Important — and Most Neglected — Criterion

Criterion D — Applying Mathematics in Real-Life Contexts — answers the question every mathematics student has ever asked: "When will I use this?" But it does so with rigour. At the highest levels, students do not just *apply* mathematics to a real situation — they evaluate whether the mathematics is the right tool, discuss the limitations of their model, and suggest improvements.

This makes Criterion D the most authentically mathematical of all four criteria. Real mathematics always involves modelling: simplifying a complex situation, applying a mathematical tool, interpreting the result in context, and critically evaluating the model's assumptions. Criterion D is where MYP mathematics most closely mirrors what working mathematicians and scientists actually do.

Yet it is consistently the criterion with the fewest quality resources available. Most textbooks offer "word problems" that are merely calculation exercises with a story wrapper. Genuine Criterion D tasks require students to engage with messy, real-world data where there is no single "correct answer" — only more or less reasonable interpretations.

## The Achievement Levels in Plain Language

**Levels 1-2:** The student identifies a real-world context but cannot connect it to the mathematics. May state "this is useful in real life" without explanation. Applies a formula but cannot interpret what the answer means.

**Levels 3-4:** The student identifies relevant mathematical elements in a real situation and applies a method correctly. The answer is stated but not fully interpreted — the student calculates 2.3 g/cm³ but does not discuss what this means for whether the object floats.

**Levels 5-6:** The student selects appropriate mathematics, applies it correctly, interprets the result in context, and discusses whether the answer is reasonable. They consider practical constraints.

**Levels 7-8:** The student justifies the degree of accuracy, discusses limitations of the mathematical model, suggests improvements, and reflects on whether the mathematical answer is the "right" answer in practice.

### The Critical Jump: Application vs. Evaluation

The difference between 5-6 and 7-8 is the difference between a student who *uses* mathematics to solve a real problem and a student who *evaluates* whether mathematics solved it well. A level 5-6 student calculates the optimal price for a school fundraiser. A level 7-8 student calculates the optimal price, then discusses why the mathematical model might not work in practice (elastic demand, competitor pricing, weather affecting attendance) and what additional data would improve the model.

## Designing Authentic Criterion D Tasks

The word "authentic" is doing critical work here. A task is authentic when the real-world context genuinely affects the mathematics, not when a real-world label is attached to a textbook problem.

**Test for authenticity:** Can you remove the real-world context and the mathematical task remains identical? If yes, the context is a wrapper, not authentic. "A farmer has a rectangular field of 120m²..." is a wrapper — the student solves a rectangle problem regardless. "Using the school's actual electricity bills from the past 12 months, determine whether installing solar panels would save money over 10 years" is authentic — the context generates the data, constraints, and evaluation criteria.

**Three design principles for Criterion D tasks:**

1. **Use real data.** School electricity bills, local weather data, census statistics, sports records, food nutrition labels. Real data is messy, which forces students to make decisions about rounding, outliers, and accuracy.

2. **Require interpretation, not just calculation.** Every Criterion D task should end with "What does your answer mean?" and "Is your answer reasonable?" and "What would you do differently?"

3. **Build in limitations.** The best tasks have no perfect answer. This is where students earn levels 7-8: by articulating what the mathematics captures and what it misses.

## Global Contexts and Criterion D

Criterion D tasks naturally connect to MYP Global Contexts. The strongest pairings:

- **Globalization and Sustainability:** Carbon footprint calculations, resource consumption modelling, population growth projections
- **Fairness and Development:** Statistical analysis of inequality (Gini coefficient, wealth distribution), access to resources
- **Scientific and Technical Innovation:** Engineering optimisation, efficiency calculations, cost-benefit analysis
- **Identities and Relationships:** Health metrics (BMI limitations), social media usage patterns, survey design and bias
