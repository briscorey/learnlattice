The Criterion B descriptors are perfectly clear — until you try to use them.

"Describe patterns as general rules consistent with findings" sounds straightforward. Then a student writes "the pattern goes up by 2 each time" and you stare at the rubric wondering: is that a general rule or just a description? Is it level 4 or level 6?

I've been there. Every MYP teacher has been there. The descriptors tell you what each level should look like in abstract language. But they don't show you what it looks like in a student's actual handwriting on an actual assessment.

So I made four assessment tasks with annotated student responses at three levels each. Here's what I learned building them — and what I think is the single most important distinction in Criterion B.

---

## The one distinction that matters

The difference between level 5-6 and level 7-8 in Criterion B comes down to one word: **why**.

A level 5-6 response says: "Here is the pattern. Here is the rule. Here is evidence that the rule works."

A level 7-8 response says: "Here is the pattern. Here is the rule. Here is **why** the rule must be true."

That's the gap between **verification** and **justification**. Verification shows that your rule produces correct answers for tested cases. Justification explains the structural reason your rule works for ALL cases — including ones you haven't tested.

---

## What this looks like in practice

Take a simple pattern investigation: L-shaped figures made from square tiles, growing by 2 tiles each step.

**Level 3-4 student:** "It goes up by 2 each time." (This describes the recursive pattern but doesn't generalise.)

**Level 5-6 student:** "$T = 2n - 1$. I checked: for figure 3, $2(3) - 1 = 5$. Correct." (This generalises and verifies.)

**Level 7-8 student:** "$T = 2n - 1$ because each L-shape has one corner tile plus two arms of length $n - 1$. So $T = 1 + 2(n - 1) = 2n - 1$. This structure holds for any figure number." (This justifies — the algebra is connected to the geometry.)

The 7-8 student doesn't just show the rule works. They show why it MUST work. They connect the formula to the physical structure of the pattern. This is the thinking we want, and it's the thinking most students never attempt because nobody explicitly teaches the difference between checking and proving.

---

## How I use the annotated responses in class

I don't just give these to students. I use them as a calibration activity:

1. After students complete a Criterion B task, I show three anonymised responses (one from each level band) on the projector
2. Students rank them without seeing the levels
3. We discuss: what makes one response stronger than another?
4. I reveal the levels and map the discussion to the Criterion B strands

The conversation that follows is more valuable than any rubric explanation. Students see — concretely, in real writing — what separates a level 4 from a level 7. And they start to understand that the gap isn't about being smarter. It's about doing one more thing: explaining why.

## One final thought

The reason level 7-8 is rare isn't that students can't do it. It's that we rarely teach them what justification looks like. We say "justify your answer" without showing them an example of what a justification IS versus what a verification IS.

These annotated responses exist so that distinction becomes visible. Show them to your students. Better yet, show them to your department. The conversation about what counts as evidence, what counts as generalisation, and what counts as justification is the conversation that raises the ceiling for everyone.
