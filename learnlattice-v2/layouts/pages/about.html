{{/* ── THE MOMENT ── */}}

I drew a staircase on the board. One step: 1 block. Two steps: 3 blocks. Three steps: 6.

I wrote: "How many blocks for 100 steps?"

Then I stopped talking.

For two full minutes, every student in the room was thinking. Not because I told them to — because the question wouldn't let them stop. One boy who normally avoids eye contact was sketching staircases on his desk. A girl who'd told me she "isn't a maths person" was arguing with her partner about triangular numbers.

That's what I want every lesson to feel like. That's what I build for.

What I believe about learning

Three beliefs that shape every resource

01

Confusion is the entry point, not a sign of failure.

The moment a student says "that doesn't make sense" is the most valuable moment in the lesson. A good problem should leave a room thinking for twenty minutes before anyone needs the teacher.

02

Every student has a mathematical identity that teaching either protects or damages.

By middle school, students have sorted themselves into "math people" and "not math people." That sorting is wrong. Open tasks and low-floor high-ceiling problems are the repair.

03

Precision in planning produces freedom in the classroom.

The most responsive, flexible lessons come from the most rigorous design. When you know exactly what understanding looks like, you can improvise without losing the destination.

Origin story

Why LearnLattice exists

I was planning a Grade 7 proportional reasoning unit and needed tasks that were genuinely open — low enough for students building fraction fluency, high enough for students ready for rate of change. Everything I found was too procedural or too thin on MYP alignment.

So I built the task I needed. Then the next one. After two years of accumulating things I had actually used in a classroom, I built the site to share them.

The pedagogical design is mine. The classroom testing is mine. AI helped scale the infrastructure — the Hugo framework, interactive games, and the teaching layer tools. One teacher can now build what previously required a team.

Everything on this site is free and always will be. If it's useful, use it. If you'd change something, tell me.

The research behind the practice

Six thinkers who changed how I teach

Not because I read their books — but because I tried their ideas on a Tuesday morning and watched what happened.

Peter Liljedahl
Thinking classrooms

Vertical surfaces, random groups, and the courage to stop talking. Building Thinking Classrooms gave me the structure to let students own the mathematics.

Jo Boaler
Mathematical mindsets

No speed pressure. No "gifted" labels. Low floor, no ceiling. Her work rewired how I think about who mathematics is for.

Dylan Wiliam
Formative assessment

Hinge questions that tell you in real time what a room understands. "Listen for" prompts on every investigation come from his framework.

Dan Meyer
Hook first, be less helpful

The image before the question. The question before the method. Every hook on this site follows the same principle: provoke before you explain.

Seymour Papert
Constructionism

Students learn by building. Name it after they've made it. Every game and simulation on this site comes from that belief.

Jerome Bruner
CPA progression

Concrete → Pictorial → Abstract. The sequence works because it mirrors how understanding actually forms — not how textbooks are structured.
