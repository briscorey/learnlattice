Teaching Philosophy

Three things I know to be true about learning

01

Confusion is the entry point, not a sign of failure.

The moment a student says "wait — that doesn't make sense" is the most valuable moment in the lesson. My job is to engineer that moment deliberately, then resist the urge to explain it away immediately. A well-designed problem should leave a room thinking for twenty minutes before anyone needs me.

02

Every student arrives with a mathematical identity that teaching either protects or damages.

By the time most students reach middle school, they have already sorted themselves into "math people" and "not math people." That sorting is wrong, and reversing it is the most important thing I do. Speed drills and right/wrong grading are often the instruments of that damage. Open tasks and low-floor high-ceiling problems are the repair.

03

Precision in planning produces freedom in the classroom.

The most responsive, flexible lessons come from the most rigorous design. I use UbD backwards planning every time — not as a compliance exercise but because starting from the evidence of learning forces every activity to justify its existence. When I know exactly what understanding looks like, I can improvise without losing the destination.

Classroom Stories

What actually happens in the room

These are not lesson descriptions. They are accounts of what broke, what surprised me, and what I changed as a result.

Mathematics Grade 6 · Fractions

The lesson I taught three times in one week

I walked into Grade 6 on a Monday with a lesson I had spent two hours designing. It was beautiful — colour-coded fraction bars, a clear sequence, good worked examples. Eighteen minutes in, I could see seventeen different levels of confusion and none of them were the productive kind. I stopped and erased the board.

I drew a rectangle and asked one question: "How many different ways can you cut this into four equal pieces?" No further instruction. I handed out markers and sent them to the whiteboards in random groups. What followed was forty minutes of the best mathematical argument I have ever witnessed in a middle school classroom. Students were defending definitions, testing edge cases, questioning what "equal" actually means. One group discovered rotational symmetry without being taught the word.

The worksheet I had planned was irrelevant by the end of the lesson. The concept — that equality is about area, not appearance — had been constructed collectively, not delivered. I taught a version of that same lesson on Wednesday and Friday, each time starting with a different shape. Each time the room did the mathematics.

What I kept

Vertical non-permanent surfaces and random grouping for every new concept introduction. The whiteboards changed my classroom more than any curriculum change I have ever made.

Earth Science Grade 8 · Plate Tectonics

The map with no labels

I projected a map on the screen. It showed thousands of dots scattered across a dark background — clustered in long curved chains, sparse in the middle of large open regions. No title. No legend. No scale bar. Just: "What is this?"

The answers came fast and wrong. Stars. A disease outbreak. Migration patterns. Cities. Population density. Each wrong answer was more interesting than the last because each one revealed what the students were already carrying as mental models of the world. A student in the third row said, "it's underground — something is happening underground" and the room went quiet.

They were looking at a map of every recorded earthquake epicentre from the last century. We spent the next three lessons building the explanation for what they had already noticed: the edges of things, the boundaries, the fracture lines. By the time I introduced the word "tectonic" they had already invented the concept.

What I kept

The first five minutes of every new unit now involves a question the students cannot answer yet. Not a hook. A genuine question. The difference in engagement between a class that is curious and a class that is being informed is everything.

Biology Grade 7 · Cell Respiration

The candle and the mouse

I opened the unit with a single sentence written on the board: "A candle and a mouse placed in a sealed jar will both stop for exactly the same reason." I said nothing else. I gave them three minutes to write privately before we talked.

The initial answers ranged from "they run out of air" to elaborate theories about carbon dioxide poisoning. The precision was low but the thinking was live. Over the next twenty minutes, the class collectively built toward the idea that both processes — combustion and cellular respiration — are fundamentally the same chemistry viewed from different angles. One student said it better than I could have: "So the mouse is basically burning sugar slowly?"

That framing — slow combustion — carried through the entire unit. Students referred back to it in their assessments. One used it in a Criterion D task to argue that inefficient respiration is why some organisms generate heat. The sentence on the board had done the conceptual work that two weeks of instruction would have laboured through.

What I kept

One carefully chosen anomaly or provocation at the start of every unit. Not a fun fact. A genuine conceptual puzzle that the unit content resolves. Students remember the puzzle long after they forget the content.

MYP Framework

Deep alignment, not surface compliance

I have been designing MYP-aligned units for three years at Nanjing International School. The framework is not a labelling exercise for me — it is the actual architecture of how I plan.

Key Concepts I work with most

Relationships Systems Change Patterns Logic Form

The most generative MYP units I have designed sit at the intersection of two key concepts — particularly Systems × Change (ecology, cell biology) and Relationships × Patterns (algebra, proportional reasoning).

Assessment Criteria — where I spend the most time

Criterion B Investigating patterns (Math) / Inquiry design (Science) — the criteria that require students to generate knowledge rather than reproduce it. I design tasks here that require genuine uncertainty.
Criterion D Real-world application (Math) / Reflection (Science) — where students must argue, not just calculate. The hardest criteria to assess fairly, and the one I have developed the most structured rubric thinking around.

ATL Skills I embed deliberately

Thinking Transfer, analysis, creative and critical thinking in every inquiry sequence
Communication Mathematical notation, scientific argument, and visual representation as distinct literacies
Research Systematic data collection, source evaluation, and evidence-based claim-making

Global Contexts I return to

Scientific & Technical Innovation Fairness & Development Globalisation & Sustainability

Every unit begins with a global context chosen for its ability to generate a genuine Statement of Inquiry, not because it fits the content neatly. The best global context for a unit creates discomfort before it creates clarity.

Why This Site Exists

LearnLattice started as a problem I couldn't solve with what already existed.

Three years ago I was planning a Grade 7 proportional reasoning unit and I needed tasks that were genuinely open — low enough for students still building fraction fluency, high enough for students ready to encounter rate of change. Everything I found was either too procedural or too thin on the MYP alignment that would make it useful for the unit I was actually teaching.

So I built the task I needed. Then the next one. And the one after that. After two years of accumulating things I had actually used in a classroom, I built the infrastructure to share them.

Every resource on this site has been taught. None of them survived their first lesson without revision. The versions here are the revised versions — after the parts that confused students in the wrong way were fixed, the parts that generated the right confusion were amplified, and the assessment connection was made explicit.

The site currently reaches teachers and students across three continents. That reach is not something I advertised for. It is something that happened because the resources are actually useful in real classrooms — including classrooms where English is not the first language, which has shaped how I write task language more than anything else.

What Shapes My Practice

The thinkers whose ideas I test in real classrooms

Jo Boaler
Mathematical identity and growth mindset in practice

The research on fixed vs. growth mathematical identity fundamentally changed how I respond when a student says "I'm not a math person." It also changed how I design tasks — low floor, high ceiling, multiple representations — and how I use mistakes in whole-class discussion.

Peter Liljedahl
Building Thinking Classrooms

Vertical non-permanent surfaces and visibly random groups are the two structural changes that have had the biggest visible impact on my students' willingness to take risks. The research on what creates a thinking classroom vs. a mimicking classroom is the most practically useful teaching research I have encountered.

Dan Meyer
Perplexity as the engine of mathematical learning

"Be less helpful" is the hardest instruction to follow when you understand something and a student doesn't. The 3-Act task structure permanently changed how I open units — the first act is always a question the students generate, not one I hand them.

Doug Lemov
The craft of teaching, made precise

Teach Like a Champion gave me the vocabulary to analyse and improve my own classroom practice with the same rigour I would apply to anything else. Cold Call, No Opt Out, and precise questioning techniques are embedded in how I run any whole-class discussion, whether in mathematics or science.

Dylan Wiliam
Embedded formative assessment

Hinge questions, exit tickets designed around the most likely misconception, and the distinction between feedback that feeds forward vs. feedback that just evaluates — Wiliam's work on formative assessment completely restructured how I use the last ten minutes of every lesson.

Ben Orlin
Mathematics has personality

The best thing Math with Bad Drawings taught me was that mathematics does not need to be stripped of humour, story, or voice to be rigorous. Some of my most effective teaching moments involve deliberate absurdity — a wrong answer defended convincingly, a graph that tells a story before it proves anything.

How I Built This

One classroom teacher, AI as a collaborator, and a decision to build what didn't exist

LearnLattice did not start as a technology project. It started as a frustration. I was designing MYP-aligned investigations every week for my classes — and every week, I searched for resources that met three criteria: genuinely inquiry-based, properly aligned to MYP assessment criteria, and designed for multilingual learners. I found fragments on TES. I found expensive toolkits. I found nothing that combined all three.

So I started building. The investigations come from my classroom — problems I have taught, revised after watching students struggle, and taught again. The teacher moves are real: they document what I actually say, what I listen for, and what I have learned to stop saying.

The technology stack is where AI enters. I used Claude as a development partner to build the Hugo framework, the Classroom Mode presenter, the interactive games, and the SEO infrastructure. The pedagogical design is mine. The classroom testing is mine. The voice is mine. The scaling is AI-assisted.

This is what I believe the future of teacher resource development looks like: practitioner knowledge amplified by AI tooling. One teacher can now build what previously required a team. The quality depends on the teacher. The reach depends on the platform.
